Grammar: 何の魚やろう uses the Kansai dubitative copula やろう (Standard だろう) to express wondering: "What kind of fish is this, I wonder?" The やろう replaces だろう systematically in Kansai dialect, and its use here frames the entire video as a mystery narrative. The fish's identity is unknown, and やろう signals open-ended speculation. 水来ちゃちゃないけど is a rich Kansai contraction: 水 (water) + 来る (to come) in te-form + しまう (regrettable completion) contracted to ちゃう + ない (negative) + けど. The full Standard form would be 水来てしまわないけど. The meaning is "the water has come [into the container] and it's a bit too much, [you see]." The vlogger is apologizing to the fish for the strong water current. The ちゃう (てしまう) adds a sense of "oh no, it happened" to the water overflow, and けど softens the complaint into an observation. ちっちゃ is the colloquial truncation of ちっちゃい (small; tiny), common in Kansai and casual speech. The final い is dropped in rapid, affectionate speech when describing something cute. めっちゃ食べるじゃん uses the Kansai intensifier めっちゃ (super; really; crazy) + verb + じゃん (from じゃない): "[It] eats like crazy, [see]!" めっちゃ is one of the most characteristic Kansai slang adverbs, stronger than とても or すごく and carrying a sense of amazed disbelief. The grammar of discovery (何の魚やろう → ちっちゃ めっちゃ食べるじゃん) moves from mystery to observation to amazed evaluation, the classic arc of encountering an unknown animal.

Grammar: 痛い痛い is the repetition of the pain adjective 痛い (itai, painful; ow), a common Japanese response to sudden minor injury. The repetition intensifies the sensation and signals distress to the audience. た落とすなよ uses the negative imperative 落とすな (otosu na, don't drop) + assertive よ. The た at the beginning appears to be a truncated particle or an exclamation, possibly a dropped あ (ah) or a self-correction fragment. 行け is the plain imperative of 行く (iku, to go), directed at the fish or at Jin (likely the dog or another animal). The triple 走る (runs, runs, runs) uses the plain non-past form as an excited narration of ongoing action, a sports-commentary style where the plain form describes events as they unfold. ちょっと見て uses ちょっと (a moment; hey) + te-form imperative 見て (look): "Look [for a moment]." ちょっと softens the imperative into a request rather than a command. ここのさ uses ここの (of here; here['s]) + さ (casual emphasis particle): "Here, [see]," a common Kansai way to direct attention to a specific spot. あれどこ行った uses the distal demonstrative あれ (that one [over there]) as a pronoun for the fish: "Where did that one go?" The omission of the subject particle は is standard in casual speech. ほらほらほらほら is the quadruple repetition of ほら (look; there), the grammar of urgent visual directing. Each repetition escalates the excitement as the fish is spotted. あれ早くて uses the te-form of 早い (fast) as a trailing description: "That one [is] fast [and...]." The sentence trails off, implying "so fast [I can't catch it]." 見たことない魚 uses the experiential negative pattern 〜たことがない (have never [done]): "a fish [I] have never seen." てるんやけど is the Kansai contraction of ているのだけど (is [there], [you see]): てる (contraction of ている) + ん (explanatory の) + や (Kansai copula) + けど (concessive). It softens the assertion and invites the audience to look, a signature Kansai pattern.

Grammar: この子ちょっと汚いけど水が uses この子 (this child) as an affectionate pronoun for the fish, a standard Japanese anthropomorphism pattern where animals are addressed with kinship terms. 汚いけど uses the concessive けど not to contrast but to soften: "[It's] a bit dirty, [you see]." The trailing 水が (the water [is the reason]) is left incomplete, implying "[it's] the water [that makes it look dirty]." This is the grammar of trailing explanation, where the speaker states a fact and hints at the cause without fully articulating it. めちゃくちゃちっちゃい uses the slang intensifier めちゃくちゃ (crazy; insanely; absurdly) + ちっちゃい (small): "crazy tiny." めちゃくちゃ is stronger than めっちゃ and carries a sense of disbelief at the extremity. 虫うよしてる uses the onomatopoeia うようよ (uyouyo, wriggling; squirming) + する in progressive form: "is wriggling like a bug." うようよ describes the sinuous, worm-like movement of small creatures, and its application to a fish emphasizes its unfamiliar, almost larval appearance. 今日見たら uses the conditional たら after the past tense of 見る: "when [I] looked today." This is the standard Japanese pattern for "when [I] checked [today], [I found that]..." いてるんやけど is the Kansai contraction of いるのだけど (is there, [you see]): いてる (contraction of いている) + ん + や + けど. The んやけど softens the assertion and invites the audience to share the discovery. なんかいてんの uses なんか (somehow; like) + いてん (contraction of い + ん) + の (explanatory): "somehow [it]'s there, [see]." The の at the end is an explanatory/assertive particle, common in casual feminine/Kansai speech. お前誰だよ uses the rough pronoun お前 (omae, you) + 誰だ (who is) + assertive よ: "Hey you, who are you?" お前 is typically rude when directed at humans but becomes playful and intimate when directed at animals, a common register shift in Japanese pet speech. The grammar of playful interrogation (お前誰だよ誰?) turns the mystery fish into a character with agency and identity.

Grammar: バットミ魚みたい uses みたい (like; resembling) as a nominal auxiliary after a noun phrase: "[It looks] like a batfish." みたい is the casual equivalent of ように and is used here for tentative identification based on appearance. The vlogger is guessing the fish might be a "batfish" (a type of fish with a flattened, angular body), though the identification is uncertain. 船みたい uses the same pattern with 船 (boat; ship): "like a boat." The two similes in sequence (batfish-like, boat-like) build a visual description through comparison, a common strategy when vocabulary for the exact appearance is lacking. 金魚やったら uses the Kansai conditional やったら (Standard だったら, if [it] is/was): "If [it's] a goldfish." The やったら replaces だったら in Kansai dialect, and its use here frames a hypothetical identification. こっから赤くなるんかな uses the colloquial contraction こっから (ここから, from here) + adverbial 赤く (red) + なる (to become) + explanatory の + かな (I wonder): "[I] wonder if [it]'ll become red from here [on]." The んかな is a soft dubitative pattern combining explanatory の with かな. The grammar of growth speculation (やったら〜なるんかな) is central to growth-diary vlogs, where the narrator projects future changes from current appearance. どうなんやろ is the Kansai form of どうなんだろう (how will it turn out; what's it gonna be like): どう (how) + なん (なるの, become) + やろ (だろう). The contraction is characteristic of rapid, thoughtful speech. 何の魚やろな uses やろ (だろう) + な (reflective particle): "What kind of fish is it, I wonder." The な softens the wonder into a gentle, ongoing question. 水の中におる虫おらんくなっちゃった uses the Kansai verb おる (いる, to exist [animate]) in both positive and negative forms within the same sentence: おる (were) → おらんく (not being) → なっちゃった (became [regrettably]). The full Standard form would be 水の中にいる虫がいくなってしまった. The Kansai version is more compact and emotionally charged. 全部食べ尽くして uses the compound verb 食べ尽くす (to eat up completely; to devour): 食べる + 尽くす (to exhaust; to use up). The te-form trails off, implying "[it] ate them all up [and now they're gone]." The grammar of voracious consumption (全部食べ尽くして) frames the tiny fish as a surprisingly effective predator, a key character trait in the growth narrative.

Grammar: こた is the Kansai colloquial pronoun for こいつ (koitsu, this one [rough/casual]), used here affectionately for the fish. In Kansai, こた carries less roughness than Standard こいつ and functions as a familiar demonstrative for animals and close companions. 食べた、食べた uses the plain past repeated twice for excited confirmation: "[It] ate, [it] ate!" The shift from past 食べた to progressive 食てる marks the transition from confirmation of completed eating to observation of ongoing eating, a temporal progression that builds narrative momentum. めっちゃ食べるじゃん uses the Kansai intensifier めっちゃ + verb + じゃん: "[It] eats like crazy, [see]!" The じゃん seeks agreement and shared amazement, drawing the audience into the feeding spectacle. だ at the end of the sentence is a truncated copula serving as a paragraph break or transition marker, the vlogger's way of saying "[That's how it is]." またポピン咲いてる uses また (again) + colloquial ポピン (poppy) + progressive 咲いてる (is blooming): "The poppies are blooming again." ポピン is a childlike or affectionate clipping of ポピー (poppy), fitting the gentle, observational tone of the growth diary. えい is an effort sound or exclamation, possibly accompanying a movement or gesture toward the fish. The quadruple おはよう (good morning x4) is directed at the fish as a morning greeting ritual, a common feature of Japanese pet vlogs where animals are treated as household members. いいな uses いい (good) + な (reflective/emotive particle): "That's nice." The な expresses the vlogger's quiet appreciation of the morning scene. で、しみご飯 uses で (and; then) + affectionate しみ (fish) + ご飯 (meal): "And, breakfast for the fish." The use of ご飯 for animal food is anthropomorphic, elevating fish food to a human meal. ちょっと少ないか uses the adjective 少ない (few; little) + dubitative か: "[Is it] a bit too little?" The self-directed question is the grammar of care assessment, where the vlogger evaluates whether the portion is adequate. ウォーターポピー咲いてる uses the loanword ウォーターポピー (water poppy, Hydrocleys nymphoides, an aquatic plant with yellow flowers) + progressive 咲いてる: "The water poppies are blooming." The grammar of morning routine (おはよう → ご飯 → 少ないか → 咲いてる) creates a pastoral sequence where fish care, self-questioning, and plant observation flow together as a single gentle ritual.

The てくる auxiliary marks the growth as a process that has been developing and is now visible, the signature grammar of growth observation. 近魚かな is likely a transcription artifact for 金魚かな (kingyo kana, I wonder if it's a goldfish). The vlogger is now tentatively identifying the mystery fish based on its growth and color change. なんか船みたい uses なんか (somehow; like) + 船みたい (like a boat): "somehow [it's] like a boat." The simile persists even as identification progresses, showing that the fish's body shape remains distinctive. 乾燥赤ムシやっけな uses the Kansai recollection form やっけな (だっけな, what was it again): "Those dried red worms, what were they again?" 赤ムシ (aka mushi, red worms; bloodworms) are the dried larvae of chironomid midges, a common fish food. The vlogger momentarily forgets the name, a humanizing touch. 黄色っぽい色に変わってる uses the っぽい suffix (〜like; somewhat) attached to 黄色 (yellow): 黄色っぽい (yellowish). The pattern [color] + っぽい + 色 + に + 変わる means "is changing to a [color]ish color." The grammar of color-change observation (黄色っぽい色に変わってる) is a growth-diary staple, documenting the visual evidence of maturation.

The やって is the Kansai te-form of だ, used to set up the following thought. It creates a suspended clause that leads into the main verb 思った (thought). 何の魚かなって思った uses the quotative って after the embedded thought 何の魚かな (I wonder what kind of fish [it is]): "thought 'what kind of fish is it?'" The quotative って marks the preceding clause as the content of thought, a standard Japanese pattern for reporting one's own cognition. やっぱり金魚や uses やっぱり (after all; as expected) + noun + Kansai copula や: "after all, [it's] a goldfish." やっぱり marks the resolution of a mystery, acknowledging that the initial guess (goldfish) was correct all along. めっちゃ可愛いじゃん uses the Kansai intensifier + adjective + じゃん: "[It's] crazy cute, [see]!" The じゃん invites shared appreciation, turning solitary observation into communal delight. だんだんオレンジ色なってきてる uses だんだん (gradually) + noun オレンジ色 (orange color) + なってきてる (is becoming [toward now]): "[It's] gradually becoming orange [coming toward now]." なってきてる is the progressive form of なる + てくる: "is in the process of becoming [and coming toward the present]." This is the quintessential grammar of growth documentation, where change is described as an ongoing process converging on the present moment. あっと懐ついてきて uses the adverb あっと (quickly; rapidly — from あっという間に, in a flash) + 懐く (natsuku, to become attached; to become tame) + てくる: "quickly got attached [coming toward me]." 懐く is the verb for animals becoming accustomed to human presence, and its てくる form marks the taming as a process that has been developing and is now complete. 赤ムシあげたら上に上がってくるようなった uses the conditional たら + compound verb 上に上がってくる (comes up [to the surface]) + ようになった (came to [do]): "when [I] give red worms, [it] came to come up [to the surface]." ようになる is the standard Japanese pattern for "came to [do]; started to [do]," marking a learned behavior. The grammar of taming (懐ついてきて〜ようになった) documents the fish's behavioral development as a relationship with the vlogger. すごい警戒心強い子 uses すごい (very) + 警戒心 (wariness; vigilance) + 強い (strong) + 子 (child): "a child with very strong wariness." The use of 子 for an animal is affectionate and anthropomorphic. 横からはこんな感じ uses 横から (from the side) + は (topic marker) + こんな感じ (like this): "From the side, [it's] like this." The hortative marks the end of the observation session and the transition to the next activity.

05 Key Grammar Patterns Summary

〜やろう / 〜やろな (Kansai Dubitative)
何の魚やろう, 何の魚やろな. The Kansai wondering copula replacing だろう, central to the mystery narrative.
〜やったら (Kansai Conditional)
金魚やったら. Kansai for だったら — "if it is/was [a goldfish]." The grammar of hypothetical identification.
〜てきてる / 〜てきた (Growth Directional)
大きくなってきた, オレンジ色なってきてる, 懐ついてきて. The signature grammar of growth observation: change converging on the present.
〜ようになった (Behavioral Development)
上に上がってくるようになった. "Came to [do]; started to [do]." The grammar of learned behavior and taming.
〜っぽい (Approximation Suffix)
茶色っぽい, 黄色っぽい. The suffix of resemblance and approximation, essential for describing subtle color changes.
〜てんの / 〜いてんの (Explanatory Progressive)
なんかいてんの. The colloquial explanatory progressive: "[it]'s there, [see]." Casual discovery grammar.
