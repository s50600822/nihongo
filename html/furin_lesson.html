Title Analysis

Title Grammar: フリン

The title is a single word in katakana: furin (フリン). It is the phonetic rendering of furin (不倫), the Japanese word for "adultery" or "extramarital affair" — literally fu (不, "not / un-") + rin (倫, "ethics / morality"), meaning "against ethics." By writing it in katakana rather than kanji, Yuzuki performs a small but decisive act of aesthetic deflation. The kanji 不倫 carries the full moral weight of Confucian ethics: the heavy fu negating the solemn rin. The katakana フリン is lighter, more casual, almost flirtatious — the word a gossip magazine would use, or a friend confessing over wine. This typographic choice announces the book's method: to examine adultery not as a sin but as a shape — something with contours, variations, and, unexpectedly, tenderness.

Title Breakdown

フリン furin "adultery / extramarital affair" — Written in katakana (フリン) rather than the standard kanji (不倫). The katakana rendering removes the moral scaffolding of the kanji, leaving only the sound. This is significant because the entire book is an argument that furin is not a single transgression but a spectrum of human behavior: the high school girl who sees her stepfather with a neighbor; the housewife who meets her ex-boyfriend; the company employee who falls in love for the last time; the elderly couple who guard a violent past. By refusing the kanji, Yuzuki refuses the judgment that comes with it. The katakana furin floats free of ethics and becomes, instead, a category of experience — like koi (恋, romantic love) or nikushoku (肉食, carnivorous), a thing that exists before morality names it.
Literary resonance: The cover of the Kadokawa Bunko edition (illustrated by Kusanagi Shinpei) shows a woman in a yellow dress walking alone along a riverside path, seen from behind, in soft watercolor. The image captures the book's central mood: solitude that looks like freedom, a figure moving away from the viewer toward something we cannot see. The riverside apartment building — the Riverside Mansion where all six stories are set — becomes a vertical village of secrets, each floor holding a different geometry of desire. The river itself is the perfect metaphor: it flows past all the windows, indifferent to what happens inside, connecting everyone's solitude without ever entering it.
Author

椰月美智子 — Yuzuki Michiko

椰

椰月美智子

Yuzuki Michiko · b. 1970 · Odawara, Kanagawa

Yuzuki Michiko is a Japanese novelist of extraordinary range, moving with apparent ease between children's literature, family sagas, young-adult fiction, and adult novels of love and transgression. Born in 1970 in Odawara City, Kanagawa Prefecture — a coastal town beneath the shadow of Mount Hakone — she graduated from a junior college and worked in an office before discovering, relatively late, that she could write. Her debut, Juunisai (十二歳 / "Twelve Years Old"), won the 42nd Kodansha Children's Literature New Writer Award in 2002, establishing her as a voice in children's fiction. But it was Shizuka na Hibi (しずかな日々 / "Quiet Days," 2006) that revealed her full power: a double winner of the 45th Noma Children's Literature Prize and the 23rd Tsubota Joji Literature Prize, it demonstrated her ability to write about childhood with an adult's understanding of grief.

What makes Yuzuki unique among Japanese novelists is her refusal of genre boundaries. She writes for children with the emotional precision of adult literary fiction; she writes about adultery with the gentleness of a children's author who believes that no one is beyond understanding. Her adult novels — Rurie (るり姉 / "Ruri-nee," 2008), Furin (フリン, 2010), Konparu Iro Kanata (こんぱるいろ彼方, 2018) — explore the fault lines of marriage, family, and desire without ever becoming salacious or punitive. She is interested not in condemning her characters but in understanding how they arrived at the choices that will damage them. This compassion is her signature: the belief that even a cheating husband, even a vengeful wife, even a teenager who sees too much, is acting from a comprehensible human need.

In 2021, Ashita no Shokutaku (明日の食卓 / "Tomorrow's Table") was adapted into a film directed by Segawa Keisuke and starring Kanno Miho, becoming her breakthrough to a wider adult audience. In 2024, Mukashi wa Ore to Onaidoshi Datta Tanaka-san to no Yuujo (昔はおれと同い年だった田中さんとの友情 / "My Friendship with Tanaka, Who Used to Be the Same Age as Me") was adapted into an NHK special drama starring Kishitani Kazuo. She continues to live in Odawara with her family, drawing the rhythms of coastal town life into fiction that is simultaneously local and universal.

Characters & The Six Stories

Furin is a linked short-story collection set in a single riverside apartment building — the Riverside Mansion. Each story follows a different resident or visitor, and each explores a different shape of furin (adultery / transgression against marriage). What links them is not plot but geography and theme: the river flows past every window, and desire flows through every life. The stories can be read independently, but together they form a composite portrait of a building where no marriage is quite what it appears, and no betrayal is entirely unforgivable.

「葵さの初恋」· "Aoi-san's First Love"

The Stepfather & The Neighbor OL

High school student Manami goes to a love hotel with her boyfriend and sees her stepfather walking with Aoi-san — an OL who lives in the same apartment building. The story is told through Manami's eyes: a girl old enough to understand what she is seeing but young enough to be confused by her own complicity. She does not condemn her stepfather; she observes him, as she observes her own emerging sexuality, with a mixture of curiosity and dread. The "first love" of the title is not Manami's but Aoi's — or perhaps it is Manami's first understanding that love and betrayal can coexist in the same gesture.

「シニガミ」· "Shinigami"

The Housewife & Her Ex-Boyfriend

A housewife repeatedly meets her ex-boyfriend for secret trysts. The title Shinigami (死神, "death god / reaper") suggests the fatal gravity of these meetings — not because anyone dies, but because each encounter carries the weight of a life that could have been. Readers frequently cite this as the collection's most chilling story: the recognition that the person you loved before marriage does not disappear when you say vows; they simply go underground, like a river that has been paved over but still flows.

「最後の恋」· "Last Love"

The Company Employee's Final Romance

A company employee falls in love — truly, for the last time. The story's power lies in its refusal of cynicism. This is not a midlife crisis or a pathetic attempt at youth; it is a person recognizing, late, that the capacity for romantic love does not expire at marriage. Yuzuki treats the protagonist's feelings with the same respect she brings to a child's first crush in her juvenile fiction. The result is heartbreaking not because the affair is tragic but because it is real, and reality is always more painful than melodrama.

「年下の男の子」· "The Younger Man"

The Older Woman & The Age Gap

A story about an affair with a younger man, exploring the power dynamics, social judgment, and unexpected tenderness of age-disparate romance. In Japan, where a woman's value is still often measured by her proximity to youth, choosing a younger lover is a transgression against social order as much as marital vows. Yuzuki examines what the woman gains, what she risks, and what she discovers about herself when she stops performing the role of "appropriate wife."

「」· The Superintendent Couple

The Elderly Caretakers & Their Violent Past

The apartment building's elderly superintendent couple guard a "spectacular past" — a history of violence or trauma that has bound them together across decades. Their story asks: what if the worst thing that ever happened to you is also the reason your marriage survived? Yuzuki's elderly characters are never merely background; they carry the full weight of lived experience, and their love, tested by catastrophe, casts the younger characters' comparatively trivial betrayals into sharp relief.

「」· The Annual Gathering

The Residents Assemble

The final story brings the residents together at their annual building gathering — a teikeikai (定期会) where the separate stories intersect. What has been private becomes, briefly, communal. The gathering functions as the collection's coda: a demonstration that the building is not merely a setting but a living organism, its inhabitants connected by walls, by the river, and by the shared fact that none of their marriages is simple. Yuzuki's genius is to make this assembly feel not like a contrivance but like the natural result of shared space.

Story

Synopsis

The Riverside Mansion as a Vertical Village

The Riverside Mansion stands on the bank of a river — the kind of mid-range apartment building found in every Japanese city, neither luxurious nor dilapidated, home to salarymen, office ladies, housewives, and retirees. From the outside, it is utterly ordinary. From the inside, it is a lattice of secrets: on one floor, a high school girl discovers her stepfather's infidelity; on another, a housewife cannot stop returning to her ex-boyfriend; elsewhere, a company employee falls in love for what he knows will be the final time, and an elderly couple tends the building while guarding a past that would destroy most marriages.

Yuzuki does not construct a single plot across these stories; she constructs an atmosphere. The river is the constant — it appears in every window, its sound a background to every conversation, its current a reminder that things move on regardless of human intention. The building's residents do not know each other's secrets, but they share walls, and Yuzuki makes us feel the proximity: the affair happening above the grieving child, the elderly couple's history vibrating through the floorboards of the younger man's apartment. The result is not a soap opera but a meditation on the simultaneity of private lives — the way a building contains, at any given moment, dozens of dramas invisible to one another.

The Collection's Central Question

The Kadokawa Bunko cover copy asks: "Ikutsu ni natte mo, hito wa koi wo suru" (いくつになっても、人は恋をする / "No matter how old you get, people fall in love"). This is the book's thesis, and it is more radical than it sounds. In a culture that still largely expects romantic love to expire at marriage — to be replaced by duty, companionship, and parental obligation — Yuzuki insists that the capacity for new love persists across a lifetime. The question is not whether these affairs are right or wrong; the question is what they reveal about the marriages they violate. Every furin in the collection is, in some way, a diagnostic: it exposes what the marriage was missing, what the person needed and could not ask for, what society demanded and the individual could not sustain.

Yuzuki's compassion is not permissiveness. She does not celebrate adultery; she humanizes it. The housewife who meets her ex-boyfriend is not a villain; she is a person who made a choice, then another, then found herself on a path she had not intended to take. The stepfather with the neighbor OL is not a monster; he is a man who has found something in Aoi-san that his marriage does not provide — and the novel's genius is to let us see what that something is without letting him off the hook. The company employee in "Last Love" is perhaps the most sympathetic: his love is genuine, his timing catastrophic, his decency intact even as he destroys the life he built. Yuzuki writes about these people as she writes about children — with the belief that understanding precedes judgment, and that most human damage is caused not by malice but by need.

Themes

  • Furin as shape, not sin: By writing the title in katakana, Yuzuki frames adultery not as a moral category but as a phenomenon with multiple forms. Each story presents a different geometry: the accidental discovery, the recurring relapse, the late blossoming, the age-transgressive, the trauma-bonded, the communal secret. What unites them is not guilt but desire — the persistence of wanting after society has told you to stop.
  • The building as confessional: The Riverside Mansion is more than a setting; it is a structure of concealment. Apartments are private boxes stacked vertically, each containing a drama invisible to the others. The elevator, the lobby, the annual gathering — these are the only places where separate stories touch. Yuzuki uses architecture as metaphor: we live stacked on top of one another's pain, separated by plaster and politeness.
  • The river as witness and indifference: The river sees everything and intervenes in nothing. It flows past the love hotel where Manami sees her stepfather, past the windows where the housewife waits for her lover's call, past the elderly couple's quarters where a violent history is stored. In Japanese literature, rivers often symbolize time and forgetting; here, they symbolize the persistence of what society prefers to forget.
  • Marriage as performance: Every married character in the collection is, to some degree, performing a role: the faithful wife, the responsible husband, the reconstructed family. Yuzuki's insight is that performance is exhausting, and exhaustion creates the conditions for transgression. The affairs do not happen because the characters are bad; they happen because the roles have become unsustainable.
  • Compassion without absolution: Yuzuki refuses both the moralism that condemns and the permissiveness that celebrates. Her characters suffer for their choices — marriages crack, children see too much, dignity is lost — but they are never reduced to their worst moment. The housewife is also a good mother; the stepfather is also a kind man; the company employee is also a conscientious worker. This refusal of moral simplification is what makes Furin literature rather than entertainment.

Text

Key Passages

These passages capture Yuzuki's characteristic gentleness: the way she writes about transgression without sensationalism, and her belief that the most devastating moments are often the most quietly observed.

01
The Promotional Thesis
いくつになっても、人は恋をする。
Ikutsu ni natte mo, hito wa koi wo suru.
"No matter how old you get, people fall in love."

Nuance Note

This is the Kadokawa Bunko cover copy, and it functions as the novel's philosophical thesis. Grammatically, it is extraordinarily simple: ikutsu ni natte mo (いくつになっても, "no matter how old one becomes") is an N4 conditional pattern (te mo, "even if / no matter"); hito wa koi wo suru (人は恋をする, "people fall in love") is a basic S-O-V sentence using koi wo suru (恋をする, "to fall in love / to be in love"). The simplicity is the point. Yuzuki is not making a complex argument; she is stating a fact that Japanese society prefers to deny. The word koi (恋) is crucial here: it means romantic love, passionate love, the kind of love associated with youth and courtship — not ai (愛, the deeper, more stable love of long partnership). By using koi rather than ai, the sentence insists that the transient, disruptive, youthful form of love persists into middle and old age. This is not a comforting message in a culture that expects adults to graduate from koi to ai, from passion to responsibility. The sentence's quiet radicalism is in its verb: suru (する, "to do"), not wasureru (忘れる, "to forget") or owaru (終わる, "to end"). People do not stop falling in love. They keep doing it. The grammar makes this continuous, inevitable, and ordinary.

02
The Daughter's Discovery
高校生の真奈美は、カレと入ったラブホテルで、自分の父親が同じマンションに住むOLの葵さんと一緒に歩いているのを目撃してしまう。
Koukousei no Manami wa, kare to haitta rabu hoteru de, jibun no chichioya ga onaji manshon ni sumu OL no Aoi-san to issho ni aruite iru no wo mokugeki shite shimau.
"High school student Manami, at the love hotel she had entered with her boyfriend, ended up witnessing her father walking together with Aoi-san, an office lady who lived in the same apartment building."

Nuance Note

This sentence is a masterclass in narrative economy. It contains two transgressions: Manami is at a love hotel with her boyfriend (she is sexually active, or about to be), and her father is with another woman (he is committing furin). The sentence places daughter and father in parallel sin: they are both in the same commercial space of illicit sexuality, separated only by timing. The grammar encodes this parallelism. Kare to haitta rabu hoteru de (カレと入ったラブホテルで, "at the love hotel she entered with her boyfriend") uses the relative-clause construction haitta (入った, "entered") modifying rabu hoteru — the hotel is defined by her entry with a man who is not her husband (she has none; she is a student). Jibun no chichioya ga (自分の父親が, "her own father") emphasizes possession and relationship — this is not any man but hers, the man who is supposed to be different from the ones in hotels. Mokugeki shite shimau (目撃してしまう) uses the te-shimau (てしまう) construction, which indicates completion with regret or unintended consequence — she did not seek this sight; it happened to her, and she cannot undo it. The shimau is the emotional core of the sentence: the discovery is a small catastrophe that has already finished occurring. Finally, chichioya (父親, "father") is worth noting: Yuzuki uses the biological/legal term rather than the intimate papa or otosan, creating distance at the moment of greatest intimacy violated. The father becomes, in this sentence, a generic male figure walking with a woman who is not Manami's mother. The grammar strips him of familial particularity just as his action strips him of paternal authority.

03
Retaliation as Mirror
夫の裏切りに、私は裏切りで仕返しする。
Otto no uragiri ni, watashi wa uragiri de shikaeshi suru.
"For my husband's betrayal, I retaliate with betrayal."

Nuance Note

This is the hardcover edition's promotional tagline, and it announces the book's most unsettling theme: the symmetry of revenge. Grammatically, the sentence is a perfect chiasmus: otto no uragiri ni (夫の裏切りに, "to the husband's betrayal") is answered by watashi wa uragiri de (私は裏切りで, "I with betrayal"). The word uragiri (裏切り, "betrayal") appears twice, once as the injury and once as the response. The particle ni (に, "to / in response to") establishes causation; the particle de (で, "with / by means of") establishes method. The sentence says, in effect: his betrayal creates the condition; my betrayal is the tool. Shikaeshi suru (仕返しする, "to retaliate / pay back") is a domestic word — it is what children do to siblings, what neighbors do to each other. It is not the grand vocabulary of vengeance (fukushuu, 復讐) but the small language of household score-settling. This domestication of revenge is crucial: the speaker is not launching a campaign; she is balancing an account. The sentence's coldness is in its arithmetic — betrayal for betrayal, measure for measure — and its tragedy is in the word watashi (私, "I"). The speaker recognizes herself as someone who has chosen to become what wounded her. This is not liberation; it is infection.

04
The Question the Collection Refuses to Answer
結婚後の恋はいけないことなの？
Kekkon go no koi wa ikenai koto na no?
"Is love after marriage a bad thing?"

Nuance Note

This question appears in the hardcover promotional copy, and it is the engine of the entire collection. Grammatically, it is simple: kekkon go no koi (結婚後の恋, "love after marriage") — the temporal go (後, "after") modifying koi (恋, romantic love), creating a category of love that society does not officially recognize. Ikenai koto (いけないこと, "a bad thing / something one must not do") uses the permissive-negative construction ikenai (いけない, "must not / cannot be allowed"), which is softer than warui (悪い, "evil / bad") but more judgmental than dasai (ださい, "uncool"). Ikenai is what parents say to children, what teachers say to students — it carries the weight of social instruction rather than moral absolutism. The final na no (なの) is the explanatory/emphatic question marker that seeks not just an answer but an explanation: the speaker is not merely asking whether it is forbidden; she is asking why. This na no transforms the sentence from a yes/no question into a demand for reasoning — and the collection's answer, implicit in every story, is that the question itself is wrong. It is not that love after marriage is "bad"; it is that marriage was never designed to accommodate the persistence of koi. The fault is not in the lovers; it is in the institution's assumption that love can be scheduled to begin before marriage and end at the altar. Yuzuki never states this directly; she lets the stories demonstrate it, story by story, betrayal by betrayal, until the reader is no longer certain that "love after marriage" is the problem. Perhaps the problem is marriage's claim to own love's calendar.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loses

Yuzuki's prose in Furin appears deceptively simple, but it relies on Japanese kinship terms, moral vocabulary, and the cultural weight of marriage as a social institution that do not transfer cleanly into English.

1. Furin (フリン / 不倫) as Katakana Defiance

JP 「フリン」
EN "Furin" / "adultery" / "affair"
Loss The English "adultery" carries Christian theological weight — it is one of the Ten Commandments, a sin against God as well as spouse. The English "affair" is lighter, more social, almost chic. Neither captures the specific Japanese concept of furin (不倫): fu (不, "not / un-") + rin (倫, "ethics / human relations / moral order"). Furin is not primarily a religious sin; it is a social one — a violation of the ethical order that binds families, workplaces, and communities. The kanji 不倫 makes this explicit: the character rin (倫) contains the person radical (人) and means the proper ordering of human relations. By writing it in katakana — フリン — Yuzuki removes the ethical scaffolding while keeping the sound. The English translator must choose between "adultery" (too religious), "affair" (too casual), "infidelity" (too clinical), or "betrayal" (too general). None carries the specific cultural meaning of furin as a transgression against the Confucian-ethical order of the Japanese family. What is lost is the sense that this is not merely a private sin but a social one — an act that disturbs the fabric of communal life because marriage in Japan is not merely a contract between two people but a node in a network of obligations.

2. Koi (恋) vs. Ai (愛) as Love's Lifecycle

JP いくつになっても、人は恋をする
EN "No matter how old you get, people fall in love"
Loss Japanese distinguishes between koi (恋) and ai (愛), and the distinction is central to Furin's meaning. Koi is romantic love — passionate, desiring, often unrequited or disruptive. It is the love of courtship, of longing, of the heart that wants what it cannot have. Ai is committed love — the deep, stable attachment of long partnership, of parental devotion, of chosen permanence. The traditional Japanese model expects koi to precede marriage and ai to follow it; the transition from koi to ai is considered maturity. Yuzuki's sentence insists that koi does not end when ai begins — people keep falling in koi even after they have committed to ai. The English "fall in love" collapses this distinction. "Love" in English encompasses both koi and ai; it does not force the reader to recognize that the love that persists after marriage is specifically koi — the disruptive, desiring, romantic form that society expects to graduate into something calmer. What is lost is the precision of the problem: it is not "love" that continues (everyone agrees married people love each other); it is koi — the specific form of love that threatens marriage because it operates outside it.

3. Chichioya (父親) as Legal Relation, Not Intimacy

JP 自分の父親が
EN "her father" / "her own father"
Loss Yuzuki's choice of chichioya (父親, "father" as a legal/biological relation) rather than otosan (お父さん, "Dad" — intimate, familial) or papa (パパ — childish, affectionate) is crucial. Chichioya is the word used in legal documents, in news reports, in distanced description. It strips the figure of paternal warmth and presents him as a male person with a specific social role. When Manami sees her chichioya with Aoi-san, she is seeing not "Dad" but "the man who is legally my father" — a figure whose authority and intimacy are simultaneously violated. The English "father" cannot fully reproduce this effect because English does not have the same gradation of father-terms. "Her own father" captures some of the shock (the jibun no, 自分の, emphasizes possession and particularity), but it cannot convey the formal distance of chichioya at the moment when distance is most devastating. What is lost is the sense that the father has, by his action, transformed himself from otosan (the man who comes home, who is known) into chichioya (the man who is merely a biological fact, walking with a woman who is not Manami's mother).

4. Ma ga Sasu (魔が差す) as Demonic Temptation, Not Character Flaw

JP 魔が差した主婦
EN "a housewife who had a moment of weakness" / "a housewife who was tempted"
Loss Ma ga sasu (魔が差す, literally "a demon passes through / a devil momentarily possesses") is one of the most culturally specific phrases in the collection. It describes not a character flaw or a moral failure but a momentary intrusion of evil — as if the person were not fully responsible because something external passed through them. The ma (魔) is the same character as in akuma (悪魔, "demon / devil") and mamono (魔物, "monster"). It is a supernatural explanation for human weakness: the devil made me do it, but rendered in Buddhist-Shinto vocabulary rather than Christian. The English "moment of weakness" domesticates this into psychology; "tempted" retains some religious resonance but loses the specific Japanese cosmology. What is lost is the sense that transgression is not inherent to the person but visits them — a passing presence rather than a stable trait. This matters because Yuzuki's entire method depends on seeing her characters as fundamentally decent people who have been visited by something they could not control. The ma is not an excuse; it is a description of how ordinary people find themselves doing extraordinary damage.

5. Ouse (逢瀬) as Poetic Tryst

JP 元カレと逢瀬を繰り返す主婦
EN "a housewife who repeatedly meets her ex-boyfriend for secret trysts"
Loss Ouse (逢瀬) is a literary, almost classical word for a lovers' secret meeting. The character ou (逢, "to meet by chance / to encounter fatefully") is the word used for fated meetings in The Tale of Genji; se (瀬, "rapids / shallows") adds a sense of place and peril — the meeting happens at the river's edge, where water moves quickly and footing is unsure. Ouse is not a neutral word for "secret meeting"; it carries the full weight of Japanese literary romanticism. It is the word used for the meetings of Heian-period lovers, for the assignations in kabuki drama, for the encounters in classical poetry that change a life. When Yuzuki uses ouse for a contemporary housewife meeting her ex-boyfriend, she elevates the encounter from sordid to poetic — not to romanticize it but to insist that the emotional reality of the meeting transcends its social classification. The English "secret tryst" captures some of this but sounds either archaic or sleazy; "assignation" is too formal; "affair" is too ongoing. What is lost is the literary dignity that ouse confers — the recognition that even transgressive love participates in a tradition of Japanese romantic expression that stretches back a thousand years.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns drawn from the title, key passages, and the thematic language of love, marriage, and transgression.

〜ても（譲歩・条件） N4

Te mo: "even if / no matter" — indicates that a condition does not prevent the main clause from being true.

いくつになっても、人は恋をする

"No matter how old you get, people fall in love" — The te mo construction makes age irrelevant to the capacity for love; the grammar enacts the book's thesis.

〜てしまう（完了・遺憾） N4

Te shimau: "to end up ~ing / to ~ completely (with regret)" — indicates completion, often with a sense of irreversibility or unintended consequence.

目撃してしまう

"ends up witnessing / cannot help but witness" — The te shimau conveys that the discovery happens to Manami against her will and cannot be undone.

〜のを〜する（名詞化・対象） N4

No wo ~ suru: "to do ~ [to the fact/sight] that ~" — nominalizes a clause with no and makes it the object of a verb.

歩いているのを目撃する

"to witness the fact that [he] is walking" — The no nominalization turns the ongoing action into a discrete event that can be seen.

〜に、〜で（対応・手段） N5

Ni, de: "to ~, [respond] with ~" — ni marks the stimulus; de marks the means of response.

夫の裏切りに、私は裏切りで仕返しする

"To my husband's betrayal, I retaliate with betrayal" — The ni / de pairing creates a perfect chiasmus: betrayal as stimulus (ni) and betrayal as tool (de).

〜なの？（説明疑問） N5

Na no?: explanatory question — seeks not just yes/no but reasoning; implies that the speaker expects an explanation.

結婚後の恋はいけないことなの？

"Is love after marriage a bad thing? (And if so, why?)" — The na no transforms the question from a request for classification into a demand for justification.

〜で（場所・手段） N5

De: "at / in / by means of" — marks location of action or means/instrument.

ラブホテルで、父親が歩いてるのを目撃する

"at the love hotel, witnesses her father walking" — The de locates the discovery in a space that is itself transgressive, compounding the daughter's disorientation.

〜が（接続・対比） N5

Ga: "but / and" — connects clauses with soft contrast or addition; often untranslated but structurally essential.

父親が同じマンションに住むOLの葵さんと一んと一緒に歩いているのを

"[her] father was walking together with Aoi-san, an OL who lived in the same apartment building" — The ga subject marker introduces the father as the grammatical subject of the witnessed action, making him the focus of the daughter's catastrophic sight.

〜と一緒に（共同） N5

To issho ni: "together with ~" — indicates accompaniment, often with emotional weight when the companion is inappropriate.

葵さんと一緒に歩いている
p class="grammar-example-en">"walking together with Aoi-san" — The phrase is grammatically neutral but contextually devastating: the father's companionship belongs, by social expectation, to the mother.

Study tip: Yuzuki Michiko's grammar in Furin is accessible (mostly N5-N4) but emotionally loaded. Pay attention to how te shimau encodes regret, how te mo makes defiant generalizations, and how the simplest particles (ni, de, ga) structure sentences of enormous moral weight. Her prose does not dazzle with complexity; it achieves its effects through the careful placement of ordinary grammatical tools in contexts where every choice carries ethical significance. This is the craft of a writer who trusts that grammar itself can hold grief, desire, and the quiet catastrophe of recognition.
