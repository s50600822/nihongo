Title Analysis

Title Grammar: 猫を処方いたします。

The title is a formal, almost bureaucratic announcement that upends expectations: a mental clinic where the prescription is not pills but cats. The verb shohou suru (処方する) is medical register; pairing it with neko (猫) creates an instant cognitive dissonance that hooks the reader. The humble polite form itashimasu adds a layer of old-fashioned service-industry courtesy, as if the book itself were bowing to you across a pharmacy counter.

猫を処方いたします。
Neko wo shohou itashimasu.
"We hereby prescribe you a cat." / "A cat is prescribed for you."

The period at the end is part of the title: a full stop that turns the sentence into a declarative, almost contractual statement.

Title Breakdown

猫を neko wo "cat [direct object]" — The object particle wo marks the cat as the thing being acted upon, the substance being dispensed.
処方 shohou "prescription" — A Sino-Japanese noun (sho 処 = dispose/manage + ho 方 = direction/method). Used exclusively in medical contexts: a doctor's written order for medication.
いたします itashimasu "humbly do" — The humble form of suru (to do), used when the speaker is lowering their own status to show respect to the listener. Common in customer service, formal letters, and traditional professions.
Register clash: The humor begins in the title itself. Shohou is cold, clinical, and scientific. Neko is warm, furry, and alive. Itashimasu is obsequiously polite. The combination sounds like a Kyoto pharmacist from the Taisho era announcing, with a deep bow, that your treatment for depression is a tabby. This tonal collision sets the stage for the entire book's blend of whimsy and genuine emotional weight.

Title as Promise

The title functions as a narrative contract with the reader. It promises:

  1. A premise: Cats will be given, like medicine, to people who need them
  2. A setting: The medical/ceremonial language implies a clinic, a doctor, patients
  3. A tone: The humble-polite register signals warmth, service, and human care
  4. A question: Can a cat really be a prescription? The book sets out to prove that, in some cases, it can

Author

石田祥 — Ishida Syou

石

石田祥

Ishida Syou · b. 1975, Kyoto

Ishida Syou is a Japanese novelist from Kyoto, best known for the Neko wo Shohou Itashimasu series, which has sold over one million copies worldwide and been translated into 36 countries and territories. Despite his immense success with cat-themed fiction, Ishida is actually a self-described "bird person" (toroha) who has never owned a cat.

  • 1975 Born in Kyoto Prefecture.
  • Post-HS After high school, joined a financial company, then later moved to a telecommunications company. Wrote fiction on the side while working full-time.
  • 2014 Debuted with Tomato no Sensei (トマトの先生), winner of the 9th Japan Love Story Grand Prize (第9回日本ラブストーリー大賞). Published by Takarajimasha.
  • 2021 Published Moto Kare no Neko wo, Azukarimashite. (元カレの猫を、預かりまして / "I Was Left with My Ex-Boyfriend's Cat"), a precursor to his cat-themed fiction.
  • 2023 Published Neko wo Shohou Itashimasu. (猫を処方いたします。), the first volume of what would become a blockbuster series. Won the 11th Kyoto Book Prize.
  • 2024 Opened his official website to commemorate his 10th anniversary as a writer. The series surpassed 240,000 copies in Japan.
  • 2025 Series surpassed 390,000 copies in Japan. Won the 13th Utsunomiya Prize (Bunko division).
  • 2026 Worldwide cumulative sales surpassed 1,000,000 copies across 36 countries and territories. Volume 6 published.

Personal note: Ishida describes himself as a "strong bird person" (kyouretsu na toraha) who, if he starts talking about birds, will continue for about two hours. He has never owned a cat, fearing that the daily care would disrupt his writing schedule. To write the series, he visited cat cafes two or three times. He describes himself as a writer of "fantasy and comedy" who prefers imagination to research: "The more freely I can expand my imagination, the better."
Cast

Main Characters

ニケ先生 · Nike-sensei

Doctor / Proprietor of Nakagyo Kokoro Clinic

A laid-back, somewhat shady doctor with a light, casual manner of speaking (nori no karui). He runs the mysterious mental clinic on the 5th floor of an old building in Kyoto's Nakagyo ward. Despite his flippant demeanor, he has a secret — and a genuine gift for matching the right cat to the right patient. His name evokes the Greek goddess of victory (Nike), but in Kyoto dialect it reads as a breezy, slightly comical moniker.

千歳さん · Chitose-san

Nurse

The clinic's only nurse. Described as a "tsundere Kyoto beauty" — outwardly cool and sharp-tongued but with a warm heart beneath. She manages the practical side of cat prescriptions: selecting cats, instructing patients on "dosage," and tracking outcomes. Her name (千歳, "thousand years") suggests longevity and enduring care.

心先生 · Kokoro-sensei

Veterinarian

A veterinarian who appears in later volumes. His name (心, "heart/mind") mirrors the clinic's focus on mental and emotional healing. He has his own troubles, adding layers to the series' exploration of who heals the healers.

椎名 · Shiina

Neighbor / Office Worker

Runs a company in the office next to the clinic. Initially skeptical of the "cat prescription" operation, he eventually comes face-to-face with Nike-sensei and the clinic's unusual methods. Represents the rational, everyday world colliding with the clinic's gentle magic.

The "Prescription Cats": Each volume introduces new patients with specific emotional wounds, and each receives a cat matched to their needs. The cats are not magical; they are simply cats — fickle, delicate, demanding, and adorable. The healing comes not from supernatural power but from the daily rhythm of caring for another living being, and from being seen by a creature that does not judge.
Story

Synopsis

Deep in a dim alley in Kyoto's Nakagyo ward, on the fifth floor of an old building with no elevator, there is a mental clinic called Nakagyo Kokoro no Byouin (中京こころのびょういん). Patients with troubled hearts find their way here, guided by word-of-mouth and the kind of directions that sound like incantations: "Go up and down Fuyacho-dori and Tomikoji-dori near Rokkaku-dori……"

The clinic is run by a strangely casual doctor named Nike-sensei and a cool, sharp-tongued nurse named Chitose-san. But the prescriptions they write are not for antidepressants or anxiolytics. They prescribe cats. Real, living cats. For a set number of days, patients must "take" their cat as directed — feed it, live with it, let it disrupt their routines and their solitude.

Each story follows a different patient: a college student anxious about her relationship, an elderly man who has lost his wife, a man working at a cat shelter who cannot connect with people, a father exhausted from childcare, an illustrator burdened by complexes, a company employee facing a crucial presentation. As they live with their prescribed cats, their hearts begin, imperceptibly, to shift. And behind Nike-sensei's flippant exterior lies a reason — a secret — for why he prescribes cats at all.

Patients in Volume 1

Vol. 1

不眠、耳鳴りの患者

A patient suffering from insomnia and tinnitus. The first to discover the clinic's secret — that the prescription is a cat, not a pill. Their confusion and gradual acceptance sets the template for the series.

Vol. 1

彼氏との関係に悩む女子大生

A female college student troubled by her relationship with her boyfriend. The emotional turbulence of young love meets the steady, nonjudgmental presence of a cat.

Vol. 1

妻に先立たれた老人

An elderly man whose wife has passed away before him. Grief, loneliness, and the question of whether a cat can fill a space that a human has left.

Vol. 1

保護猫センターで働く男

A man who works at a cat shelter. Surrounded by cats all day, yet unable to form human connections. The irony of his situation is not lost on Nike-sensei.

Structure: The book is a rensaku tanpen (連作短編) — a series of interconnected short stories. Each chapter centers on a new patient, but the clinic, Nike-sensei, and Chitose-san provide continuity. The series has now reached six volumes, with over one million copies sold worldwide.
Text

Key Passages

These passages capture the book's distinctive voice: the Kyoto dialect, the whimsical premise, and the gentle emotional undercurrent that has made the series a global phenomenon.

01
The Clinic's Location
京都市中京区麩屋町通上ル六角通西入……呪文のように長い住所に導かれ、古びたビルの五階にある「中京こころのびょういん」を訪れた患者たち。
Kyoto-shi Nakagyo-ku Fuyacho-dori Agaru Rokkaku-dori Nishiiru…… jumon no you ni nagai juusho ni michibikare, furubita biru no go-kai ni aru "Nakagyo Kokoro no Byouin" wo otozureta kanja-tachi.
"Guided by an address as long as an incantation — 'Kyoto City, Nakagyo Ward, up Fuyacho Street, west on Rokkaku Street…' — the patients arrive at the Nakagyo Kokoro Clinic on the fifth floor of an old building."

Nuance Note

Kyoto addresses are notoriously complex, relying on a grid system of dori (streets) and agaru/itaru/sagaru (go up/go to/go down) rather than street numbers. The comparison to a jumon (呪文, magic spell/incantation) is apt: only those who are meant to find the clinic can navigate its labyrinthine directions. The phrase furu-bita biru (古びたビル, aged/worn building) evokes the slightly run-down, unglamorous reality behind the formal language of the title. The clinic is not sleek or modern; it is a place that has accumulated time.

02
Nike-sensei's Welcome
「ここは『中京こころのびょういん』です。ご覧の通り僕と看護師の二人だけで細々とやってますんで、ほんまは新患さんはお断りしてるんですけど、特別ですよ。せっかくここまで来てくれはったんでね」
"Koko wa 'Nakagyo Kokoro no Byouin' desu. Goran no toori boku to kangoshi no futari dake de hosoboso to yattemasu n de, honma wa shinkan-san wa okotowari shiteru n desu kedo, tokubetsu desu yo. Sekkaku koko made kite kurehatta n de ne."
"This is the Nakagyo Kokoro Clinic. As you can see, it's just me and the nurse, barely keeping things running, so normally we turn away new patients. But you're special. You went to all the trouble of finding your way here, after all."

Nuance Note

This passage is rich with Kyoto dialect (Kyo-kotoba). Honma (ほんま) is Kyoto's version of hontou ni (really/truly). Kurehatta (くれはった) is the Kyoto honorific form of kureta (gave/did for me), showing respect toward the patient's effort. Hosoboso to yattemasu (細々とやってます) means "barely managing / keeping things going on a small scale" — a humble expression that downplays the clinic's operations. The casual self-deprecation (we normally turn away new patients, but you're special) is classic Nike-sensei: flippant, charming, and slightly suspicious. The reader immediately senses that this clinic operates by its own rules.

03
The Prescription Revealed
心の不調を抱えてこの病院を訪れた患者に、妙にノリの軽い医者が処方するのは、薬ではなく、本物の猫だった！？
Kokoro no fuchou wo kakaete kono byouin wo otozureta kanja ni, myou ni nori no karui isha ga shohou suru no wa, kusuri de wa naku, honmono no neko datta!?
"To the patients who visit this hospital with troubled hearts, the strangely casual doctor prescribes not medicine, but a real cat!?"

Nuance Note

Myou ni nori no karui (妙にノリの軽い) is a wonderfully compressed phrase. Myou ni = strangely, oddly. Nori (ノリ) is slang for vibe/mood/energy. Karui = light, casual, flippant. Together: "oddly casual-vibed" or "strangely flippant." The interrobang (!?) is characteristic of Japanese book-copy style, signaling both surprise and amusement. Honmono (本物, "the real thing") is crucial here: the prescription is not a toy, not a robot, not a metaphor. It is a real, living, unpredictable cat. The contrast between kokoro no fuchou (心の不調, troubled heart / mental distress) — serious, clinical — and neko (猫) — domestic, soft, slightly absurd — is the engine of the entire series.

04
Taking the Medicine
戸惑いながらも、決められた日数、猫を「服薬」する患者たち。気紛れで繊細、手がかかるけど愛くるしい猫と暮らすことで、彼らの心も少しずつ変化していく。
Tomadoi nagara mo, kimerareta nissuu, neko wo "fukuyaku" suru kanja-tachi. Kimagure de sensai, te ga kakaru kedo airurushii neko to kurasu koto de, karera no kokoro mo sukoshizutsu henka shite iku.
"Bewildered, the patients nonetheless 'take' their cat for the prescribed number of days. By living with cats — fickle, delicate, demanding, yet adorable — their hearts gradually begin to change."

Nuance Note

Fukuyaku (服薬, "taking medicine") in quotation marks is the book's central joke and its deepest truth. The scare quotes acknowledge the absurdity of treating a cat like a pill, while the narrative proceeds to prove that, in some ways, a cat functions exactly like medicine: it must be administered regularly, it has side effects, it requires compliance, and it alters the patient's internal chemistry. The four adjectives applied to cats — kimagure (気紛れ, fickle/capricious), sensai (繊細, delicate/sensitive), te ga kakaru (手がかかる, demanding/high-maintenance), airurushii (愛くるしい, adorable/charming) — form a perfect haiku-like portrait of felinity. The phrase sukoshizutsu (少しずつ, little by little) emphasizes that healing is gradual, not miraculous. There are no sudden cures in this clinic.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loses

The series' charm depends heavily on Kyoto dialect, medical register, and the cultural specificity of Japanese mental health discourse. Much of this is difficult to replicate in English.

1. Shohou Itashimasu (処方いたします) — The Humble-Polite Prescription

JP 猫を処方いたします。
EN "We Prescribe You a Cat" / "A Cat Is Prescribed"
Loss Itashimasu is not merely polite; it is the humble form of suru, used when the speaker lowers themselves to show respect. It evokes traditional shopkeepers, doctors in white coats, and formal service. English "prescribe" is neutral; it carries none of this bowing, self-effacing warmth. The title sounds, in Japanese, like something a kindly old pharmacist would say as they slide a warm bundle of fur across the counter instead of a pill bottle.

2. Kyoto Dialect (Kyo-kotoba)

JP ほんまは新患さんはお断りしてるんですけど
EN "Actually, we normally turn away new patients, but……"
Loss Honma (ほんま) is quintessentially Kansai — warmer, softer, and more intimate than standard hontou ni. Nike-sensei's speech is peppered with Kyoto-isms that signal his local roots and his unpretentious character. In English, he becomes merely "casual"; the specifically Kyoto flavor of his flippancy — the way Kyoto people are famously indirect, polite, and slightly elusive — is flattened. A Kyoto reader hears "one of us"; an English reader hears "a laid-back doctor."

3. Fukuyaku (服薬) — "Taking" a Cat

JP 猫を「服薬」する患者たち
EN "patients who 'take' their cat as medicine"
Loss Fukuyaku is a formal medical term for medication adherence — the act of following a prescription correctly. It implies dosage, timing, and compliance. Applying it to a cat, even in quotes, creates a conceptual frame: the cat is not a pet; it is a treatment protocol. The English "take" is too generic to carry this bureaucratic precision. The joke is that cats, unlike pills, cannot be taken on schedule; they take you on their schedule. The word fukuyaku sets up this reversal.

4. Kokoro no Fuchou (心の不調)

JP 心の不調を抱えてこの病院を訪れた患者
EN "patients who visit this hospital with troubled hearts"
Loss Kokoro no fuchou literally means "disorder of the heart/mind." It is gentler and more holistic than clinical terms like seishin byou (mental illness) or utsu (depression). It encompasses anxiety, grief, burnout, loneliness, and existential malaise without diagnosing. English "troubled hearts" captures some of this but sounds more poetic and less everyday. In Japanese, kokoro no fuchou is the kind of thing ordinary people say about themselves when they know something is wrong but cannot name it medically.

5. The Address as Incantation

JP 麩屋町通上ル六角通西入……呪文のように長い住所
EN "an address as long as an incantation…… up Fuyacho Street, west on Rokkaku Street"
Loss Kyoto's addressing system is culturally unique and functionally maddening. Streets are navigated by agaru (go up/north), sagaru (go down/south), nishiiru (enter from west), and higashiiru (enter from east). The joke that these directions sound like a jumon (magic spell) only works if you have experienced the frustration of trying to find a place in Kyoto. For English readers, the charm lies in the metaphor; for Japanese readers, it is a knowing laugh of recognition.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns drawn from the title, synopsis, and key passages, useful for intermediate to advanced learners.

〜を〜いたします（謙譲語） N4-N3 / Keigo

Wo ~ itashimasu: the humble form of wo ~ suru, used to lower the speaker's status.

猫を処方いたします。

"We humbly prescribe you a cat." — Itashimasu is the humble form of suru. In modern Japanese, this pattern is common in service industries and formal correspondence.

〜んです（説明・理由） N4

N desu (often n' desu): explanatory/emphatic copula, giving a reason or explanation.

ほんまは新患さんはお断りしてるんですけど

"The truth is, we normally turn away new patients, but……" — The n desu softens the refusal and invites the listener into the speaker's reasoning. The kedo (but) at the end leaves the sentence open, creating suspense.

〜てくれはった（京都方言・敬語） N3 / Dialect

Kyoto dialect honorific form of te kureta (did for me), showing gratitude for someone's action.

せっかくここまで来てくれはったんでね

"You went to all the trouble of coming here, after all……" — Kurehatta is the Kyoto honorific of kureta. Standard Japanese would be kite kureta or kite kudasatta.

〜ながらも（逆接） N2

Nagara mo: although / even though / despite (stronger contrast than nagara alone).

戸惑いながらも、決められた日数、猫を「服薬」する

"Despite their confusion, they 'take' their cat for the prescribed number of days." — The mo intensifies the contrast between bewilderment and compliance.

〜ことで（手段・方法） N3

Koto de: by doing / through the act of (nominalizer + particle).

猫と暮らすことで、彼らの心も少しずつ変化していく

"By living with cats, their hearts gradually begin to change." — Koto de turns the verb phrase into a noun clause, allowing it to function as a means or method.

〜ずつ（分配・反復） N4

Zutsu: apiece / each / gradually / little by little.

彼らの心も少しずつ変化していく

"Their hearts gradually change, little by little." — Sukoshizutsu (少しずつ) is one of the most common zutsu collocations, emphasizing incremental change.

〜けど（接続助詞） N5-N4

Kedo: but / although (casual form of keredomo).

お断りしてるんですけど、特別ですよ

"We turn them away, but you're special." — In casual speech, kedo often functions less as a strong "but" and more as a softener or turn-giver, inviting the listener to infer what comes next.

〜に導かれて（受身形） N3

Ni michibikarete: led/guided by (passive form of michibiku).

呪文のよう長い住所に導かれ……病院を訪れた患者たち

"Patients who, led by an address as long as an incantation, visited the clinic……" — The passive form removes the agent; no one actively guides the patients. They are drawn by fate, word-of-mouth, or something almost supernatural.

Study tip: This series is excellent for N3-N2 learners because it blends standard written Japanese (descriptions, narration) with authentic Kyoto dialect (Nike-sensei's dialogue). The contrast trains your ear to distinguish register. Pay attention to how Nike-sensei's honma, kurehatta, and yattemasu n de create a voice that is simultaneously professional and deeply local — a Kyoto doctor who happens to prescribe cats.
