01 Verse 1

鉄の弾が 正義の証明
Tetsu no tama ga, seigi no shoumei.
"The iron bullet is the proof of justice."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
鉄tetsuNouniron; steel
のnoGenitive particleof; 's
弾tamaNounbullet; ball; sphere
がgaSubject particlemarks the subject
正義seigiNounjustice; righteousness
のnoGenitive particleof
証明shoumeiNounproof; evidence; demonstration
Grammar: Two juxtaposed noun phrases with the copula omitted, a common feature of poetic and lyrical Japanese. 鉄の弾が正義の証明(だ) carries the full sentence meaning with だ dropped for compression and impact. The stark equation of "bullet = justice" establishes the song's central moral paradox immediately. The particle が marks 鉄の弾 as the subject of this implied copular sentence.
貫けば 英雄に近づいた
Tsuranukeba, eiyuu ni chikazuita.
"If it pierces through, I drew closer to being a hero."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
貫けばtsuranukebaVerb (conditional)if (it) pierces through — conditional of 貫く tsuranuku
英雄eiyuuNounhero
にniDirectional particleto; toward
近づいたchikazuitaVerb (past)drew near; approached — past of 近づく chikazuku
Grammar: [Verb in conditional] + ば sets up a conditional clause: "if / when [verb]." 近づく is an intransitive motion verb meaning "to approach, to draw near," and it takes に to mark the target or direction. The main clause is in the past tense (近づいた), creating a lyrical structure where the condition and result are compressed into a single fatal realization: the act of piercing (killing) made the speaker feel closer to heroism. The moral horror lies in how natural this equation once felt.

JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: [Te-form] + みる is the attemptive auxiliary: "to try doing [verb]." Here, 触れてみる = "to try touching." The conditional form みれば then turns this into "if (I) try to touch." The te-form 閉じて connects the two actions sequentially: closing the eyes, then attempting to touch. The line imagines reaching out to the dead enemy and discovering their shared humanity. The conditional frame creates a hypothetical space where empathy becomes possible, if only for a moment.
同じ形 同じ体温の悪魔

"A demon of the same shape, the same body temperature."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: This line uses poetic omission: the full grammatical form would be 同じ形(をし)た、同じ体温の悪魔 or 同じ形で、同じ体温の悪魔. The particle の links the modifiers to 悪魔, but 同じ形 lacks its own の, creating a compressed, breathless rhythm. The revelation is devastating: the enemy is not a monster but a human with the same body. The word 悪魔 is redefined; it no longer refers to the enemy but to the shared condition of being human in a world that demands killing.

02 Verse 2

僕はメで あいつはいいの？
Boku wa dame de, aitsu wa ii no?
"I'm no good, but they're okay?"

はwaTopic particleas for me

Grammar: The te-form で connects two contrasting topics in a single emotional question. ダメで = "being no good [I am rejected]," and this condition is contrasted with あいつはいいの (they are acceptable). The final particle の turns the statement into a question, but a soft, wounded one rather than an aggressive interrogation. あいつ is a deliberately distancing pronoun; the speaker refuses to name or humanize the other, even while questioning why they deserve to live more. The line captures the arbitrariness of who gets labeled enemy and who gets labeled hero.
そこに壁があっただけなのに
Soko ni kabe ga atta dake na no ni.
"Even though there was only a wall there."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
そこにsoko niAdverbial phrasethere
壁kabeNounwall; barrier
がgaSubject particlemarks the subject
あったattaVerb (past)existed; was — past of ある aru
だけdakeAdverbial particleonly; merely; just

Grammar: だけ after a verb or copula restricts the meaning to "only; merely." あっただけ = "there was only." なのに combines the explanatory/emphatic のだ with the concessive particle に, creating a phrase of frustrated contradiction: "despite the fact that ~ / even though ~." The wall is both literal (the walls of Paradis) and metaphorical (the arbitrary border between "us" and "them"). The tragedy is that something so simple, so constructed, is enough to justify mass violence. The concessive grammar carries the weight of this injustice.

JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: [Te-form] + しまう expresses completion with a shade of regret or irreversibility: "to end up ~ing; to unfortunately ~." 生まれてしまった = "having ended up being born," suggesting that being born into this world of conflict is itself a kind of tragedy. The phrase modifies 運命 with an implied の. 嘆くな is the negative imperative: [Verb in dictionary form] + な = "don't [verb]." The command is stark and almost cruel in its optimism; it rejects self-pity while acknowledging that the world into which one is born is fundamentally unjust.
僕らはみんな 自由なんだから

"Because we are all free."

Freedom in this context is not a gift but a burden; because we are free, we must bear the weight of our choices, including the choice to kill. The explanatory のだ insists on this logic as an undeniable truth.

03 Pre-Chorus

Grammar: [Noun] + のように is the standard comparative construction: "like [noun]; in the manner of [noun]." あれば is the conditional form of ある (to exist; to have). The hypothetical is classic: if humans had wings, they could escape their boundaries. But the song immediately undercuts this freedom in the next lines. The bird metaphor resonates deeply with Attack on Titan's imagery of caged humans and the desire to break free.
どこへだって行けるけど
Doko e datte ikeru kedo.
"We could go anywhere, but..."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: どこへだて combines the interrogative どこ (where) with へ (directional particle) and だって (even; emphatic inclusive marker). Together: "even to anywhere" = "anywhere at all." 行ける is the potential form of 行く: "can go." The trailing けど sets up the crucial contrast of the pre-chorus: physical freedom means nothing without an emotional anchor. The grammar of concession (けど) mirrors the thematic concession that freedom without belonging is empty.
帰る場所が なければ

"If there's no place to return to."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: 帰る場所 is a noun phrase where the attributive verb 帰る modifies 場所: "a place to which one returns." なければ is the negative conditional form of ある, created by conjugating the negative adjective ない: ない → なければ. It means "if [something] does not exist." The condition is heartbreaking in its simplicity: without a home, without someone waiting, flight becomes exile rather than freedom.
きっとどこへも行けない
Kitto doko e mo ikenai.
"Surely, we cannot go anywhere."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: In negative sentences, も after an interrogative word creates a universal negative: どこへも行けない = "cannot go anywhere" (literally: "cannot go, even to where"). きっと is an epistemic adverb expressing strong conviction: "surely; no doubt." The potential negative 行けない combines ability (potential) with negation. The line completes the pre-chorus's logical chain: wings grant physical freedom, but without a place to return to, that freedom collapses into paralysis. The speaker cannot move because there is nowhere meaningful to move toward.

Chorus

The line rejects passive existence; the speaker demands that life have meaning, direction, and stakes, even if those stakes require terrible choices. This rejection of mere survival is what drives the song's tragic heroism.

The grammar of concession (それでも) makes love an act of resistance rather than complacency.

Chorus

"Even if it is a mistake, I will not doubt."

This is the logic of the demon child, the one who chooses love over morality.

Like the opening line, the copula is omitted, leaving a stark noun phrase: "[the] scene of iron rain scattering." The repetition of 鉄 (iron) from the first line creates a bookend effect, but where 鉄の弾 was a single bullet of justice, 鉄の雨 is collective, indiscriminate destruction.

Grammar: それなら is a conditional conjunction: "if that [is true], then." It introduces a logical consequence or challenge to what was just said. なんで is the casual equivalent of なぜ or どうして (why). The question is rhetorical and self-accusing: if war is truly "unrelated," why does the speaker feel hatred at all? The emotion betrays the claimed indifference.

07 Bridge

"If these words too are translated."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: 訳される is the passive form of 訳す (to translate; to interpret). The passive conditional 訳されれば = "if [they] are translated." The line introduces a metalinguistic concern: language itself fails to carry meaning across boundaries, whether linguistic, cultural, or emotional. The inclusive particle も (too; even) suggests this is one more thing among many that cannot be faithfully transmitted. The bridge shifts from political contradiction to epistemological doubt: if words can be translated, can they still mean what they originally meant?

Grammar: 伝わる is the intransitive/passive counterpart of 伝える (to convey; to transmit). It means "to be conveyed; to be transmitted; to get across." The negative form 伝わらない expresses impossibility: the true meaning will fail to reach its destination. This is not a failure of effort but a structural limit of language and mediation. The line justifies the speaker's turn away from words toward direct experience in the lines that follow.

Chorus

"The shadow of the chosen person, the corpse of what was discarded."

Grammar: Two parallel noun phrases built on the same pattern: [Verb in past attributive] + [Noun] + の + [Noun]. 選んだ人の影 = "the shadow of the person [who was] chosen." 捨てたものの屍 = "the corpse of what was discarded." もの is a pronominal noun referring to people or things in a generalized, often pitiful sense. 屍 (shikabane) is a literary, almost biblical word for corpse, elevating the diction. The juxtaposition is brutal: every choice creates a shadow (the one saved) and a corpse (the one sacrificed). The grammar is bare, refusing to soften the arithmetic of survival.
Outro

Grammar: 気づいたんだ combines the past tense of 気づく (to realize; to notice) with the explanatory のだ: "I have realized [the fact that] ~." 育つのは nominalizes 育つ: "what grows; the thing that grows." The structure 育つのは悪魔の子だ is a cleft-like sentence where the nominalized verb becomes the topic: "What grows is a child of evil." The realization is not that the speaker has become evil, but that evil has been growing within them all along, nurtured by the very choices they made to protect what they loved. The grammar of realization (気づく) plus nominalization (のは) makes this a formal, almost clinical self-diagnosis.

正義の裏 犠牲の中 心には悪魔の子

"Behind justice, within sacrifice, in my heart is a child of evil."

The copula is omitted, as in the opening line, creating a stark equation: [In these places] = [demon child]. The song ends by naming its subject: the demon child is not the enemy, not the other, but the self that grows in the hidden spaces behind every act of justice and inside every sacrifice.

09 Key Grammar Patterns Summary

〜のは〜だ / 〜のに
Nominalization + topic/copula: "What ~ is ~" / concessive: "even though / and yet." Used to define righteousness and express tragic contradiction.
〜ても / 〜にしても / 〜だとしても
Concessive patterns: "even if; no matter what." The chorus stacks these to express absolute, unconditional commitment.
〜たりしない
"Don't do things like ~; never ~." The tari-form + negative expresses categorical refusal: "I will never doubt."
〜とは
Definitional: "What ~ means is..." Used to redefine righteousness as subjective conviction rather than external morality.
〜きれない / 〜やしない
Emphatic inability: "can't completely ~" and "absolutely cannot ~." Expresses the limits of suppression and explanation.
〜ばかり / 〜ばっか
"Only; nothing but." The casual ばっか form adds raw frustration: "nothing but contradictions."
〜なんて
Belittling or exclamatory: "things like ~ / how ~." Dismisses war as foolish, then expresses dismay at human contradiction.
〜てしまう / 〜のだ
Regretful completion + explanatory emphasis: "ended up ~ing; the fact is that ~." Adds emotional weight to realizations.
