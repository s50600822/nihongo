02 German Motto — Hunter and Prey

Sind Sie das Essen? Nein, wir sind der Jäger! DE
"Are you the food? No, we are the hunter!"
The iconic German exchange that frames humanity's reversal from prey to predator. Essen (food/meal) represents the Titans' view of humans; Jäger (hunter) reclaims agency.
GermanPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: The German uses the formal second-person Sie (you) in a question-answer structure. Sind Sie das Essen? uses verb-subject inversion for the yes/no question. Nein, wir sind der Jäger. uses the first-person plural wir (we) with the masculine nominative der Jäger. The line plays on the ambiguity of the original Japanese: the ASR/misheard version often transcribed as "Sie sind das Essen und wir sind die Jäger" (You are the food and we are the hunters), but the official lyrics use the question-answer form. The use of formal Sie creates a distancing, almost military tone, as if addressing the Titans directly across a battlefield. Jäger is cognate with English "yeoman" and "hunter," carrying Germanic heroic resonances.

The compound feuerrot combines Feuer (fire) + rot (red), parallel to Japanese 紅蓮 (guren, crimson lotus). German strong adjective declension appears when no article carries the case ending. Pfeil und Bogen forms a natural pair like English "bow and arrow" (though German reverses the order: arrow and bow). The line functions as a subtitle or second title, grounding the Japanese 紅蓮の弓矢 in Germanic martial vocabulary.

The nominalizer のは turns the verb phrase into a noun for topicalization. The use of 《》 (double angle brackets) marks the phrase as a conceptual or thematic key term.

Grammar: 屍踏み越えて uses the noun 屍 (corpse, literary reading kabane instead of common shikabane) + compound verb te-form 踏み越えて (stepping over = 踏む + 越える): "Stepping over corpses." The te-form here creates a participial phrase setting the scene for the following address. 進む意思を 嗤う豚よ uses the attributive phrase 進む意思 (will to advance = 進む + 意思) + object を + literary verb 嗤う (sneer at = warau, classical/written form of 笑) + noun 豚 (pig) + vocative よ: "O pigs who sneer at the will to advance." The pigs metaphorically represent those who accept submission and mock those who resist. 家畜の安寧 / 虚偽の繁栄 uses parallel possessive phrases: "The peace of livestock / The false prosperity." Both are noun phrases without verbs, functioning as accusatives in an implied sentence: "[We reject] the peace of livestock, [we reject] the false prosperity." 死せる餓狼の「自由」を！ uses the classical attributive 死せる (dead = 死す, a classical verb meaning "to die," + せる attributive) + noun 餓狼 (starving wolf) + possessive の + quoted noun 「自由」 (freedom) + object particle を + implied imperative: "[Give us] the 'freedom' of the dead-starved wolves!" The classical 死せる carries Shinto funeral-text resonance, elevating the wolves to tragic/heroic status. The quotation marks around 自由 question whether true freedom can exist for the dead, or frame it as a concept wrested back from the pigs' mockery.

The compound verb 研ぎ澄ます combines 研ぐ (sharpen, as a blade) + 澄ます (clear; purify), suggesting both physical and spiritual refinement. The rough pronoun お前 creates a direct, almost confrontational address to the listener/fighter.

The idiom die Zähne zeigen (show one's teeth) means to show defiance or aggression.

A 尖兵 is a military advance scout, suggesting courage as the forwardmost element of the freedom movement. 奔る奴隷に勝利を！ uses the literary attributive 奔る (running = はしる, classical form) + noun 奴隷に (to the slaves [dative]) + noun 勝利を (victory [object]) + implied imperative: "Victory to the running slaves!" The classical form 奔る instead of 走る carries epic/poetic resonance, evoking images of charging cavalry or stampeding forces.

12 Key Grammar Patterns Summary

Classical and Literary Japanese in Modern Lyrics
死せる (shi-seru, classical attributive of 死す), 其の (sono, classical demonstrative), 嗤う (warau, literary for 笑う), 屠る (hofuru, classical for slaughter), 迸る (hotobashiru, literary for surging), 穿つ (ugatsu, literary for piercing), 奔る (hashiru, classical for running), 止めどなき (tome-donaki, classical negative), 架せられる (kaserareru, causative-passive). Revo deploys classical grammar and literary vocabulary to create an epic, biblical register within a modern rock song.
Nominalization and Topicalization: のは / もの / 事
変えるのは戦う覚悟だ (what changes [it] is resolve), 何かを捨てる事が出来るもの ([one] who can throw away something), 変える事が出来るのは (what can change [something]). The grammar of turning verbs into nouns for philosophical statements about agency and capability.
Passive and Suffering Passive
支配されていた (was being dominated), 囚われていた (was imprisoned), 踏まれた (was trampled), 囚われた (imprisoned), 架せられた (was imposed), 奪われた (was stolen), 侵されながら (while being violated). The grammar of victimhood, oppression, and violated agency that drives the song's revolutionary narrative.
〜て + ながら / 〜ずに / 〜ままで (Concessive and Continuative)
灼きながら (while burning), 知らずに (without knowing), 背負わないままで (while not bearing), 侵されながら (while being violated). The grammar of simultaneous action, negative condition, and maintained state in contexts of struggle and sacrifice.
〜でも...ない / 〜ところで (Negative and Concessive)
凶器でも技術でもない (is neither weapon nor technique), 祈ったところで何も変わらない (even if [you] pray, nothing changes). The grammar of negation and futile condition that frames the necessity of violent action.
German Grammar in J-Pop: Questions, Relatives, and Idioms
Sind Sie das Essen? (verb-second question), Wer nur...beklagt (relative clause with wer), zum Schwert greifen (idiom for taking up arms), die Zähne zeigen (idiom for showing defiance), zweischneidige Klinge (compound adjective). The grammar of German military-poetic rhetoric embedded in a Japanese song structure.
Buddhist and Classical Mythology Vocabulary
紅蓮 (guren, crimson lotus of hell), 嚆矢 (koushi, signal arrow), 餓狼 (garou, starving wolf), 宵闇 (yoiyami, dusk), 冥府 (meifu, underworld), 屍 (kabane, corpse), 緋 (hi, scarlet). The specialized lexicon of death, war, and the afterlife drawn from Buddhist, Shinto, and classical Japanese sources.
Quotation Marks and Brackets as Thematic Framing
《不本意な現状》, 《狩人》(Jäger), 《殺意》, 「標的」, 「自由」, 《少年》. Revo uses 《》 (double angle brackets) and 「」 (corner brackets) to mark concepts as contested, ironic, or archetypal, creating a meta-textual layer where words are weapons and labels.
Imperative and Vocative: Commanding the Arrow and the Hunter
壁を越え海まで飛んで行け (fly beyond the walls), 死せる餓狼の「自由」を ([give us] freedom), 奔る奴隷に勝利を (victory to the slaves), 己を矢に込めて (pour yourself into the arrow), 全てを貫いて征け (pierce through everything and conquer).
