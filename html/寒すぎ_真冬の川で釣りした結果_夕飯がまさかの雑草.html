Grammar: なよ is a compound sentence-ending particle combining the prohibitive な (don't) with the assertive よ (I tell you): "[Hey], don't [do that]." Its repetition adds urgency and is commonly used with children and animals. やっとおった uses the Kansai dialect verb おる (oru, to exist [animate]) in past tense おった: "finally found [one]." The vlogger repeats this for emphasis, creating a rhythmic expression of relief after prolonged searching. いっぱい耕しちゃった uses いっぱい (a lot; full) + 耕す (to till; to plow; to dig up) + ちゃった (regrettably did): "[I] dug up a lot [regrettably]." The と coordinates the items, and の assigns each ingredient to its dish. でこっちがニラ玉 uses で (and; then) + こっち (this [side]; here): "And this [side] is nira-tama." こっち is the colloquial form of こちら, and its use here creates an informal, pointing gesture. 雑草に紛れて生えてたニラと卵焼いたやつ uses the attributive clause 雑草に紛れて生えてた (was growing mixed in with weeds) modifying ニラ, + と (with) + 卵焼いた (fried egg) + やつ (thing): "[The] thing [made by] frying eggs with nira that was growing mixed in with weeds." やつ is a casual demonstrative noun that can refer to things or people, and its use here creates a humble, unpretentious description of the dish. ほんで uses the Kansai conjunction ほんで (hon de, and then; so), a variant of それで or 本で: "And then." これの皮滅の唐揚げで全く関係ないこう振ってこんな感じ uses a complex chain: [this] の [kawabou karaage] で [completely unrelated] [like this] 振って (toss) [て] こんな感じ (this kind of feeling): "And with this kawabou karaage, [it is] completely unrelated [but] toss [it] like this, [and it becomes] this kind of feeling." The で here is the instrumental/manner particle, and こう is the manner demonstrative "like this." The phrase acknowledges that the karaage is unrelated to the other dishes but contributes to the overall meal aesthetic when tossed together. やった is the success exclamation, marking the menu improvisation as a victory. ちょっとここに唐揚げ、魚の唐揚げ入るはずやったんけど uses ちょっと (a bit) + ここに (here) + noun phrase + 入るはずやった (was supposed to go in) + んけど (but): "[There] was supposed to be karaage, fish karaage, [going] here a bit, but..." The Kansai past やった (from だった) attaches to はず (supposed to), and んけど softens the contrast. メニュー変更してかき揚げになりました uses the suru-verb メニュー変更 (menu change) in te-form + なりました (became): "[the] menu changed and [it] became kakiage." The spontaneous-change なる marks the dish transformation as something that happened rather than was deliberately chosen, preserving the improvisational narrative. じゃあ食べていきます uses じゃあ (well then) + directional progressive 食べていきます: "Well then, [I will] go eat." The いく marks the eating as a process about to begin, a standard meal-starting formula. いただきます is the canonical Japanese meal blessing, derived from the humble verb いただく (to receive humbly). It expresses gratitude to all who contributed to the meal, including the plants, the land, and the animals. Its use here after a foraged meal carries special weight, acknowledging the direct relationship between the vlogger and the natural sources of the food.

Grammar: まずやっぱり白ご飯から uses まず (first of all) + やっぱり (after all) + 白ご飯 (white rice) + から (from): "First, [I will start] from white rice after all." The やっぱり frames the rice-first approach as the natural, expected order, reaffirming traditional Japanese meal etiquette even in an improvised foraged meal. うん and うんふ are eating interjections, non-lexical sounds of satisfaction and chewing that add visceral authenticity to the tasting sequence. サクサクや uses the onomatopoeia サクサク (crispy; crunchy; light and flaky) + Kansai copula や: "[It is] crispy, ya." サクサク is the standard Japanese texture word for fried foods, tempura, and pastries, and its use here describes the kakiage. The Kansai や replaces standard だ, marking the vlogger's dialect consistently through the tasting. なんかタンポポは何食べてるかわからへん uses なんか (somehow) + topic タンポは + embedded question 何食べてるか (what [I am] eating) + Kansai negative わからへん: "Somehow [I] don't know what [I am] eating with the dandelion." The topic は topicalizes the dandelion, and the embedded question turns the eating experience into a guessing game. The Kansai わからへん (from わからない) maintains dialect consistency. ムカゴはイモって感じ uses the quotative って (colloquial for と/という) + 感じ (feeling): "Mukago [has] a potato-like feeling." The quotative って感じ is a ubiquitous Japanese colloquial pattern for describing the impression or category of something: "[It is] like a potato." This is the grammar of taste categorization, where an unfamiliar wild food is mapped onto a familiar domestic equivalent. ムカゴ独特の香りがある uses the attributive phrase ムカゴ独特の (mukago's distinctive) modifying 香り (aroma) + がある (there is): "Mukago has a distinctive aroma." The がある existential frames the aroma as a property possessed by the mukago, and 独特 (dokutoku, distinctive; unique) elevates the description from mere taste to sensory characterization. ご視聴ありがとうございました uses the honorific ご- prefix on 視聴 (viewing; watching) + past polite ありがとうございました: "Thank you very much for watching." The past tense marks the video as completed, and the honorific elevates the audience, a standard Japanese vlog closing formula.

〜てくれません / 〜てくれる (Benefactive)
出てきてくれません. "Won't come out for me." The grammar of animals and nature doing or withholding favors.
〜そうにない (Negative Conjectural)
釣れそうにない. "Does not seem catchable." The grammar of assessing possibility from observable signs.
〜だらけ (Excessive Suffix)
穴だらけ. "Full of holes." The grammar of negative excess applied to environmental description.
〜気配がない (Absence of Signs)
食べてくれる気配がない. "No sign of biting." The grammar of reading absence through subtle environmental cues.
〜てみる (Trial Auxiliary)

〜ばっかりじゃん (Restrictive + Emphatic)
ないのばっかりじゃん. "Only ones that aren't here, jeez!" The grammar of colloquial frustration in live narration.
