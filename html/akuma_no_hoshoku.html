Title Analysis

Title Grammar: 悪魔の飽食

The title Akuma no Hōshoku (悪魔の飽食) is a noun phrase of devastating simplicity: [devil] + [genitive] + [gluttony / satiation]. The word akuma (悪魔, "devil / demon / evil spirit") is not a casual metaphor in Japanese; it carries the full weight of Buddhist and Christian demonology, of beings that have abandoned their humanity for absolute evil. The genitive particle no (の) creates a possessive relationship that is also descriptive: this is not merely "the devil's gluttony" but "devilish gluttony" — gluttony of such magnitude and moral depravity that it can only be described as demonic. Hōshoku (飽食) combines hō (飽, "satiety / having eaten enough") and shoku (食, "food / eating / meal"), and it means "gluttony / eating to satiation / feasting." But hōshoku is not merely overeating; in the context of this book, it is the satiation of scientific curiosity through the consumption of human lives — the "feeding" of a research apparatus on living human bodies. The title thus transforms Unit 731 from a military institution into a demonic organism with an appetite, a creature that devours people in order to grow its knowledge. The grammar is the grammar of horror: two nouns, one particle, and a universe of moral condemnation.

Title Breakdown

悪魔 akuma "devil / demon / evil spirit" — The word akuma (悪魔) combines aku (悪, "evil / bad / wickedness") and ma (魔, "demon / devil / magic / that which obstructs Buddhist practice"). In Japanese religious vocabulary, ma refers specifically to the forces that obstruct enlightenment — the Mara of Buddhist cosmology who tempted the Buddha. Combined with aku, the word becomes a comprehensive term for absolute moral evil, a being that has not merely committed evil acts but has become evil itself. By calling Unit 731 "devils," Morimura is making a theological judgment, not merely a moral one: these were not soldiers following orders or scientists pursuing knowledge; they were beings who had crossed a line into a category of existence defined by the destruction of others. The choice of akuma over milder terms like waru (悪, "evil") or akutoku (悪徳, "vice / immorality") is significant: akuma is a category of being, not merely a quality of action. Once you are an akuma, you are no longer human; you have become something else. The title thus frames the book's investigation as an inquiry into non-humanity — the process by which human beings, through the structures of war and institutional loyalty, transform themselves into demons. The word also carries Christian connotations for Japanese readers familiar with Western literature and film: the devil as the adversary, the tempter, the one who bargains for souls. Unit 731's scientists made precisely such a bargain — trading their medical ethics for military advancement, their Hippocratic oaths for data points.
の no genitive particle — The particle no (の) creates a possessive or attributive relationship between akuma and hōshoku. In akuma no hōshoku, the no can be read in multiple ways: possession ("the devil's gluttony"), attribution ("devilish gluttony"), or even apposition ("the gluttony that is devilish"). This ambiguity is productive because it allows the title to function on multiple levels simultaneously. On the surface, it refers to the gluttony of the devils who ran Unit 731 — their insatiable appetite for experimental subjects, for data, for the power that knowledge conferred. At a deeper level, it describes the gluttony itself as demonic — the very act of consuming human beings for research purposes is a form of devilishness, regardless of who performs it. The no thus both particularizes (this was the gluttony of specific devils) and universalizes (this gluttony is itself demonic). The grammatical simplicity of the title — just two nouns and a particle — creates a density of meaning that a longer title could not achieve. There are no verbs, no qualifiers, no excuses: just the fact of demonic gluttony, stated as a noun phrase that functions as an accusation.
飽食 hōshoku "gluttony / satiation / eating one's fill" — The word hōshoku (飽食) is composed of hō (飽, "satiety / having had enough / being full") and shoku (食, "food / eating / meal"). In ordinary usage, hōshoku describes the state of having eaten to satisfaction — a neutral or even positive condition. But in the context of this title, the word takes on horrifying connotations: the "satiety" is achieved not through food but through human suffering; the "eating" is the consumption of lives in the name of science. The word suggests not merely hunger but excess — the Unit did not just experiment on a few people; it conducted experiments on thousands, with a systematic thoroughness that can only be described as gluttonous. The hō (飽) element is particularly important: it means "having had enough," which implies that the devils could have stopped but chose not to. They were not starving; they were gorging. They had sufficient data, sufficient subjects, sufficient knowledge — and yet they continued, because the structure of the institution and the logic of war demanded ever more consumption. The title thus contains a theory of evil: evil is not always the result of necessity or ignorance; sometimes it is the result of satiation without limit, of appetite without conscience. What is lost in English is the specifically visceral quality of hōshoku — the word carries the physical memory of eating, of fullness, of the body's satisfaction. In English, "gluttony" is a theological sin; in Japanese, hōshoku is a physical state that has become morally charged. The English reader sees a moral judgment; the Japanese reader feels a bodily revulsion.
Full title context: The complete title of the first edition was Akuma no Hōshoku: "Kantōgun Saikinsen Butai" Kyōfu no Zenbō! (悪魔の飽食 「関東軍細菌戦部隊」恐怖の全貌!, "The Devil's Gluttony: The Terrifying Full Picture of the 'Kwantung Army Bacteriological Warfare Unit'!"). The subtitle performs an important documentary function: it identifies the specific institution being accused (Kantōgun Saikinsen Butai / 関東軍細菌戦部隊, "Kwantung Army Bacteriological Warfare Unit") and promises the "full picture" (zenbō / 全貌) of its horror (kyōfu / 恐怖). The quotation marks around the unit's official designation signal Morimura's skepticism toward the military's sanitizing language — this was not merely a "prevention and water supply unit" (bōeki kyūsui-bu / 防疫給水部) as the army claimed, but a bacteriological warfare unit. The exclamation mark at the end of the subtitle is the only emotional punctuation in the title, and it functions as a cry of outrage — the author's own voice breaking through the documentary restraint.
Author

森村 誠一 — Morimura Seiichi (1933–2023)

森

森村 誠一

Morimura Seiichi · 1933–2023 · Saitama / Tokyo

Morimura Seiichi was one of Japan's most prolific and socially engaged writers, a novelist who used the skills of mystery fiction to investigate real crimes — both individual and historical. Born on January 2, 1933, in Kumagaya, Saitama Prefecture, he was twelve years old when the Pacific War ended, old enough to remember the final air raids and the atmosphere of militarism, young enough to spend his adult life questioning what his country had done. The Kumagaya air raid of August 14-15, 1945 destroyed his family home and his beloved book collection — an experience he later described as the "original trauma" that shaped his desire to become a writer. The boy whose books were burned by war grew into the man who refused to let history be buried.

After graduating from Aoyama Gakuin University's Department of English and American Literature, Morimura spent nearly a decade working in hotels — first in Tokyo, then in Osaka. The hotel world gave him two things that would define his fiction: an intimate knowledge of social hierarchy and class difference, and a front-row seat to the hidden lives of strangers. His hotel mysteries — beginning with his debut — are masterpieces of social observation, using the confined space of a luxury hotel to expose the inequalities and secrets of Japanese society. In 1965, he made his literary debut under the pen name Yukishiro Keitarō with a business book; in 1969, he won the 15th Edogawa Rampo Prize for Kōsō no Shikaku (高層の死角, "The Blind Spot in the High-Rise"), a hotel-set mystery that launched his career as a professional novelist.

The 1970s established Morimura as the leading practitioner of shakai-ha (社会派, "social school") mystery fiction — mysteries that used the puzzle-plot structure to investigate social problems: corruption, discrimination, environmental destruction, the dark side of Japan's economic miracle. Fushoku no Kōzō (腐蝕の構造, "The Structure of Corrosion," 1973) won the Japan Mystery Writers Association Prize and confirmed his reputation. But it was Ningen no Shōmei (人間の証明, "Proof of the Man," 1976) that made him a household name — a blockbuster that sold over 7.7 million copies, was adapted into a hit film, and established the "proof" (shōmei / 証明) series that would define his popular reputation. The novel's famous tagline — "Read it first, or see it first" — became a cultural phenomenon, and the theme song became one of the best-selling singles in Japanese music history.

By 1981, Morimura was at the height of his commercial success — and he chose to risk it all. While serializing a novel about nuclear power (Shi no Utsuwa / 死の器, "Vessel of Death") in the newspaper Akahata (赤旗, "Red Flag"), the organ of the Japan Communist Party, he received a letter from a former Unit 731 member: "What you've written about Unit 731 is nothing compared to the reality. If you want to know the truth, I'll cooperate with your investigation." This letter changed Morimura's life. With the financial resources from Ningen no Shōmei and the journalistic assistance of Shimosato Masaki, a reporter for Akahata, Morimura embarked on an investigation that would take him to Harbin, the former site of Unit 731's headquarters, and into interviews with former unit members, victims, and historians. The result was Akuma no Hōshoku (悪魔の飽食), published in November 1981 by Kōbunsha — a book that would sell over three million copies, be translated into multiple languages, and subject its author to years of harassment, threats, and physical danger.

The backlash was immediate and vicious. When the sequel contained erroneously included photographs (some unrelated images mixed among genuine materials), right-wing media and political groups seized on the error to attack the entire work as fraudulent. Morimura's office door was splashed with red paint. He received continuous threatening phone calls. Stones were thrown at his windows at night. Right-wing sound trucks (gaisensha / 街宣車) surrounded his home. He was forced to wear a bulletproof vest when leaving his house, and the Kanagawa Prefectural Police provided constant protection, warning him to avoid leaving the prefecture. The book was temporarily discontinued — but Kadokawa Shoten later republished it, with Kadokawa's president himself requiring security protection. Morimura never retracted the book's central claims; he corrected the photo error and continued to stand by his investigation. He went on to publish sequels: Zoku Akuma no Hōshoku (続・悪魔の飽食, 1982), Akuma no Hōshoku Dai-san-bu (悪魔の飽食 第三部, 1983), Akuma no Hōshoku Nōto (悪魔の飽食 ノート, 1982), and No More Akuma no Hōshoku (ノーモア悪魔の飽食, 1984). In 2002, he published a revised and expanded edition.

Morimura continued writing until his late 80s, producing over 300 works across mystery, historical fiction, essays, and reportage. His total sales exceeded 100 million copies. He received the Japan Mystery Literature Grand Prize in 2003 and the Yoshikawa Eiji Literature Prize in 2011 for Akudō (悪道, "The Wicked Path"), a historical novel. He founded the "Yamamura Classroom" (山村教室) in 1999 to nurture young writers. In his final years, he wrote about aging itself — Oiru Imi (老いる意味, "The Meaning of Aging"). He died on July 24, 2023, at the age of 90, from pneumonia. His epitaph might be the words he lived by: "A writer must report on their era" (Sakka wa jidai wo ripōto shinakya ikenai / 作家は時代をリポートしなきゃいけない).

History

Unit 731: The Historical Context

What Was Unit 731?

Unit 731 — officially known as the Epidemic Prevention and Water Supply Department of the Kwantung Army (Kantōgun Bōeki Kyūsui Bu / 関東軍防疫給水部), and later as the Man-shū Dai 731 Butai (満州第七三一部隊, "Manchuria Unit 731") — was a covert biological and chemical warfare research and development unit of the Imperial Japanese Army that operated primarily in Harbin, Manchuria (northeastern China) during the Second Sino-Japanese War and World War II. Under the command of Lieutenant General Ishii Shiro (石井四郎), a microbiologist and army medical officer, the unit conducted lethal human experimentation on Chinese, Korean, Mongolian, Russian, and other prisoners of war.

The unit's activities included: vivisection without anesthesia to study the effects of diseases and injuries; forced infection with pathogens including plague, cholera, anthrax, and typhoid; frostbite experiments in which prisoners' limbs were frozen and then struck with sticks to test tissue damage; weapons testing including the use of prisoners as targets for grenades and flamethrowers; and the development of biological weapons including plague-infested fleas dropped from aircraft onto Chinese cities. Victims were referred to as maruta (丸太, "logs" / "lumber") — a dehumanizing term that reduced living human beings to experimental material, much as Nazi Germany used numbers to erase individual identity.

Estimates of the death toll vary, but most historians agree that at least 3,000 people died in Unit 731's facilities, with the total number of victims of Japan's biological warfare program — including field experiments and attacks on civilian populations — reaching into the hundreds of thousands. The unit's headquarters at Pingfang (平房), a suburb of Harbin, covered six square kilometers and included over 150 buildings, including laboratories, prisons, an airfield, and a crematorium.

The Postwar Cover-Up

At the end of the war in August 1945, Unit 731's members destroyed most of the physical evidence — burning documents, blowing up buildings, and killing remaining prisoners. Ishii Shiro and other senior officers were initially detained by American occupation authorities, but instead of being tried as war criminals, they were granted immunity in exchange for providing the United States with their research data on biological warfare. This arrangement, documented in what became known as the "Thompson Report" and other declassified American documents, meant that the full extent of Unit 731's crimes remained largely unknown to the Japanese public for decades.

The Tokyo War Crimes Tribunal (1946-1948) did not prosecute Unit 731's leaders, and the Soviet Union's Khabarovsk War Crime Trials (1949), which did try some lower-ranking members, received little attention in Japan or the West. For thirty years after the war, Unit 731 existed in a peculiar historical limbo: known to specialists, occasionally mentioned in foreign publications, but largely absent from Japanese public consciousness. This was not accidental — the Japanese government, the American occupation authorities, and the medical establishment all had reasons to keep the story buried. The doctors who had participated in the experiments returned to civilian life as respected physicians, researchers, and medical school professors. The structure of silence was reinforced by shame, by institutional self-protection, and by the Cold War logic that made Japan's biological warfare data valuable to American military planners.

Before Morimura: The Slow Emergence

Before Morimura's book, there had been scattered attempts to bring Unit 731 to light. In 1970, journalist Tsuneishi Keiichi (常石敬一) began researching the unit's history. In May 1981, months before Morimura's book, Tsuneishi published Kieta Saikinsen Butai (消えた細菌戦部隊, "The Disappeared Bacteriological Warfare Unit") through a small publisher (Kaiminsha / 海鳴社). This was a serious academic investigation, but it appeared from an obscure press and did not reach a mass audience. Morimura's achievement was not to be the first to discover Unit 731 — he was not — but to be the first to tell the story with the narrative power and moral urgency that could break through the wall of public indifference. A famous novelist, a bestselling author, was accusing his own country's military of crimes comparable to Auschwitz. The combination of Morimura's literary reputation and the horror of the material made the book impossible to ignore.

The International Dimension

The book's impact extended far beyond Japan. It was translated into Chinese, Korean, Russian, English, and other languages, becoming a key text in the international understanding of Japanese war crimes. In China, where Unit 731's victims were primarily located, the book confirmed what many had known from family memory but could not prove. In South Korea, it resonated with the experience of colonial domination. In the United States, it raised uncomfortable questions about the postwar immunity deal. The book became a focal point for debates about historical memory, war responsibility, and the politics of apology — debates that continue to shape Japan's relationships with its neighbors today.

Story

Synopsis & Structure

The Investigation

The book opens with Morimura's account of how the investigation began: the letter from a former Unit 731 member, the gradual establishment of trust, the first horrifying testimonies. Morimura adopts the stance of a detective novelist approaching a real case — he follows leads, corroborates testimony, builds a picture from fragments. But this is not fiction, and the narrator is not detached: his voice carries shock, anger, and a relentless determination to make the truth visible. The book's power comes from this combination of investigative rigor and moral passion — Morimura is not merely documenting; he is accusing, mourning, and demanding accountability.

The first part of the book focuses on the perpetrators' testimonies — former unit members who, decades after the events, were willing to speak about what they had seen and done. These testimonies are the book's evidentiary core: descriptions of laboratories where prisoners were infected with plague and cholera; of frostbite experiments conducted in the Manchurian winter; of vivisections performed without anesthesia because anesthesia would have affected the experimental results; of the crematoriums that disposed of the bodies; of the "comfort system" that provided women for the unit's personnel. Morimura presents these testimonies with minimal editorial intervention, allowing the facts to speak with their own terrible eloquence. But his arrangement of the material is itself an argument: by placing the banal details of daily life in the unit alongside the atrocities, he shows how ordinary people — doctors, nurses, technicians, soldiers — could participate in extraordinary evil without feeling themselves to be evil.

The Structure of Evil

The book's middle sections examine the institutional and ideological structures that made Unit 731 possible. Morimura traces the career of Ishii Shiro, the unit's founder and commander — a brilliant, charismatic, and utterly amoral medical officer who convinced the army to fund his vision of biological warfare. He examines the recruitment of doctors and scientists from Japan's top medical schools, men who saw the unit as an opportunity for career advancement and research funding. He analyzes the ideology of the "superior race" (minzoku / 民族) that allowed Japanese personnel to view Chinese and Russian prisoners as subhuman — not worthy of the protections extended to fellow Japanese, let alone the ethical constraints of medical practice.

But Morimura's analysis goes beyond the easy explanation of racism. He shows how the structure of military bureaucracy — the chain of command, the culture of obedience, the compartmentalization of knowledge — allowed individuals to participate in atrocities while maintaining a sense of personal innocence. The doctor who designed the experiment did not perform the vivisection; the nurse who prepared the patient did not choose the pathogen; the soldier who guarded the facility did not know what happened inside the laboratories. Each person had only a partial view, and each could tell themselves that their own role was minor, technical, necessary. The book's title — The Devil's Gluttony — refers not to any single individual's appetite but to the collective appetite of an institution that consumed human beings as a system consumes fuel.

The Aftermath and the Cover-Up

The later sections of the book trace what happened after the war: the destruction of evidence, the American immunity deal, the reintegration of Unit 731 personnel into Japanese society. Morimura documents the specific fates of key figures: Ishii Shiro, who died in 1959 after a postwar career as a businessman and continued contact with American intelligence; other doctors who became professors at medical schools, heads of pharmaceutical companies, respected members of their communities. The book's anger is most visible in these passages — not just at what was done during the war, but at what was not done after it. The absence of trials, the absence of apology, the absence of any systematic reckoning with the medical profession's complicity in war crimes.

The sequels expand the investigation. Zoku Akuma no Hōshoku (続・悪魔の飽食) focuses on the postwar fate of the unit's research and the American connection, using declassified documents including the Thompson Report. Akuma no Hōshoku Dai-san-bu (悪魔の飽食 第三部) shifts perspective to the victims — based on Morimura's 1982 trip to China, where he interviewed survivors and visited the ruins of the Pingfang facility. This third volume is the most emotionally devastating of the series, because it restores humanity to those who had been reduced to maruta — names, faces, family memories, and the ongoing pain of a wound that history refused to close.

The Photo Controversy

The book's history includes a significant controversy that must be acknowledged. In the sequel, Zoku Akuma no Hōshoku, some photographs included in the book's gravure section were later found to be unrelated to Unit 731 — images from other sources that had been mistakenly or fraudulently provided to Morimura by a source. Right-wing critics seized on this error to attack the entire work as fraudulent, claiming that if the photos were fake, the content must be fake as well — a logical fallacy that ignored the thousands of pages of testimony, documentary evidence, and archival research that supported the book's claims. Morimura acknowledged the error, corrected it in subsequent editions, and continued to stand by the book's central thesis. The controversy had a chilling effect on public discussion of Unit 731 in Japan for years, and it remains a cautionary tale about the vulnerability of investigative journalism to discreditation campaigns — and about how a single error can be weaponized to dismiss an entire body of truth.

Kantōgun dai-nana-san-ichi butai no sensō-zai wo kokuhatsu shita kono sakuhin wa, moto-taiin kara teikyō sareta shashin no naka ni inchiki shashin ga konnyū sarete ita. Kore wo miwakeru koto ga dekizu, gurabia shashin ni goyō shita koto kara, masukomi ni fukuro-daki ni sare, ukyoku kara mo kōgeki wo uketa. Kōgi-denwa wa nari-tsuzuke, yonaka, mado ni tōseki sareta. Shigoto-ba no doa ni aka-penki wo buchimakerare, renjitsu, kōgi no tegami ya kyōhaku-jō ga haitatsu sareta. Uyoku no ga-isensha ga oshikakete kita. Watashi wa shusshutsu-ji, bōdan-chokki wo kita. Kanagawa-kenkei ga tsune ni goei-shite kurete, kengai ni wa narubeku de-nai yō ni to keikoku sareta."
"Of approximately 300 works, none has stirred up so much controversy. This work, which accused the Kwantung Army's Unit 731 of war crimes, had fake photographs mixed in among images provided by former unit members. Unable to distinguish them, I mistakenly used them in the gravure section, and as a result was beaten like a drum by the mass media and attacked by the right wing as well. Protest phone calls rang continuously; in the middle of the night, stones were thrown at my windows. Red paint was splashed on my office door, and day after day, protest letters and threatening notes were delivered. Right-wing sound trucks descended on my home. When I went out, I wore a bulletproof vest. The Kanagawa Prefectural Police constantly escorted me and warned me to avoid leaving the prefecture if possible."

Nuance Note

This passage, from Morimura's 2002 author's commentary on the book, is one of the most important statements about the cost of truth-telling in modern Japan. The grammar is deceptively simple — declarative sentences in the passive and active voices, recounting facts — but the cumulative effect is devastating. Let's examine the key phrases. Zen-sakuhin yaku sanbyaku-ten-chū, kore hodo mōgi wo kamoshita sakuhin wa nai (全作品約作品約３００点中、これほど物議を醸した作品はない): The construction kore hodo... wa nai (これほど…はない, "never before so much...") is an absolute superlative — of 300 works, this one stands alone in the intensity of controversy it generated. Mōgi wo kamoshita (物議を醸した, "stirred up controversy / fermented dispute") uses kamosu (醸す, "to brew / to ferment"), a verb associated with the slow transformation of ingredients into something potent — suggesting that the controversy was not immediate but developed over time, like alcohol or vinegar. Kokuhatsu shita (告発した, "accused / denounced / exposed") is the key verb of the book's purpose: not merely "wrote about" or "described" but kokuhatsu — a public accusation, a legal and moral charge. Fukuro-daki ni sare (袋叩きにされ, "was beaten like a drum / was ganged up on") is a vivid idiom: fukuro-daki (袋叩き) literally means "beating a bag" and refers to a group of people surrounding and attacking someone from all sides — the media, the right wing, the political establishment, all converging on a single target. The passive ni sare (にされ, "was made to be / was subjected to") emphasizes Morimura's victimhood in this assault. The list of harassments that follows uses the passive voice consistently — stones "were thrown" (tōseki sareta / 投石された), paint "was splashed" (buchimakerare / ぶちまけられ), letters "were delivered" (haitatsu sareta / 配達された) — creating a grammar of relentless persecution in which Morimura is the object of actions initiated by unseen agents. The shift to active voice in the final sentences — watashi wa... bōdan-chokki wo kita (私は…防弾チョッキを着た, "I wore a bulletproof vest") — marks a change: Morimura refuses to remain merely the object of persecution. He takes action, even if that action is only defensive. The bulletproof vest becomes a symbol of the writer's body as a target — the physical reality of moral courage. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese cultural coding of these harassments: the ga-isensha (街宣車, right-wing sound trucks) as a recognized feature of Japanese political life; the aka-penki (赤ペンキ, red paint) as a traditional marker of accusation and shame; the involvement of the prefectural police as both protectors and implicitly as validators of the danger (if the police assign protection, the threat is real). The English reader sees a list of threats; the Japanese reader sees a familiar pattern of political intimidation applied to a writer who broke a national taboo.

02
The "Logs"
実験体はマルタと呼ばれ、モノとして扱われる。
"Jikkentai wa maruta to yobare, mono to shite atsukawareru."
"The experimental subjects were called 'logs' and treated as things."

Nuance Note

This sentence, which appears in reader summaries and discussions of the book, encapsulates the moral horror of Unit 731 in eleven Japanese syllables. The grammar is devastatingly simple: jikkentai wa (実験体は, "as for the experimental subjects") + maruta to yobare (マルタと呼ばれ, "were called 'logs'") + mono to shite atsukawareru (モノとして扱われる, "are treated as things / objects"). The passive voice runs through both clauses — the subjects "were called" and "are treated" — emphasizing that this was not their choice but something done to them. The word jikkentai (実験体, "experimental subject / test body") is itself a dehumanizing term — tai (体) means "body," not "person"; the compound reduces a human being to a material substrate for experimentation. But the true horror is maruta (丸太, "log / timber / lumber") — a word from the timber industry applied to living people. In the unit's internal euphemism, prisoners were "logs" because they were delivered to the facility like lumber to a mill, processed like raw material, and burned in the crematorium like waste wood. The word maruta also carried a practical function: it allowed unit members to discuss the disposal of human beings in logistical terms — "how many logs today?" — without confronting the reality of what they were doing. The final clause — mono to shite atsukawareru (モノとして扱われる, "treated as things") — uses the grammar of instrumentalization: mono to shite (モノとして, "as things") + passive verb atsukawareru (扱われる, "are handled / are treated"). The word mono (モノ / 物) is the most generic word for "thing" in Japanese — an object without consciousness, without rights, without subjectivity. To be treated mono to shite is to be removed from the category of the human entirely. What is lost in English is the specifically industrial resonance of maruta — the word belongs to logging, to construction, to the processing of natural resources into usable products. In English, "logs" sounds almost rustic; in Japanese, maruta is a commercial term, a unit of industrial inventory. The prisoners were not merely renamed; they were reclassified — from human beings to raw material for a production process whose output was scientific data.

03
The Writer's Duty
作家は時代をリポートしなきゃいけない。
"Sakka wa jidai wo ripōto shinakya ikenai."
"A writer has to report on their era."

Nuance Note

This sentence, Morimura's most famous statement of authorial purpose, is grammatically casual and morally absolute. The construction ...shinakya ikenai (…しなきゃいけない) is a colloquial contraction of shinakereba ikenai (…しなければいけない), the standard pattern for expressing moral or practical necessity — "must / have to / cannot avoid ~ing." The colloquial form shinakya (しなきゃ) gives the sentence the quality of a personal credo spoken in conversation rather than a formal manifesto — this is not a grand statement of literary theory but a working writer's rule of thumb. The verb ripōto suru (リポートする, "to report") is a loanword from English journalism, and its use here is significant: Morimura does not say "a writer must write about their era" or "must criticize their era" but must report on it — using the vocabulary of journalism rather than literature. This choice reflects his social-school mystery background: the writer as investigator, as witness, as the one who records what others prefer to forget. The word jidai (時代, "era / age / times") is broad and encompassing — not "society" (shakai / 社会) or "the nation" (kuni / 国) but jidai, the whole temporal span in which the writer lives, with all its contradictions, horrors, and possibilities. The particle wo (を) marks jidai as the direct object of ripōto — the era is something the writer acts upon, something they have a responsibility to capture and transmit. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese cultural weight of this statement. In the context of postwar Japanese literature, where many writers had either supported or silently acquiesced to militarism, Morimura's declaration is a repudiation of aesthetic detachment. It is also a claim to the legacy of tenkō (転向, ideological conversion) literature — the tradition of writers who, having once supported the war, turned to documenting its crimes. But Morimura was not a converted militarist; he was a child during the war, too young to be complicit. His statement thus carries a generational meaning: those who were not responsible for the crimes are responsible for the memory. The English "A writer has to report on their era" captures the meaning but loses the moral urgency embedded in shinakya ikenai — the sense that this is not optional, not a career choice but a categorical imperative.

04
The Victim's Turn
第一、二部が加害者の証言の上に成されたのに対し、本書は現地取材に基づく被害者側からの告発の書である。
"Dai-ikku, ni-bu ga kagaisha no shōgen no ue ni nasareta no ni taishi, honsho wa genchi-shuzai ni motozuku higaisha-gawa kara no kokuhatsu no sho de aru."
"While the first and second parts were built upon the testimonies of the perpetrators, this book is a work of accusation from the victims' side, based on on-site research."

Nuance Note

This sentence, from the introduction to Akuma no Hōshoku Dai-san-bu (Part Three, 1983), marks a crucial shift in Morimura's method and in the book's ethical architecture. The grammar creates a deliberate contrast: Dai-ikku, ni-bu ga kagaisha no shōgen no ue ni nasareta no ni taishi (第一、二部が加害者の証言の上に成されたのに対し, "while the first and second parts were built upon the testimonies of the perpetrators"). The phrase no ni taishi (のに対し, "in contrast to / while") establishes a formal opposition between the first two volumes and the third. Kagaisha (加害者, "perpetrator / assailant / offender") is the legal and moral term for those who commit harm — distinct from hannin (犯人, "criminal / culprit"), which implies legal conviction. By using kagaisha, Morimura acknowledges that these were the ones who caused harm, regardless of whether they were ever prosecuted. The phrase shōgen no ue ni nasareta (証言の上に成された, "were built upon testimony") uses the honorific passive nasareta (成された), the respectful form of nasu (成す, "to build / to form / to accomplish") — a strangely elevated verb for describing the construction of a book from war criminals' testimonies, as if Morimura is acknowledging the moral ambiguity of his own method in the first two volumes: he built his case on the words of those who had participated in the crimes. The second half of the sentence shifts perspective entirely: honsho wa genchi-shuzai ni motozuku higaisha-gawa kara no kokuhatsu no sho de aru (本書は現地取材に基づく被害者側からの告発の書である, "this book is a work of accusation from the victims' side, based on on-site research"). Genchi-shuzai (現地取材, "on-site research / local coverage") is the vocabulary of investigative journalism — Morimura went to China, walked the ruins of Pingfang, interviewed survivors. Higaisha-gawa (被害者側, "the victims' side") uses the suffix -gawa (側, "side / party / faction") to indicate perspective — not merely "victims" but "the victims' side," a positionality rather than an identity. Kokuhatsu no sho (告発の書, "a work of accusation / a book of denunciation") uses sho (書, "book / writing"), a formal, almost legal term that elevates the book to the status of a formal indictment. The copula de aru (である), the written-language form of "is," gives the sentence the declarative authority of a legal document. What is lost in English is the moral trajectory encoded in the grammar: the book moves from kagaisha (perpetrator) to higaisha (victim), from shōgen (testimony offered by the accused) to kokuhatsu (accusation made by the injured), from archival research to genchi-shuzai (being present at the site of the crime). The sentence is not merely descriptive; it is a confession of the limits of the first two volumes and a promise that the third will correct the imbalance. In a work about crimes that reduced humans to maruta, the shift to the victims' perspective is not merely methodological; it is a restoration of the humanity that the unit tried to destroy.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Morimura's prose in Akuma no Hōshoku is the prose of documentary restraint — but much of what makes it powerful is embedded in Japanese vocabulary of war memory, moral judgment, and the specific grammar of accusation and victimhood.

1. Kokuhatsu (告発) vs. "Exposure" or "Revelation"

JP 戦争犯罪を告発した
EN "accused of war crimes" / "exposed war crimes" / "denounced war crimes"
Loss The word kokuhatsu (告発) is one of the most legally and morally charged words in Japanese, and no English equivalent captures its full weight. Kokuhatsu combines tsugu (告, "to tell / to inform / to announce") and hatsu (発, "to issue forth / to emit / to send out"), and it means "to accuse / to denounce / to report to authorities / to indict." But it is not merely "exposing" a fact or "revealing" a secret — kokuhatsu is an active accusation that demands a response from society, from the legal system, from history itself. In Japanese law, kokuhatsu refers to the act of reporting a crime to prosecutors; in journalism, it describes investigative reporting that names perpetrators; in literature, it is the genre of moral witness. When Morimura says his book kokuhatsu shita (告発した) Unit 731's war crimes, he is not saying he "wrote about" them or "revealed" them — he is saying he accused the unit, in the court of public opinion, of crimes against humanity. The English "exposed" suggests the removal of a cover; kokuhatsu suggests the filing of a charge. "Denounced" captures the moral intensity but loses the legal precision. "Accused" captures the legal dimension but loses the public, performative quality — kokuhatsu is something done before others, a speech act that calls for witnesses. What is lost in English is the sense that Morimura understood his book as a formal indictment, not merely a historical account. The title kokuhatsu no sho (告発の書, "a work of accusation") frames the book as a legal document as much as a literary one — a charge sheet against an institution that had escaped prosecution.

2. Maruta (丸太) as Dehumanization

JP 実験体はマルタと呼ばれた
EN "The experimental subjects were called 'logs'"
Loss The translation of maruta (丸太) as "logs" is accurate but inadequate. In English, "log" is a neutral word for a piece of wood — it can be rustic, cozy, even poetic (a log fire, a log cabin). In Japanese, maruta is a commercial and industrial term: it refers to timber in its raw, unprocessed form — the product of logging operations, measured in units, transported, sold, and milled. A maruta is not a piece of firewood; it is inventory. It is a commodity before it becomes a product. By calling prisoners maruta, Unit 731 performed a double erasure: it removed the prisoners from the category of the human (ningen / 人間), and it placed them in the category of raw material for industrial processing. The English "logs" captures the wooden metaphor but loses the commercial coding. A more accurate translation might be "lumber" or "raw timber" — but these sound awkward when applied to people. And that awkwardness is precisely the point: the Japanese word maruta was also awkward when applied to people, and the unit used it precisely to create distance, to make the unthinkable thinkable by making it part of a logistics vocabulary. What is also lost is the visual specificity of maruta: maru (丸) means "round / circular," and ta (太) means "thick / fat" — a maruta is specifically a thick, round piece of timber, the trunk of a tree before it has been split or shaped. The word thus carries a visual image of cylindrical rawness, of unformed mass — an image that, when applied to human bodies, carries a horrifying suggestion of the body reduced to mere mass, to meat, to material without form or soul. The English "logs" is too slender, too shaped, too domesticated to carry this resonance.

3. Rekishi no Yami (歴史の闇) as Historical Darkness

JP 歴史の闇のヴェールに包まれていた
EN "wrapped in the veil of the darkness of history" / "shrouded in the veil of historical darkness"
Loss The phrase rekishi no yami (歴史の闇, "the darkness of history") is a common expression in Japanese discourse about war memory, but it carries a specifically Japanese philosophical resonance. Yami (闇) means "darkness" — physical darkness, moral darkness, and the Buddhist avidyā (無明, ignorance / spiritual darkness). In Japanese, yami is not merely the absence of light; it is a positive force, a presence that obscures and conceals. When history is described as having yami, the suggestion is not merely that some facts are unknown but that there are forces — institutional, political, psychological — that actively maintain the darkness. The "veil" (vēru / ヴェール) that wraps Unit 731's history is thus not a passive covering but an active concealment, maintained by the American immunity deal, the Japanese government's silence, the medical establishment's self-protection, and the collective shame of a society that preferred not to know. The English "darkness of history" captures the metaphor but loses the Buddhist undertone of yami as spiritual ignorance — the darkness that comes from refusing to see what is before you. In Japanese war memory discourse, rekishi no yami is also a call to action: the darkness can be dispelled, the veil can be lifted, but only by those willing to look. Morimura's entire book is an exercise in yami-dispelling — the writer as a light source directed at the dark corners of national memory. What is lost in English is the moral agency embedded in the metaphor: in Japanese, history does not merely "have" darkness; the darkness is something done to history by those who benefit from concealment.

3. Kagaisha (加害者) and Higaisha (被害者) as Moral Categories

JP 加害者の証言 / 被害者側からの告発
EN "perpetrators' testimony" / "accusation from the victims' side"
Loss The paired terms kagaisha (加害者, "perpetrator / offender / assailant") and higaisha (被害者, "victim / injured party") form a moral and legal binary that structures Japanese discourse on war crimes and historical responsibility. The grammar of these words is revealing: both end in -sha (者, "person / one who"), making them identities rather than merely descriptions of action. A kagaisha is not merely someone who committed harm; they are a "harm-causing person" — the harm becomes part of their categorical identity. Similarly, a higaisha is a "harm-receiving person" — the victimhood is not an event but a status. This grammatical structure has important implications: in Japanese, one can "become" a higaisha (被害者になる) or "be" a kagaisha (加害者である) — these are states of being, not merely historical facts. The English "perpetrator" and "victim" are also identities, but they lack the transparent etymology of the Japanese terms. Kagaisha breaks down into ka (加, "to add / to inflict") + gai (害, "harm / damage / injury") + sha (者, "person") — literally "a person who inflicts harm." Higaisha breaks down into hi (被, "to receive / to suffer") + gai (害, "harm") + sha (者) literally "a person who receives harm." The symmetry is perfect and morally transparent: the same gai (harm) flows from the ka-person to the hi-person. What is lost in English is this etymological clarity — the way the Japanese words make visible the transfer of harm from one category of person to another. The English reader sees "perpetrator" and "victim" as legal terms; the Japanese reader sees a moral equation written into the structure of the language itself. When Morimura shifts his book's perspective from kagaisha to higaisha, he is not merely changing sources; he is crossing a moral boundary, moving from the side of those who caused harm to the side of those who received it — and in doing so, he restores the balance that the unit's euphemisms (maruta) had destroyed.

4. Shinakya Ikenai (しなきゃいけない) as Moral Necessity

JP 作家は時代をリポートしなきゃいけない
EN "A writer has to report on their era"
Loss The grammatical pattern ...nakya ikenai (…なきゃいけない) is a colloquial contraction of ...nakereba ikenai (…なければいけない), one of the most important patterns for expressing necessity in Japanese. The full form contains a conditional (nakereba, "if [one] does not") and a negative evaluation (ikenai, "it is not good / it will not do"), creating a double-negative logic: "if I do not do X, it will not be good" = "I must do X." The colloquial shinakya (しなきゃ) compresses this logic into a single fluid syllable cluster that feels almost like a single word — the grammatical complexity hidden inside casual speech. This pattern is distinct from other necessity expressions in Japanese: ...nakereba naranai (…なければならない) is more formal and objective; ...nai to ikenai (…ないといけない) is slightly softer; ...ni chigainai (…に違いない) expresses certainty rather than obligation. ...nakya ikenai carries a personal, almost intimate quality — it is what you say when you are explaining why you are doing something difficult, not because you want to but because you cannot not do it. When Morimura uses this pattern to describe a writer's duty, he is not issuing a grand proclamation; he is stating a personal rule, a working principle that has guided him through death threats and harassment. The English "has to" or "must" captures the obligation but loses the colloquial warmth and the double-negative logic that makes the Japanese expression feel like a discovery rather than a decree. What is also lost is the specifically Japanese cultural context of writerly responsibility — the legacy of writers like Ishikawa Tatsuzō and Ōoka Shōhei who believed that literature must engage with the moral catastrophes of the age. Morimura's shinakya ikenai places him in this lineage not as a theoretician but as a practitioner — someone who discovered through doing that a writer must report on their era, because the alternative is a kind of moral silence that is itself a form of complicity.

〜と呼ばれ、〜として扱われる（受身・分類） N4

...to yobare, ...to shite atsukawareru: passive classification — "is called ~ and is treated as ~"

実験体はマルタと呼ばれ、モノとして扱われる

"The experimental subjects were called 'logs' and treated as things" — The double passive creates a grammar of systematic dehumanization.

〜ずに（打消し・方法） N3

...zu ni: "without ~ing" — negative gerund indicating absence of action.

見分けることができず、グラビア写真に誤用した

"unable to distinguish them, I mistakenly used them in the gravure" — The zu ni form creates a causal link between failure and consequence.

〜ようにと警告された（引用・受身） N4

...yō ni to keikoku sareta: "was warned to ~" — quotative passive with imperative force.

県外にはなるべく出ないようにと警告れた

"was warned to avoid leaving the prefecture if possible" — The yō ni to construction embeds an imperative within a passive, showing authority's reach.

〜の書である（断定・文語） N3

...no sho de aru: formal written copula — "is a work of ~" — declarative authority.

被害者側からの告発の書である

"is a work of accusation from the victims' side" — The de aru copula gives the sentence the formal authority of a legal or historical document.

Study tip: Morimura's grammar in Akuma no Hōshoku is the grammar of documentary witness — declarative, restrained, and morally charged. Pay attention to how the passive voice creates a grammar of persecution and victimhood, how ni taishi creates moral pivots between perpetrator and victim perspectives, how shinakya ikenai turns personal conviction into categorical imperative, and how de aru transforms testimony into historical record. The grammar is as sober as the subject demands, and as uncompromising.
