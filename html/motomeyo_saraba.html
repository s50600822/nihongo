The Biblical Echo: Matthew 7:7

The title is a direct allusion to the Japanese translation of Matthew 7:7: 求めよ、さらば与えられん ("Ask, and it shall be given you; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened unto you"). In the full biblical phrase, さらば functions as a conjunction meaning "then" or "in that case," introducing the consequence: "Ask, and then [you shall be] given." By truncating the verse after さらば, Okuda creates a title that is simultaneously a promise and a farewell. さらば in modern Japanese also carries the meaning of "farewell" (as in さらばなら or さらばだ). The title thus oscillates between two readings: "Ask, and it shall be given" (the biblical promise of fulfillment) and "Ask, and farewell" (the act of seeking leads to parting).

This double meaning is the novel's emotional architecture. Shiori and Seita have spent seven years asking: asking for a child, asking for tests to work, asking their bodies to cooperate, asking fate to be kind. The novel asks whether the act of seeking itself transforms the seeker, and whether the thing sought, if finally given, can be received without damage. The title's truncation transforms a promise into a question. The "it shall be given" is omitted; we are left only with the asking and the さらば that follows, which may be fulfillment or may be loss.

求めよ (motomeyo): The Imperative of Desire

The imperative form 求めよ is literary, almost biblical in register. Modern Japanese would use 求めなさい (motome nasai) or 求めて (motomete) for an imperative. ～よ as an imperative ending belongs to classical grammar and formal written style, carrying a weight of command, invocation, or prayer. By using this form, the title elevates the act of "asking" from a casual request to a fundamental existential demand. Shiori's desire for a child is not a preference; it is a 求めよ, a deep, structuring need that organizes her entire life. The title names this need in its most absolute form.

さらば (saraba): The Conjunction That Became a Farewell

さらば is an interesting word in Japanese. Etymologically it derives from さらば (更に + 場), meaning "if [it be] furthermore" or "if that be the case," functioning as a conditional conjunction. In classical and biblical Japanese, it introduces a consequence: "If you ask, then..." Over time, through its use in parting scenes ("If that is the case, then I shall go"), it acquired the secondary meaning of "farewell." Modern readers encountering さらば immediately feel both meanings: the logical "then" and the emotional "goodbye."

In the context of the novel, both meanings are active. The "then" points to the promise: if you ask, then something will happen. The "farewell" points to Seita's disappearance, to the marriage that may not survive the asking, to the self that is left behind when desire is pursued to its end. The title contains the entire plot in three words: they ask, and something ends.

The title's emotional logic: 求めよ、さらば is a promise with the second half cut off. It invites the reader to supply the missing words: "Ask, and it shall be given" (hope) or "Ask, and farewell" (despair). The novel refuses to choose between these readings until its final pages. The title is not a statement; it is a suspended sentence.

02 About the Author: 奥田亜希子

Okuda Akiko is one of the most distinctive voices in contemporary Japanese fiction, a writer who approaches the themes of love, family, and obsession with the analytical precision of a philosopher and the emotional directness of a confessional poet. Her background in Eastern philosophy, her early career in sales for a free-paper company, and her self-described love of "one-sided love" (片想い) all inform a body of work that is intellectually rigorous and viscerally affecting. She is a writer of the "slightly twisted" (ねじれた): premises that seem familiar until she turns them a few degrees, revealing an unexpected facet.

From Philosophy Graduate to Literary Star

Okuda's philosophical training is not incidental to her fiction. A degree in Eastern philosophy means she has spent years reading Buddhist, Confucian, and Daoist texts, thinking about the nature of desire, attachment, suffering, and selfhood. These questions permeate her novels not as explicit themes but as structural assumptions. When she writes about a woman who uses a love potion to win a man's heart, she is not writing a fantasy; she is writing a philosophical experiment about authenticity, identity, and the ethics of desire. When she writes about a couple undergoing infertility treatment, she is exploring what it means to want something so deeply that the wanting reshapes the self.

After university, she worked in sales for a company that published free papers (フリーペーパー), those advertising magazines distributed at train stations and shops. This is not the typical path to literary success. The job gave her an intimate understanding of ordinary Japanese working life, of the small businesses and local communities that advertise in free papers, of the gap between corporate language and lived experience. When her characters work, they work in recognizably real jobs: translation, programming, izakaya service. There is no romantic bohemianism in Okuda's employment landscape.

The Subaru Prize and Debut

In 2013, Okuda won the 37th Subaru Literary Prize with "Hidarime ni Utsuru Hoshi" (左目に映る星, "The Star Reflected in the Left Eye"), which she had submitted under the title "Another Planet." The novel, published by Shueisha in 2014, introduced her signature concerns: a young woman navigating contemporary Tokyo's social and romantic landscape with a mixture of detachment and longing, capable of casual sex but searching for something she cannot name. The protagonist, Sakiko, goes to group dates (gōkon) and sometimes sleeps with men she has just met, not out of promiscuity but out of a kind of existential experimentation: what happens if I do this? What does it mean that I feel nothing, or too much?

This debut established Okuda as a writer of the "slightly damaged" (こじらせ) generation: young adults who have absorbed all the correct attitudes about gender equality and sexual freedom but find themselves emotionally unmoored, capable of analysis but struggling with feeling. Her characters often understand their own problems better than they can solve them.

A Career of Consistent Experimentation

Since her debut, Okuda has published a remarkable string of novels, each exploring a different facet of love and identity: Tōmei Ningen wa 204 Gōshitsu no Yume o Miru (透明人間は204号室の夢を見る, 2015), about a failed writer; Family-less (ファミリー・レス, 2016), about family dysfunction; Itsutsu-boshi o Tsukete yo (五つ星をつけてよ, 2018), about online reviews and romantic deception; Seishun no Jōkā (青春のジョーカー, 2019), about adolescent sexuality and school hierarchy; Mahō ga Toketa ato mo (魔法がとけたあとも, 2020, bunko title: Kanata no Idol), about fandom and identity; and Crazy for Rabbit (クレイジー・フォー・ラビット, 2022), among others.

Each novel is formally and thematically distinct, yet all share Okuda's core concerns: how do we know our feelings are real? How does social performance shape authentic emotion? What are the ethics of wanting? Motomeyo, Saraba represents a maturation of these concerns into a fully adult register. The characters are no longer in their twenties, experimenting with identity; they are in their thirties, committed to a marriage, facing the consequences of choices made years ago.

03 The Book

Motomeyo, Saraba is a novel about the anatomy of a marriage under pressure. It asks a question that most romance novels avoid: what happens after the happy ending? Shiori and Seita have the marriage that their friends envy: compatible, warm, sexually alive, mutually respectful. He is the "ideal husband" (理想の旦那) in their social circle. They have been together for twelve years, married for seven. The only absence is a child, and that absence, pursued through three years of infertility treatment, becomes the crack through which the marriage's foundation is revealed to be not quite what it seemed.

The Structure: Three Movements

The novel is divided into three chapters, each shifting narrative perspective and temporal position, creating a prismatic view of the relationship. Chapter 1 is Shiori's present-tense experience of the marriage's unraveling. Chapter 2 moves back in time to trace how they met, fell in love, and decided to marry. Chapter 3 returns to the present for the confrontation and resolution. This structure is essential to the novel's effect: we must understand what they were to each other before we can judge what they have become, and we must see the origin of their love to understand the origin of their damage.

Chapter 1: Shiori's perspective. The couple has been undergoing infertility treatment for three years. The cycle of hope and despair has become routine. Seita is supportive, gentle, seemingly perfect. Then Shiori discovers a shocking post on Seita's social media: "I'm using my wife for my own life." Shortly after, Seita vanishes, leaving behind a letter of confession. The chapter tracks Shiori's emotional freefall as the stable world of her marriage dissolves.

Chapter 2: Seita's perspective, moving back to their meeting. They were university students working part-time at the same izakaya. Shiori was vivid, funny, slightly unreachable. Seita was quiet, observant, deeply attracted. Okuda traces their courtship with the precision of someone who understands that love is not a feeling but a series of decisions, small concessions, and mutual accommodations. We learn what Seita did, why he did it, and how the secret has lived inside him for twelve years.

Chapter 3: The convergence. The perspectives intertwine as the couple faces each other again. The novel refuses easy redemption or easy condemnation. It asks: if you learned that your partner's love began with an act you cannot approve of, would the years of genuine care that followed matter? Can love be both real and founded on a lie? The ending is not a simple yes or no; it is a negotiation, a choice made in full knowledge of damage.

The Characters

辻原志織 (Tsujihara Shiori) — 34 years old, a freelance translator specializing in IT-related texts. She is intelligent, self-aware, and increasingly desperate in a way that shames her. The infertility treatment has made her body feel like an enemy; every month brings a new cycle of hope (maybe this time) and grief (not this time). She loves Seita, trusts him completely, and has never been hurt by him in twelve years. This absence of injury is itself a kind of vulnerability: she has no practice in forgiving him.

辻原誠太 (Tsujihara Seita) — Shiori's husband, a programmer at a software development company. He is, by all external measures, a good man: steady, kind, attentive. His hobby is photography. He has supported Shiori through every stage of the infertility treatment, attended appointments, absorbed her despair, never complained. And yet he carries a secret: something he did at the beginning of their relationship, an act born of desire and insecurity, which he has never confessed. The novel's moral complexity comes from the fact that his subsequent twelve years of love and care are genuine, even as their origin is compromised.

竹内杏奈 (Takeuchi Anna) and 本島衿子 (Motojima Eriko) — Shiori's friends from university, former izakaya co-workers. They function as a chorus, reflecting the social world's view of the marriage, and as foils, representing alternative paths (married with children, single and searching). Their presence reminds Shiori that her marriage, which feels uniquely damaged to her, is also watched and judged by others.

辻原隼太 (Tsujihara Hayata) — Seita's younger brother, a university student. He provides a glimpse of Seita's family context and serves as a bridge between the couple's private crisis and the social world.

The "Love Potion" Origin

In interviews, Okuda has revealed that the novel's origin was a casual conversation: "If there were a love potion, would you use it?" The question fascinated her not as a fantasy premise but as a moral one. What would it mean to use one? What would it mean to discover you had been the object of one? Motomeyo, Saraba is her exploration of this question in realistic terms: not a literal magic potion but the small manipulations, the strategic disclosures and withholdings, the calculated self-presentations by which people influence each other's feelings. Seita's secret act is a kind of love potion in retrospect: something done to ensure love, which then succeeds too well, creating a marriage too good to be allowed to stand on a compromised foundation.

Critical Reception

The novel won the 2nd "Bookstore-Selected Adult Romance Novel Prize" (第2回本屋が選ぶ大人の恋愛小説大賞), an award voted on by bookstore staff who know exactly what makes readers buy and finish a book. Their praise was immediate and intense: "I was seized by how much I understood the feelings" (気持ちがわかりぎて、わしづかみにされた). The award recognized what reviewers had noted: this is a romance novel for adults not because it contains explicit content but because it refuses childish moral binaries. Seita is neither villain nor victim; Shiori is neither saint nor fool. They are two people who love each other and have hurt each other, navigating a crisis with the tools that twelve years of marriage have given them, which turn out to be both more and less than they need.

Reviewers consistently highlighted the novel's unflinching portrayal of infertility treatment: the medical invasiveness, the monthly cycle of hope and despair, the way it affects sex, self-image, and the relationship to one's own body. Okuda researched the subject thoroughly, and it shows not in technical detail but in emotional accuracy. Shiori's envy when friends announce pregnancies, her guilt at feeling envy, her gratitude to Seita for his patience, her fear that his patience is a kind of performance: these are the fine textures of a lived experience, not generic suffering.

05 What Translation Loses

A. 求めよ、さらば (Motomeyo, Saraba)

The Truncated Promise

The title's full biblical source is 求めよ、らば与えられん ("Ask, and it shall be given you"). By cutting off 与えられん (it shall be given), Okuda creates a title that is grammatically incomplete and semantically double. さらば in classical/biblical Japanese means "then" or "in that case"; in modern Japanese, it primarily means "farewell." A Japanese reader feels both meanings simultaneously: the promise (ask, and then...) and the farewell (ask, and goodbye). An English reader seeing "Ask, and It Shall Be Given" gets only the promise. "Ask, and Farewell" gets only the despair. No English title can capture the superposition of hope and loss that the Japanese title achieves through its biblical echo and its modern semantic drift. The title is a Schrödinger's cat of emotional states: until you finish the novel, it is both promise and threat.

B. 理想の旦那 (risō no danna)

The Social Category of the Perfect Husband

理想の旦那 is not simply "ideal husband." 旦那 (danna) is a specific, slightly old-fashioned word for "husband" that carries connotations of the man as provider and head of household. It is the word a wife might use when speaking to others about her husband, not the intimate あなた (anata) or 夫 (otto, the neutral term). "Ideal husband" in English suggests a private judgment; 理想の旦那 suggests a social category, a type that others recognize and comment on. When Shiori's friends call Seita 理想の旦那, they are not saying he is good; they are saying he fits a socially recognized template of husbandly excellence. This makes the revelation of his secret more devastating: he was not privately good but secretly bad; he was publicly ideal and privately compromised. The social confirmation of his goodness becomes, in retrospect, part of the deception.

C. 申し訳ない (mōshiwakenai)

Shame Beyond Apology

申し訳ない is often translated as "sorry," but this understates its emotional weight. Etymologically, it means "there is no excuse I can offer" (申す = to say, 訳 = reason/excuse). It is the word for a shame that has no justification, a failure so complete that explanation is impossible. When Seita writes that he feels 申し訳ない about not being able to support Shiori, he is not offering an apology; he is naming a state of being. "Ashamed" is closer but too psychological; "inexcusable" is closer but too moralistic. Mōshiwakenai is a social emotion: the recognition that one has failed in an obligation so fundamental that no words can repair it. It is the emotion that makes Seita disappear rather than talk.

D. 不妊治療 (funin chirō)

The Specificity of Japanese Infertility Treatment

不妊治療 means infertility treatment, but the Japanese term carries a specific cultural and medical context. In Japan, infertility treatment is not merely a medical procedure; it is a social and emotional journey that involves basal body temperature charting, timed intercourse, hormonal injections, IUI, IVF, and often years of monthly cycles of hope and disappointment. The treatment is partially covered by national health insurance under certain conditions, but the emotional cost is not. The Japanese term 不妊 (funin, infertility) also carries a stigma that "infertility" in English, with its clinical neutrality, does not fully capture. 不 (fu) = not; 妊 (nin) = pregnancy. The word names an absence as a condition. In the novel, this absence becomes a character, a third presence in the marriage that shapes every conversation, every decision, every act of love.

E. 片想い (kataomoi)

One-Sided Love as a Literary Genre

Okuda has said in interviews that she loves writing 片想い (one-sided love / unrequited love). 片想い is not merely "unrequited love" in English; it is a recognized genre of Japanese emotional experience, with its own aesthetics, rituals, and literary traditions. The word 片 (kata) means one-sided, partial, incomplete; 想い (omoi) is feeling, thought, love. Kataomoi is love that is not returned, but it is also love that exists fully on one side, complete in its own way. Seita's love for Shiori began as 片想い: he loved her before she loved him, and the novel's central moral question is whether the act he committed to bridge that gap invalidates the love that grew from it. "Unrequited love" in English sounds pathetic; kataomoi in Japanese can be noble, beautiful, even triumphant in its persistence. The novel asks: what if 片想い never fully ends, even inside a marriage?

F. 協力的 (kyōryoku-teki)

The Japanese Virtue of Cooperative Support

When Shiori describes Seita as 不妊治療にも協力的だ (cooperative about infertility treatment), the word 協力的 carries specific Japanese workplace and marital connotations. 協力 (cooperation, collaboration) is one of the highest virtues in Japanese social life; to be 協力的 is to subordinate one's own preferences to the group's needs. In the context of infertility treatment, this means attending appointments, managing charts, providing emotional support, all without complaint. "Supportive" in English captures the meaning but misses the social valence: 協力的 is the word you would use for a good colleague or team member, and its use here suggests that Seita approaches the marriage with the same ethic he would bring to a professional collaboration. This is both his virtue and, perhaps, his limitation: he is 協力的 even about the most intimate aspects of their shared life, which means he is always performing a role, even when he seems most natural.

G. 惚れ薬 (hore-gusuri)

The Love Potion as Moral Thought Experiment

The novel's origin, as Okuda has revealed, was a conversation about 惚れ薬 (love potion / philter). In Western folklore, the love potion is a magical device; in Japanese popular culture, it appears in anime, manga, and fantasy fiction as a comic or romantic trope. But Okuda treats it as a philosophical question: what would it mean to use one? What would it mean to discover you had been the object of one? The Japanese term 惚れ薬 is bluntly literal: 惚れる (to fall in love) + 薬 (medicine/drug). It does not have the medieval romance associations of the English "love potion." It sounds almost like a pharmaceutical product, something you might find advertised online. This modern, prosaic quality makes the moral question more urgent: in a world of psychopharmaceuticals, dating apps, and behavioral manipulation, what is the difference between a 惚れ薬 and the ordinary techniques by which people influence each other's feelings? The novel's answer is disturbing because it suggests the difference may be one of degree, not kind.

06 Grammar Points

～きれない (kirenai) — "Cannot do completely / Cannot fully"

志織を支えきれなかった = "I couldn't fully support Shiori"

～きれない expresses inability to complete an action to the full extent, often due to physical, emotional, or situational limitation.

Formation: [Verb stem] + きれない

Examples:

  • 信じきれない = "can't quite believe"
  • 言いきれない = "can't say for certain"
  • 食べきれない = "can't eat it all"

The grammar is central to Seita's character: he has tried to be supportive, but the infertility treatment has exceeded his capacity. Kirenai is the grammar of insufficiency, of effort that falls short of its goal. It is more nuanced than simple failure; it implies that the goal was large and the attempt genuine.

～ずつ (zutsu) — "Each / Little by little / Per"

少しずつ形を変えていく = "gradually changing shape little by little"

～ずつ indicates distribution in equal amounts or gradual progression by increments.

Distinction:

  • 少し変わる = "changes a little" (simple change)
  • 少しずつ変わる = "changes little by little" (gradual, incremental)

The grammar captures the novel's temporal structure: the marriage does not collapse suddenly; it 少しずつ changes, month by month, treatment by treatment, until the accumulated small shifts become a transformation. Zutsu is the grammar of erosion.

～をきっかけに (wokikkakeni) — "Triggered by / Taking as an opportunity"

不妊治療をきっかけに少しずつ形を変えていく = "triggered by the infertility treatment, [their days] gradually change shape"

～をきっかけに marks a specific event as the catalyst for a larger change. It is stronger than ～から (because) because it emphasizes the event as a turning point.

Examples:

  • 留学をきっかけに = "taking studying abroad as an opportunity"
  • その出会いをきっかけに = "with that encounter as the catalyst"

The grammar is essential to the novel's architecture: the infertility treatment is not merely a background condition but the きっかけ, the trigger that exposes what was already fragile in the marriage. Without it, Seita might never have confronted his secret.

～ようとする (yō to suru) — "To try to / To attempt to"

私より先に受け止めようとしてくれる = "he tries to absorb [the disappointment] before I do"

～ようとする indicates an attempt or effort toward an action. When combined with ～てくれる (doing something for me), it expresses someone else's considerate effort on one's behalf.

Distinction:

  • 受け止める = "absorbs" (simple statement)
  • 受け止めようとする = "tries to absorb" (attempt, effort)
  • 受け止めようとしてくれる = "tries to absorb [for my sake]" (considerate attempt)

The grammar reveals Seita's character through syntax: he is always ようとしてくれる, always trying to do things for Shiori, always making the effort. The tragedy is that this very effort, this constant 協力的 trying, may be a form of performance that hides his authentic guilt.

～かもしれない (kamoshirenai) — "Might / Perhaps"

君は私を選ばなかったかもしれない = "you might not have chosen me"

～かもしれない expresses possibility with a note of uncertainty and often anxiety. It is softer and more tentative than ～だろう (probably).

Distinction:

  • 選ばなかっただろう = "probably wouldn't have chosen" (confident speculation)
  • 選ばなかったかもしれない = "might not have chosen" (anxious possibility, leaving room for doubt)

The grammar is the engine of Seita's paranoia: not certainty but possibility, the かもしれない that has haunted him for twelve years. He does not know if Shiori would have rejected him; he only knows that it was かもしれない, and that possibility was enough to make him act.

～しかない (shika nai) — "Have no choice but to / Can only"

愛すしかないと思っていたのか = "did you think you had no choice but to love?"

～しかない expresses that there is only one option, often under constraint.

Formation: [Verb in dictionary form] + しか + negative verb

Examples:

  • 待つしかない = "can only wait"
  • 信じるしかない = "have no choice but to believe"

Seita's most devastating question uses this grammar: did Shiori love him freely, or did she 愛すしかない, love him because the situation he created allowed no alternative? Shika nai is the grammar of coercion disguised as choice, and the novel's central moral question is whether Shiori's love was ever truly free.

～だろうか (darō ka) — "I wonder if / Could it be that"

それが、私の見落としだったのだろうか = "could that have been my oversight?"

～だろうか is a tentative question form, softer than ～ですか, expressing self-directed doubt or speculation.

Distinction:

  • 見落としだったのですか = "Was it your oversight?" (direct question)
  • 見落としだったのだろうか = "Could it have been my oversight?" (self-questioning, doubtful)

The grammar captures Shiori's interiority: she is not accusing Seita; she is questioning herself. Darō ka is the sound of a mind turning inward, re-examining twelve years of memory for signs she missed. It is the grammar of retrospective doubt.

07 Cultural & Historical Context

Infertility Treatment in Contemporary Japan

Infertility is a significant and growing social issue in Japan. Approximately one in 5.5 couples seeks infertility treatment, and the number has been rising as the average age of first marriage and first birth increases. The Japanese government provides partial subsidies for infertility treatment under the national health insurance system, particularly for IVF (in vitro fertilization), reflecting the country's concern about its declining birth rate. However, the emotional and physical toll of treatment is not covered by any insurance.

The experience of infertility in Japan carries specific cultural weight. In a society that still places considerable emphasis on lineage and family continuation, childlessness can be experienced as a social as well as personal failure. The monthly cycle of hope and despair, the invasive medical procedures, the way it transforms sex from an act of intimacy into a scheduled procedure, the envy toward friends who conceive easily, the guilt at feeling envy: all of these are vividly present in Okuda's novel. Shiori's experience is not generic suffering; it is specifically Japanese in its combination of medical modernity and traditional family pressure.

What distinguishes Okuda's treatment is her refusal to make infertility the novel's only subject. It is the trigger (きっかけ), not the content. The infertility treatment exposes the fault line in the marriage; it does not create it. This is a more sophisticated narrative choice than making the couple's problems derive solely from external pressure. The treatment is the pressure that reveals the structure, like a load test on a bridge.

The Izakaya as a Japanese Social Institution

Shiori and Seita meet while working part-time at an izakaya (居酒屋), the Japanese pub that serves as a cornerstone of Japanese social life. For university students, izakaya part-time work is a common job: it pays reasonably well, offers flexible hours, and provides a social environment. The izakaya is where Japanese people learn to drink, to converse across hierarchies, to manage drunken customers, and to build the informal relationships that often last into professional life.

In the novel, the izakaya is not merely a setting; it is the social space where Shiori and Seita's relationship is formed. The dynamics of service, of watching customers, of the hierarchies between staff members, all shape how they see each other. Seita falls in love with Shiori while watching her work: her competence, her humor, her ability to handle difficult situations. This is a specifically Japanese romantic origin: love born not from grand gestures but from observing someone at work, seeing how they behave when they don't know they are being watched. The izakaya provides the cover of ordinariness under which intense feeling can develop.

"Bookstore-Selected Adult Romance Novel Prize" (本屋が選ぶ大人の恋愛小説大賞)

The award this novel won, the 本屋が選ぶ大人の恋愛小説大賞, is significant because it is chosen by bookstore staff, the people who stand at the front line of reader recommendation. Unlike juried literary prizes, which are selected by critics and writers, this prize reflects what booksellers believe will move readers, what they feel confident pressing into customers' hands. The fact that Motomeyo, Saraba won this prize indicates that it succeeds not just as literature but as a reading experience: it is a book that booksellers trust to deliver emotional payoff.

The category "adult romance" (大人の恋愛) is also culturally specific. In Japan, "adult romance" does not primarily mean sexually explicit content; it means romance that addresses adult problems: marriage, infertility, career, aging parents, financial pressure, the accumulated weight of shared history. It is romance for people who have lived long enough to know that love is not a solution but a commitment to facing problems together. Okuda's novel fits this category precisely: it is not about falling in love but about staying in love when love has become complicated by time, secrets, and disappointment.

The Ethics of Manipulation in Love

The novel's philosophical core, derived from Okuda's "love potion" thought experiment, connects to a long tradition of ethical inquiry about the authenticity of love. If you create the conditions under which someone falls in love with you, is their love real? This question appears in Plato's Phaedrus, in Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream (which literally uses a love potion), in contemporary debates about consent and manipulation.

Okuda's contribution is to make the question concrete and contemporary. Seita does not use a magic potion; he does something much more ordinary, something that many people do in the early stages of romance: he presents himself strategically, withholds information, creates a situation favorable to his desires. The novel asks: at what point does strategic self-presentation become deception? At what point does deception invalidate the love that grows from it? These are not abstract questions in the novel; they are Shiori's lived crisis. She must decide whether twelve years of genuine happiness can be retroactively poisoned by their origin, and whether a love that began in manipulation can be, twelve years later, "real."

The Japanese Marriage at the Seven-Year Mark

The "seven-year itch" is a Western concept, but Japan has its own understanding of marriage at this stage. After seven years, a marriage has moved from the romantic phase into the managerial phase: the couple knows each other thoroughly, has established routines, has likely faced at least one major crisis. In Japan, where divorce, while no longer rare, still carries more social stigma than in the West, the seven-year mark is often a point of quiet renegotiation rather than dramatic rupture.

Okuda's choice of the seven-year mark is precise. Shiori and Seita are not newlyweds discovering each other; they are partners who have built a life. They have shared jokes, shared friends, shared furniture, shared debt, shared history. The question the novel poses is whether this shared life can survive the discovery that its foundation was compromised. In a culture where marriage is still partly understood as a social contract rather than purely a romantic union, the answer is not obvious. The novel's achievement is to make both choices, staying and leaving, feel equally valid and equally painful.

For language learners: Okuda's prose is precise, emotionally direct, and grammatically rich. The novel's alternating perspectives provide exposure to both female and male interior monologue in contemporary Japanese. The vocabulary of infertility treatment, marriage, and emotional negotiation is practical and culturally significant. The grammar of doubt (～だろうか), possibility (～かもしれない), and constrained choice (～しかない) is central to the novel's moral architecture. For intermediate to advanced learners interested in contemporary Japanese fiction, marriage culture, and the language of emotional precision, this is an excellent and approachable text.

08 Study Guide

Reading Strategies

  • Track the alternating perspectives. Notice how the same events feel different from Shiori's and Seita's viewpoints. What does each narrator withhold, consciously or unconsciously?
  • Pay attention to time markers. The novel moves between present and past, and the shifts are often subtle. Note when the tense changes and what this reveals about the narrator's emotional position.
  • Follow the metaphor of the title. How does the biblical promise resonate through the novel? Where does "asking" appear, and what follows it?
  • Analyze Seita's use of polite and humble language. How does his speech change when he is being "the ideal husband" versus when he is confessing? In Japanese, honorific shifts are significant narrative signals.
  • Compare the izakaya scenes in Chapter 2 with the present-day medical scenes in Chapter 1. How has the couple's dynamic changed? What has been lost, and what has been gained?

Discussion Questions

  1. The novel's origin was a conversation about a love potion. How does the story translate this fantastical premise into realistic terms? What is the "love potion" that Seita actually uses?
  2. Shiori says she has never been hurt by Seita in twelve years. Is this a sign of a good marriage, or is it a sign that something was being suppressed? Can a marriage be too conflict-free?
  3. Seita's secret is revealed through a social media post sent by an unknown account. Why does Okuda choose this method of revelation? What does it suggest about privacy, anonymity, and the digital mediation of relationships?
  4. The novel asks whether love that began in manipulation can become "real." What evidence does the novel provide for each side of this question? How does the ending resolve it, if at all?
  5. Infertility treatment is the novel's trigger, not its main subject. How would the novel be different if the couple had a child, or if they had never wanted one? Is the infertility essential to the moral drama, or is it a particularly intense form of the pressure that any long marriage faces?
