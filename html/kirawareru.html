Title Analysis

Title Grammar: The Paradox of Chosen Dislikeability

The title Kirawareru Yūki (嫌われる勇気, "The Courage to Be Disliked") is a grammatical paradox that contains the entire philosophy of Adlerian psychology in four syllables. It combines the passive potential kirawareru (嫌われる, "to be disliked / to be capable of being disliked") with the noun yūki (勇気, "courage / bravery"), creating a noun phrase that sounds like an oxymoron: courage is normally the quality we summon to avoid being disliked, to win approval, to succeed in the social arena. By making "being disliked" the object of courage, the title performs a radical inversion: it asks the reader to consider that the path to freedom may run directly through the very thing they have spent their lives avoiding. The grammar of the title is thus not merely descriptive but performative — it enacts the Adlerian reversal that the book will spend five nights explaining.

Title Breakdown

嫌われる kirawareru / "to be disliked / to be capable of being disliked" "to be disliked [passive potential]" — Kirawareru is the passive potential form of kirau (嫌う, "to dislike / to hate / to find disagreeable"), combining the passive suffix -rareru (られる) with the potential sense embedded in the passive of subjective verbs. The word kirau (嫌う) is a strong verb in Japanese — stronger than sukiní naranai (好きにならない, "not to come to like"), more visceral than fumanzoku (不満足, "dissatisfied"). It carries the sense of active aversion, of turning away from, of finding something or someone repugnant. The passive potential kirawareru thus means not merely "to be disliked" but "to be in a state where one can be disliked / to be capable of being disliked" — a grammatical nuance that English "to be disliked" cannot fully capture. The potential aspect is crucial: the title is not talking about someone who is disliked (a fact) but about someone who is willing to be disliked (a capacity, a choice, a risk). The Japanese passive potential preserves this sense of capability and willingness; English "to be disliked" sounds like a mere state. The title's use of the passive also performs an important philosophical operation: it removes the agent of dislike. We do not know who dislikes; the sentence has no "by" phrase. This agentless passive makes "being disliked" into a general condition, an atmospheric possibility, rather than a specific interpersonal event. The philosophical implication is profound: Adlerian psychology is not about managing specific enemies but about developing the capacity to tolerate social disapproval as such — from anyone, in any context, for any reason. The grammar of kirawareru thus encodes the universality of the book's concern.
勇気 yūki / "courage / bravery / nerve" "courage" — Yūki (勇気) combines yū (勇, "bravery / valor / daring") with ki (気, "spirit / energy / mind"), literally "brave spirit." It is the word used for physical courage (saving someone from a fire), moral courage (speaking truth to power), and psychological courage (facing one's own fears). In Japanese, yūki is not merely the absence of fear but the presence of something positive — the spirit that moves forward despite fear. The title's genius is to attach this noble noun to the most socially stigmatized passive verb in everyday life. "Courage" is normally associated with heroic actions: yūki ga aru (勇気がある) means "has courage," and we say this of firefighters, activists, people who speak up in meetings. By making yūki the head noun and kirawareru its modifier (via the attributive form), the title creates a phrase that sounds almost shocking: "the courage of being-disliked." The shock is pedagogical. It forces the reader to ask: Why would anyone need courage to be disliked? Isn't being disliked precisely what happens when you lack courage — when you fail to please, to conform, to perform? The Adlerian answer, which the book will unfold across five nights of dialogue, is that being disliked is not a failure but a consequence of freedom. If you live by your own standards rather than others', if you stop seeking approval, if you separate your tasks from others' expectations — you will inevitably, at some point, be disliked by someone. The courage the title names is the courage to accept this consequence as the price of authenticity. What is lost in the English translation "The Courage to Be Disliked" is the attributive compactness of the Japanese. "The courage to be disliked" makes courage into an infinitive-directed capacity (courage to do something); the Japanese kirawareru yūki makes "being-disliked" into a quality that modifies courage itself — a courage of a specific, paradoxical kind. The English sounds like a self-help injunction ("Have the courage to be disliked!"); the Japanese sounds like a philosophical category ("the being-disliked type of courage").

Authors

岸見一郎 & 古賀史健 — The Philosopher and the Writer Who Made Adler Japanese

岸見一郎 / Kishimi Ichirō (b. 1956)

Kishimi Ichirō was born in 1956 in Kyoto Prefecture. He graduated from Kyoto University Faculty of Letters and completed the doctoral program at Kyoto University Graduate School of Letters (PhD coursework, Western Philosophy major), leaving after fulfilling all requirements. From 1989, he began studying Adlerian psychology in parallel with his primary academic field of ancient Greek philosophy (especially Plato). He has served as a part-time lecturer at Kyoto University of Education, Konan University, Nara Women's University, and Kyoto St. Catherine High School Nursing Department, and worked as a counselor at Maeda Clinic (psychiatry). He is a certified counselor of the Japan Adlerian Psychological Association and serves as its advisor. His bibliography before The Courage to Be Disliked included numerous academic and popular works on Adler and Plato: Adler Shinrigaku Nyūmon (『アドラー心理学入門』, "Introduction to Adlerian Psychology," 1998), translations of Adler's Kojin Shinrigaku Kōgi (『個人心理学講義』, "Lectures on Individual Psychology"), and works on Platonic philosophy including translations of the Timaeus and Critias. For decades, Kishimi was known primarily within academic and clinical circles — a philosopher who happened to work with Adlerian concepts. What he lacked was a medium through which to reach the general reader. That medium turned out to be a writer from Fukuoka who understood that philosophical truth, to travel, must wear the clothes of narrative.

古賀史健 / Koga Fumitake (b. 1973)

Koga Fumitake was born in August 1973 in Higashi Ward, Fukuoka City, Fukuoka Prefecture. He attended Fukuoka University Ohori High School (where he was a bench member of the soccer team that reached the National High School Soccer Tournament) and graduated from the Faculty of Arts at Kyushu Sangyo University. After working at an eyeglass shop and then at a publishing house, he became an independent writer and editor in 1998. Before The Courage to Be Disliked, he had already built a successful career as a ghostwriter and editor, contributing to over 80 books including Zero (『ゼロ』, by Horie Takafumi) and numerous interview collections. In 2014, he received the Special Jury Prize at the Business Book Awards for his contributions to elevating the status of business writers. In 2015, he founded Batons Inc. (株式会社バトンズ), a writer's agency dedicated to training the next generation of writers and passing on reliable writing techniques. Koga's role in The Courage to Be Disliked was not merely editorial but creative: he spent months interviewing Kishimi, then shaped the material into the five-night dialogue structure that gives the book its narrative drive. The "Philosopher" character speaks in Kishimi's voice; the "Youth" character speaks in Koga's — skeptical, resistant, emotionally volatile, representative of every reader who has ever said "But that's impossible" to good advice. This division of labor — philosopher provides the content, writer provides the form — is the secret of the book's success. Without Kishimi, there would be no Adlerian depth; without Koga, there would be no narrative accessibility. The collaboration is a living demonstration of the book's central teaching: that horizontal relationships (two equals bringing different strengths) produce better results than vertical ones (expert lecturing novice).

History

Adler in Japan: From Obscurity to Cultural Phenomenon

Alfred Adler (1870–1937): The Third Giant

Alfred Adler was born in Vienna in 1870, the second of seven children. A sickly child who nearly died of pneumonia at age 5, he experienced intense feelings of inferiority that would shape his entire psychological theory — the theory of "inferiority feeling" (Minderwertigkeitsgefühl) and its compensation through "striving for superiority" (Streben nach Überlegenheit). He received his MD from the University of Vienna in 1895, opened an ophthalmology practice, then shifted to general medicine and psychiatry. In 1902, he joined Freud's weekly "Wednesday Society" discussions, becoming one of the founding members of the psychoanalytic movement. But Adler's ideas diverged fundamentally from Freud's: where Freud emphasized unconscious drives, childhood trauma, and the determinism of the past ("the unconscious is always right"), Adler emphasized conscious goals, social interest, and the creativity of the present ("the individual is always right" — meaning, the individual's logic is always coherent from their own perspective). In 1911, the split became public; Adler resigned from the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society and founded the Society for Individual Psychology. The term "individual psychology" (Individualpsychologie) was chosen not to emphasize individuality but to signal indivisibility — Adler believed the human being must be understood as a unified whole, not divided into id, ego, and superego. His key concepts — Gemeinschaftsgefühl (community feeling / social interest), the courage to be imperfect, the rejection of diagnostic labels, the emphasis on encouragement (Mutmachen) over condemnation — formed a psychology of democratic humanism that was decades ahead of its time. Adler died in Aberdeen, Scotland, in 1937, while on a lecture tour. In the English-speaking world, his influence waned after his death, overshadowed by Freudian orthodoxy and later by behaviorism and cognitive psychology. In Japan, he was virtually unknown to the general public before 2013.

Adler Comes to Japan: The Long Road to Bestseller

Adlerian psychology was introduced to Japan in the 1920s and 1930s through translations and academic articles, but it remained confined to specialized circles — clinical psychologists, educators, and a small group of devoted readers. The first significant Japanese translation of Adler's work appeared in the postwar period, and the Japan Adlerian Psychological Association was founded in 1984. For decades, Adler remained a "specialist's psychologist" — respected by those who knew him, unknown to everyone else. The Freudian narrative (trauma, repression, the unconscious) dominated popular understanding of psychology; the cognitive-behavioral model (thoughts cause feelings) dominated the self-help market. Adler's unique combination of teleology (future-directed purpose), social embeddedness, and philosophical depth had no popular champion. This changed in 2013, when Kishimi and Koga published The Courage to Be Disliked. The book's timing was perfect: Japanese society in the early 2010s was experiencing what sociologists call muen shakai (無縁社会, "relationless society") — a perceived breakdown of community bonds, increased isolation, and widespread anxiety about social evaluation in the age of social media. The book's message — that freedom comes from separating your tasks from others' expectations, that you can stop seeking approval, that all problems are interpersonal problems — landed with explosive force. Within two years it had sold a million copies; within six, two million; by 2024, over three million domestically and nearly twelve million worldwide. The "Adler boom" (Adler būmu / アドラーブーム) transformed the psychology landscape in Japan: Adlerian concepts entered everyday language (kadai no bunri / 課題の分離, kyōdōtai kankaku / 共同体感覚), Adlerian counseling centers proliferated, and a wave of secondary literature followed. What had been a niche academic interest became, almost overnight, a national conversation about how to live.

2013: The Self-Help Landscape and the Dialogue Revolution

The year 2013 was a pivotal one for Japanese publishing. The self-help market had been dominated for years by American translations — Covey's The 7 Habits, Carnegie's How to Win Friends, Kiyosaki's Rich Dad Poor Dad — and by Japanese business books that applied these frameworks to corporate life. What was missing was a genuinely philosophical self-help book: one that engaged with fundamental questions of human existence (freedom, happiness, relationships, meaning) without resorting to bullet points and action steps. The Courage to Be Disliked filled this gap by doing something unprecedented: it presented a complete psychological system in the form of a Platonic dialogue. The choice of form was as important as the content. By structuring the book as five nights of conversation between a Philosopher and a Youth, Kishimi and Koga solved the central problem of philosophical writing: how to anticipate and respond to the reader's objections without breaking the narrative frame. Every time the Youth says "But that's impossible!" or "I can't accept that!", he is voicing the reader's own resistance. The Philosopher's patient, Socratic responses model the very Adlerian virtue the book advocates: the courage to continue a difficult conversation without forcing agreement. The dialogue form also allowed the book to be emotionally engaging in a way that standard self-help prose rarely achieves. Readers do not merely learn Adlerian concepts; they experience the process of being persuaded by them, of moving from skepticism to acceptance, of feeling their own resistance and watching it dissolve. This narrative dimension — the emotional arc of the Youth's transformation — is what made the book a "page-turner" despite being a work of philosophy. It is also what made it infinitely shareable: readers wanted to give it to friends who were struggling with the same issues the Youth struggles with — approval anxiety, interpersonal conflict, fear of change.

The Global Adler Boom: From Japanese Phenomenon to World Literature

The book's international success is as remarkable as its domestic explosion. Translated into over 30 languages, it has become a genuine global phenomenon — particularly in East Asia, where Adlerian concepts resonated with cultural concerns about social pressure, filial obligation, and the tension between individual desire and collective expectation. In South Korea, where the book sold over 2 million copies, it became a touchstone for discussions of "hell Joseon" (the sense of social entrapment among young people) and the pressure to conform to parental expectations. In China (3.9 million copies), it fed into broader conversations about "inner卷" (neijuan, involution / rat race) and the search for psychological freedom within a high-pressure society. In Taiwan (300,000 copies), it became popular among young professionals struggling with workplace hierarchy and social comparison. The book's success in the West has been more gradual but equally significant: it has been embraced by therapists, coaches, and readers seeking an alternative to the trauma-focused, past-oriented model that dominates American psychology. What all these markets share is a recognition that Adler's psychology — mediated through Kishimi's philosophical interpretation and Koga's narrative craft — speaks to a universal human predicament: the terror of social disapproval and the longing for authentic freedom. The Japanese book that made Adler famous worldwide is, in this sense, not merely a translation of Adler but a creative transformation of him — a work that makes Adler's ideas accessible without simplifying them, and that demonstrates through its own existence the very principle it advocates: that horizontal collaboration (between philosopher and writer, between author and reader, between Adler and his Japanese interpreters) can produce something neither party could achieve alone.

Structure

Five Nights That Change a Life: The Architecture of Adlerian Dialogue

The Architecture: Five Nights, Five Doors to Freedom

The book is structured as five nights of dialogue between a Philosopher (哲人, tetsujin) who has mastered Adlerian psychology and a Youth (青年, seinen) who arrives at the Philosopher's house determined to prove him wrong. This structure — five sequential conversations of increasing depth and intensity — is a deliberate echo of Platonic dialogue (particularly the Meno and Republic) and of the Buddhist kōan collections in which a student confronts a master across multiple encounters. Each night has a thematic focus that builds on the previous nights, creating a cumulative argument that is impossible to fully grasp until the final page. Night One ("Deny Trauma") introduces the foundational Adlerian concepts of teleology (purpose-driven psychology) versus aetiology (cause-driven psychology), and argues that human beings are not determined by their past. Night Two ("All Problems Are Interpersonal Relationship Problems") establishes the book's central thesis and explores the concepts of inferiority feelings, superiority complexes, and the life tasks (work, friendship, love). Night Three ("Separation of Tasks") presents the book's most famous and controversial concept — the radical division between one's own tasks and others' tasks — and argues that all interpersonal conflict arises from violating this boundary. Night Four ("Where the Center of the World Is") introduces Gemeinschaftsgefühl (community feeling) as the antidote to isolation and explains the shift from vertical to horizontal relationships. Night Five ("To Live in Earnest in the Here and Now") brings together all previous themes into a philosophy of radical presentness, arguing that life is a series of moments and that happiness is found in contribution to others. The five-night structure is not merely organizational; it is therapeutic. Each night dismantles a layer of the Youth's (and the reader's) resistance, moving from intellectual skepticism to emotional acceptance to practical commitment. By Night Five, the Youth who arrived shouting objections has become someone who can contemplate, with genuine curiosity, the possibility of a completely different way of living.

The Philosopher: Adler as Socratic Midwife

The Philosopher character is a deliberate construction: not a therapist, not a professor, but a philosophical practitioner who lives alone on the outskirts of the city and spends his evenings in dialogue with troubled visitors. This figure combines the Socratic maieutikos (midwife who helps others give birth to their own understanding) with the Adlerian counselor who refuses to diagnose, label, or condemn. The Philosopher's most important quality is his refusal to be drawn into the Youth's emotional drama. When the Youth shouts, the Philosopher speaks softly. When the Youth makes sweeping generalizations, the Philosopher asks specific questions. When the Youth demands comfort, the Philosopher offers clarity. This steady, unflappable presence is the book's hidden teaching: the demonstration that it is possible to engage with another person's suffering without being swallowed by it, to maintain one's own emotional boundaries while remaining fully present. The Philosopher never tells the Youth what to do; he asks questions that force the Youth to examine his own logic. This Socratic method is the practical application of "separation of tasks": the Philosopher does not take responsibility for the Youth's change; he merely provides the conditions in which change becomes possible. The reader who finds the Philosopher irritating — his calm, his refusal to be provoked, his relentless questioning — is experiencing exactly the resistance that the Youth feels, and the book's wager is that this very irritation is the first sign that something important is being challenged.

The Youth: Every Reader's Surrogate Self

The Youth is one of the most cleverly constructed reader surrogates in contemporary literature. He is not merely skeptical; he is actively hostile to the Philosopher's ideas. He arrives with a "folder of refutations" — notes he has compiled to prove Adlerian psychology wrong. He interrupts, shouts, accuses the Philosopher of being naive, cruel, and out of touch with reality. He brings concrete examples from his own life: his fear of social interaction, his conflict with his parents, his inability to change, his terror of being disliked. Every reader who has ever thought "Yes, but my situation is different" will find their objection voiced by the Youth in the first few pages. This construction is crucial to the book's effectiveness. If the Philosopher were speaking to a passive, accepting student, the reader would feel preached at. But because the Youth fights back at every step — because he forces the Philosopher to clarify, to give examples, to address real-world complexities — the reader receives not a lecture but a process. The Youth's gradual softening, his moments of stunned silence, his reluctant admissions ("I see... that makes sense, but...") mirror the reader's own journey. By the end of Night Five, the Youth has not become a disciple; he has become a fellow traveler — someone who has understood enough to continue the journey on his own. This is the Adlerian ideal of education: not to create dependency but to foster independence, not to give answers but to develop the capacity to find them.

The Five Nights as Dismantling of Resistance

Each night of the dialogue targets a specific form of reader resistance. Night One attacks the "trauma excuse" — the comfortable belief that our present suffering is caused by past events and therefore not our responsibility. The Youth's fury at this suggestion ("Are you saying my suffering is my own fault?") is the fury of every reader who has found solace in the aetiological narrative. Night Two attacks social comparison — the belief that our worth is determined by how we measure against others. The Youth's discovery that his inferiority feelings are not facts but interpretations is the book's first genuine breakthrough moment. Night Three attacks approval-seeking — the engine that drives most human behavior and the source of most human misery. The concept of "separation of tasks" (kadai no bunri) is the book's most liberating and terrifying idea: liberating because it frees you from others' expectations, terrifying because it means you can no longer blame others for your choices. Night Four attacks isolation — the belief that we are separate atoms competing in a zero-sum game. The introduction of Gemeinschaftsgefühl reframes human existence as fundamentally cooperative rather than competitive. Night Five attacks postponement — the belief that life will begin "once I..." (graduate, get married, succeed, retire). The concept of "earnestness" (isagiyosa / 潔さ) as living fully in each moment brings the argument to its practical conclusion. The cumulative effect is a complete reconstruction of the reader's worldview — not through new information but through the dismantling of old assumptions. This is why the book rewards rereading: each pass reveals new layers of resistance in oneself that the dialogue is designed to dissolve.

Text

Key Passages

These passages contain the book's most transformative sentences — the statements that sound outrageous until they sound obvious, the arguments that dismantle a lifetime of assumptions in a single paragraph.

01
"All Problems Are Interpersonal Relationship Problems"
人間の悩みは、すべて対人関係の悩みである。
"Ningen no nayami wa, subete tājin kankei no nayami de aru."
"All human troubles are troubles of interpersonal relationships."

Nuance Note

This sentence — the book's central thesis, stated with almost shocking simplicity in the opening pages of Night Two — is the Adlerian axiom from which everything else follows, and its grammar performs the very reduction it describes. Ningen no nayami wa (人間の悩みは, "as for human troubles / worries [topic]") sets the subject with the topic marker, making "human troubles" the frame within which a universal claim will be made. Subete (すべて, "all / everything / without exception") is the universal quantifier, delivered without qualification or hedging. The absence of hedging language (no "almost," no "often," no "in many cases") is the sentence's most striking feature: it states a universal truth with the flat certainty of a mathematical proof. Tājin kankei no nayami de aru (対人関係の悩みである, "are troubles of interpersonal relationships") uses the formal copula de aru (である) rather than the conversational da (だ), giving the sentence the weight of a philosophical proposition rather than a casual observation. The grammar of identity (A wa B de aru / AはBである, "A is B") makes this not an opinion but a definition — a statement about the essential nature of human troubles. What is lost in English is the specific resonance of nayami (悩み). "Troubles," "worries," "suffering," "anguish" — none quite captures the full range of nayami, which encompasses everything from mild unease to deep spiritual distress, from practical problems to existential despair. Nayami is the word used for the Buddha's recognition that "life is suffering" (shōtai ku / 生老病死, the Four Sufferings); it is also the word used for everyday worries about work and relationships. By using nayami rather than a more clinical term like kunō (苦悩, "anguish") or shūshū (愁傷, "grief"), the sentence makes its claim applicable to every reader's experience, from the trivial to the profound. And the phrase tājin kankei (対人関係, "interpersonal relationships") is not merely "relationships" but specifically person-to-person relations — the tai (対) indicating face-to-face, reciprocal, mutual connection. The sentence thus claims not that all problems involve other people (trivially true) but that all problems are problems of the relational field itself — that the medium of human suffering is always the space between selves. This is the Adlerian revolution in a single line: not "you have problems and they affect your relationships" but "your relationships are your problems."

02
"Trauma Does Not Exist"
トラウマは、存在しない。
"Torauma wa, sonzai shinai."
"Trauma does not exist."

Nuance Note

This sentence — the Philosopher's most provocative claim in Night One — is the book's declaration of war against Freudian aetiology, and its grammar creates a philosophical shockwave through sheer brevity. Torauma wa (トラウマは, "as for trauma [topic]") takes a word that has become almost sacred in contemporary discourse — the trauma that explains everything from phobias to relationship patterns to political positions — and places it under examination. The comma pause after wa creates the space in which the reader braces for qualification ("Trauma is... complex" / "Trauma is... misunderstood") and then receives not qualification but annihilation. Sonzai shinai (存在しない, "does not exist / is nonexistent") uses the formal negative of sonzai suru (存在する, "to exist / to be extant"), a verb of ontological weight — the word used in philosophy to discuss being versus non-being. To say something sonzai shinai is not merely to say "it doesn't happen" or "it's rare"; it is to deny it reality-status, to remove it from the catalogue of things that are. The sentence's five syllables thus perform an extraordinary rhetorical operation: they take a concept that has structured an entire field of psychology (trauma studies, PTSD treatment, recovered memory therapy) and declare it void. What is lost in English is the full philosophical violence of sonzai shinai. "Does not exist" captures the meaning but softens the blow because English uses "exist" more casually; in Japanese, sonzai suru is a weighty verb, the word of Heideggerian ontology (Sein / 存在) and Buddhist metaphysics (sonzai to mu / 存在と無, "being and non-being"). The Philosopher is not saying "trauma is overstated" or "trauma is often misdiagnosed"; he is saying that the concept itself, understood as a causal mechanism that determines present behavior, has no valid place in a psychology of freedom. The sentence is thus not empirical but definitional: if psychology is the study of how people can change, and if "trauma" is defined as something that prevents change, then "trauma" in that sense does not exist — because people can change, and the belief that they cannot is itself the obstacle. The English reader must work harder to feel the full heresy of this claim, because "trauma" in English has clinical, political, and moral dimensions that make its denial sound like cruelty. The Japanese torauma (トラウマ), being a loanword, carries slightly less moral weight — it sounds more clinical, more technical, more debatable — which may be why the book's Japanese readers were able to hear the philosophical argument beneath the provocation.

03
"Separation of Tasks"
あることをするのが誰か課題であるかを考え、そこから自分の課題と他者の課題を切り分ける。そして、その他者の課題から手を引く。これが、課題の分離である。
"Aru koto wo suru no ga dareka no kadai de aru ka wo kanga'e, soko kara jibun no kadai to tasha no kadai wo kirabuneru. Soshite, sono tasha no kadai kara te wo hiku. Kore ga, kadai no bunri de aru."
"Consider whose task it is to do a given thing, and from there, separate your own tasks from other people's tasks. And then, withdraw your hands from those other people's tasks. This is the separation of tasks."

Nuance Note

This passage — the book's most practical and controversial concept, introduced in Night Three — is the Adlerian technology of freedom, and its grammar creates a procedure so simple it sounds like a household chore and so radical it restructures every relationship. Aru koto wo suru no ga dareka no kadai de aru ka (あることをするのが誰かの課題であるか, "whose task it is to do a certain thing") uses the nominalizing no (の) with the explanatory ka (か) to create a question about ownership — not "what should be done" but "whose kadai is it to do this." The word kadai (課題, "task / problem / assignment / issue") is crucial: it is not merely "work" or "job" but a comprehensive term for anything that requires one's attention and effort — homework is kadai, a research question is kadai, a life problem is kadai. By framing interpersonal boundaries in terms of kadai-ownership, Adlerian psychology makes ethics into a kind of property law: who owns this problem? who is responsible for solving it? Kirabuneru (切り分ける, "to separate / to divide / to distinguish") uses the continuative kiri (切り, "cut") with the potential wakeru (分ける, "to divide"), creating a word that means "to cleanly separate" — the image is of a knife cutting along a joint, separating meat from bone with precision. This is not "boundaries" as a vague concept; it is "separation" as a specific operation. Te wo hiku (手を引く, "to withdraw one's hands / to pull back") is the physical metaphor that makes the abstract concrete: you literally remove your hands from what is not yours. The phrase carries the gentle gravity of someone stepping back from a craft project that is not theirs to complete — a gesture of respect rather than rejection. Kore ga, kadai no bunri de aru (これが、課題の分離である, "this is the separation of tasks") uses the formal copula to deliver the definition with pedagogical clarity. What is lost in English is the full force of kadai as a life-category. "Task" in English sounds work-related; "problem" sounds negative; "issue" sounds clinical. Kadai encompasses all of these and something more — the sense of something given to you (ka / 課, "to assign") that you must address (dai / 題, "topic / theme"). The "separation of tasks" is thus not merely about minding your own business; it is about recognizing that every human being has their own kadai assigned to them by the simple fact of existence, and that violating this assignment — by taking over others' kadai or allowing others to take over yours — is the root of all interpersonal evil. The English "separation of tasks" sounds like project management; the Japanese kadai no bunri sounds like a philosophy of existence.

04
"Community Feeling"
共同体感覚とは、他者と共生する感覚のことである。自分以外の人間を夥しい敵としてではなく、どこかでつながっている仲間として感じることだ。
"Kyōdōtai kankaku to wa, tasha to kyōsei suru kankaku no koto de aru. Jibun igai no ningen wo oobaka na teki to shite de wa naku, dokoka de tsunagatte iru nakama to shite kanjiru koto da."
"Community feeling is the feeling of living in symbiosis with others. It is feeling that people other than yourself are not innumerable enemies but companions connected to you somewhere."

Nuance Note

This passage — the introduction of Adler's central ethical concept in Night Four — is the book's most beautiful statement of human solidarity, and its grammar creates a feeling-tone as warm as the concept it names. Kyōdōtai kankaku to wa (共同体感覚とは, "as for community feeling [topic + quotative]") uses the definitional construction to wa (とは) — the quotative particle combined with the topic marker — which signals that a formal definition is coming. This is the grammar of encyclopedias and philosophical treatises: "By X we mean..." Tasha to kyōsei suru kankaku no koto de aru (他者と共生する感覚のことである, "is the feeling of co-living with others") uses the verbal noun kyōsei (共生, "symbiosis / co-living / co-existence") — a biological term for mutually beneficial coexistence — to describe a psychological state. The biological metaphor is deliberate: Adlerian Gemeinschaftsgefühl is not merely "being nice to people" but a fundamental mode of organismic relatedness, as basic as breathing. Jibun igai no ningen (自分以外の人間, "humans other than oneself") uses the exclusion marker igai (以外, "except / other than") to create the category of "everyone who is not me" — a category that, for the isolated modern self, can feel like a hostile army. Oobaka na teki to shite de wa naku (夥しい敵としてではなく, "not as innumerable enemies") uses the adjective oobaka (夥しい, "innumerable / countless / vast") to evoke the overwhelming sensation of facing a crowd of strangers — the social anxiety that the concept of community feeling is designed to dissolve. Dokoka de tsunagatte iru nakama to shite kanjiru koto da (どこかでつながっている仲間として感じることだ, "it is feeling them as companions connected somewhere") uses the continuative tsunagatte iru (つながっている, "are connected") to describe a state of ongoing connection, and nakama (仲間, "companions / comrades / fellows") — a word of deep emotional warmth in Japanese, evoking the bonds of shared purpose and mutual recognition. What is lost in English is almost everything. "Community feeling" is the standard translation of Adler's Gemeinschaftsgefühl, but it sounds like civic engagement — voting, volunteering, neighborhood associations. The German Gemeinschaftsgefühl means literally "community-feeling" — a felt sense of belonging to the human community — and the Japanese kyōdōtai kankaku (共同体感覚) preserves this: kyōdōtai (共同体, "community / collective body") + kankaku (感覚, "feeling / sensation / sense"). But "community feeling" in English cannot convey the existential depth of what is being described: not a social preference but an ontological condition, the recognition that one's own existence is inseparable from the existence of others. The Japanese nakama (仲間) is also untranslatable — "companions" is too literary, "comrades" too political, "fellows" too archaic. Nakama is the word for the people you belong with, the ones who share your world, your burdens, your fate. The passage's movement from oobaka na teki (夥しい敵, "innumerable enemies") to tsunagatte iru nakama (つながっている仲間, "connected companions") is the movement from hell to home — and the grammar makes this movement feel not like a moral injunction but like a description of what becomes visible when fear dissolves.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Kishimi and Koga write with philosophical precision and narrative craft — and the translation of their blockbuster raises urgent questions about how to carry Adler's German, Kishimi's Japanese, and the dialogue's dramatic tension into English and other languages.

1. Kadai no Bunri (課題の分離): The Untranslatable Core

JP 課題の分離 — separation of tasks / the clean division of whose problem is whose / the property-law of interpersonal ethics
EN "separation of tasks" / "task separation" / "mind your own business" — each loses the philosophical depth
Loss As discussed in the passage analysis, kadai no bunri (課題の分離) is the book's central practical concept and its most untranslatable phrase. The standard English "separation of tasks" makes it sound like project management — a technique for dividing work in an office. But kadai is not "task" in the narrow sense; it is the entire domain of human responsibility, from "should I take this job?" to "should I confront my parents?" to "should I end this relationship?" The "separation" (bunri / 分離) is not merely organizational but existential — a recognition that each human being has their own kadai assigned by life, and that violating this assignment is the fundamental sin against human freedom. English "boundaries" captures the general idea but loses the Adlerian specificity; "mind your own business" captures the attitude but loses the philosophical architecture; "non-interference" sounds political rather than psychological. The German original Gemeinschaftsgefühl has at least entered English as a technical term; kadai no bunri has no such foothold. The loss matters because the entire book is an extended meditation on this concept, and readers who encounter it only in translation may think they are learning a productivity technique rather than a philosophy of existence. The Japanese reader hears in kadai the echo of every school assignment, every work project, every life decision — the full spectrum of what one is responsible for — and in bunri the clean precision of a surgical cut. The English reader hears "task management."

2. Kyōdōtai Kankaku (共同体感覚): From German Feeling to Japanese Sense

JP 共同体感覚 — community feeling / the felt sense of belonging to the human community / social interest as an organ of perception
EN "community feeling" / "social interest" / "social feeling" — each captures one dimension
Loss Adler's original German term Gemeinschaftsgefühl combines Gemeinschaft (community / fellowship) with Gefühl (feeling / emotion), and it has been translated into English as both "community feeling" and "social interest" — two quite different concepts. "Community feeling" emphasizes the affective, experiential dimension (how it feels to belong); "social interest" emphasizes the ethical, action-oriented dimension (concern for the welfare of others). The Japanese kyōdōtai kankaku (共同体感覚) chooses a third path: kyōdōtai (共同体, "community / collective body") + kankaku (感覚, "sense / sensation / feeling / perception"). Kankaku is broader than kanjō (感情, "emotion"): it includes physical sensation, aesthetic perception, and intuitive awareness. By using kankaku rather than kanjō, the Japanese translation suggests that Gemeinschaftsgefühl is not merely an emotion (like love or compassion) but a mode of perception — a way of sensing the world that includes others as part of one's own field of awareness. This is philosophically rich but difficult to convey in English. "Community feeling" loses the perceptual dimension; "social interest" loses the experiential dimension; "social sense" sounds like manners or etiquette. The loss is compounded by the fact that Adler considered Gemeinschaftsgefühl the single most important measure of mental health — the capacity that distinguishes the "well-adapted" from the "maladjusted" individual. Without a precise translation, English readers may underestimate the centrality of this concept, thinking it merely one idea among many rather than the organizing principle of Adler's entire system.

3. Yūkidzuke (勇気づけ): More Than Encouragement

JP 勇気づけ — giving courage / the act of helping another find their own bravery / not praise, not comfort, but the awakening of self-efficacy
EN "encouragement" / "empowerment" / "cheering on" — each too weak or too different
Loss The word yūkidzuke (勇気づけ) is the nominalized form of yūki dzukeru (勇気づける, "to give courage to / to encourage"), but its meaning in Adlerian psychology is highly specific and almost impossible to translate adequately. English "encouragement" is the standard translation, but it carries unfortunate associations: cheerleading, empty praise, "You can do it!" posters. Adlerian yūkidzuke is almost the opposite: it is not about telling someone they are great but about helping them discover that they are capable — not through praise but through trust, not through reassurance but through the refusal to do for them what they can do for themselves. The etymology of yūki (勇気, "courage / brave spirit") is important: Adlerian psychology is a psychology of courage, not of adjustment or insight or behavior modification. The therapist's job is to "give courage" (yūki wo tsukeru / 勇気をつける) to the client — to help them find the bravery to face their life tasks, to risk disapproval, to live by their own values. "Empowerment" captures some of this but sounds too political and too external (someone "empowers" you); "cheering on" is too superficial; "support" is too vague. The Japanese yūkidzuke preserves the etymological connection to the book's title (yūki / 勇気, "courage") — the entire book is, in a sense, an act of yūkidzuke directed at the reader. The English translation severs this connection: the book is about "courage" but the therapeutic act is "encouragement" — related words, but the etymological thread that binds them is invisible. The loss is thus both lexical and thematic: without yūkidzuke's explicit connection to yūki, the reader may not see that the book's title is not merely a metaphor but a description of the therapeutic process itself — the process of giving the reader the courage to be disliked.

4. Kirawareru (嫌われる): The Passive Potential of Social Risk

JP 嫌われる — to be disliked [passive potential] / to be capable of being disliked / to be in the state where dislike is possible
EN "to be disliked" / "being disliked" / "the possibility of being disliked" — each loses the grammatical nuance
Loss As discussed in the title analysis, the passive potential kirawareru (嫌われる) carries a grammatical nuance that English cannot reproduce. "To be disliked" is a simple passive — it describes a state or an event. But kirawareru is the passive potential of kirau (嫌う), meaning "to be in a state where one can be disliked / to be capable of being disliked." This potential aspect transforms the sentence from a description of fact into a description of risk-capacity. The book is not about people who are disliked; it is about people who are willing to be disliked — who have developed the capacity to tolerate the possibility of social rejection. This is a crucial distinction for the book's philosophy: Adlerian freedom is not about becoming invulnerable to dislike (impossible) but about becoming able to act despite the risk of dislike. The passive potential encodes this "despite" — the capacity to function under conditions of social threat. English "to be disliked" cannot convey this; it makes the title sound like advice for people who are already disliked ("Here's how to have courage when everyone hates you"), whereas the Japanese title speaks to everyone who fears being disliked — which is to say, everyone. The loss is thus not merely grammatical but psychological: without the potential aspect, the title loses its universality and its emphasis on willingness rather than fact.

5. Nattoku (納得): Understanding as Embodied Acceptance

JP 納得 — understanding-through-acceptance / the moment when resistance dissolves and the body says "yes"
EN "understanding" / "acceptance" / "agreement" / "conviction" — each captures one facet
Loss The word nattoku (納得) appears throughout the book as the Youth's gradual response to the Philosopher's arguments — not immediate agreement but a slow, reluctant, embodied acceptance that something is true. Etymologically, it combines nō (納, "to accept / to receive / to put away") with toku (得, "to obtain / to gain / to understand"), suggesting the image of receiving something into oneself and making it one's own. Nattoku suru (納得する) is not merely intellectual understanding (rikai suru / 理解する); it is understanding that has passed through resistance and emerged on the other side — the "aha" moment that includes the body, not just the mind. When the Youth says nattoku dekinai (納得できない, "I cannot accept / I am not convinced"), he is not saying "I don't understand"; he is saying "I understand but I resist." The English translations — "I can't accept that," "I'm not convinced," "That doesn't make sense to me" — each capture one dimension but lose the specific quality of nattoku as a stage in the process of philosophical persuasion. The word is essential to the book's dramatic structure: each night follows an arc from resistance (hanron / 反論) through struggle to partial nattoku to new resistance. Without nattoku, this arc becomes invisible; the Youth simply seems to change his mind arbitrarily. The loss is thus narrative and pedagogical: nattoku is the word for the moment when philosophy enters the body, and English has no single word that carries this meaning.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns from the language of philosophical dialogue, Adlerian psychology, and the Socratic dismantling of everything you thought you knew about yourself.

〜は、〜である（断定・定義） N4

...wa, ...de aru: "[topic] is [definition]" — formal copular construction for stating definitions and universal truths

人間の悩みは、すべて対人関係の悩みである

"All human troubles are troubles of interpersonal relationships" — The grammar of philosophical axioms.

〜とは、〜のことである（定義・説明） N3

...to wa, ...no koto de aru: "by X we mean / X is..." — definitional construction using quotative + topic marker

共同体感覚とは、他者と共生する感覚のことである

"Community feeling is the feeling of co-living with others" — The grammar of conceptual definition.

〜のが〜であるか（所有・帰属の問い） N4

...no ga ...de aru ka: "whether the one who does X is [someone's] task" — nominalized subject with question marker asking about ownership of responsibility

あることをするのが誰かの課題であるか

"whose task it is to do a certain thing" — The grammar of responsibility-assignment.

〜として〜ず（役割＋否定） N4

...to shite...zu: "not as... but as..." — role-marker with negative creating contrastive redefinition

夥しい敵としてではなく、どこかでつながっている仲間として感じる

"feeling them not as innumerable enemies but as connected companions" — The grammar of relational reframing.

〜せよ（命令・古風） N3

...se yo: "[do]!" — archaic imperative form creating philosophical or literary command

トラウマを否定せよ

"Deny trauma!" — The grammar of philosophical provocation.

〜ずに（否定の連用） N4

...zu ni: "without doing..." — negative continuative form indicating absence of action

承認を得ずに生きる

"to live without gaining approval" — The grammar of absence as freedom.

〜から〜を引く（空間・比喩的撤退） N5

...kara...wo hiku: "to pull [something] away from [somewhere]" — physical metaphor for withdrawal or disengagement

他者の課題から手を引く

"to withdraw one's hands from others' tasks" — The grammar of respectful retreat.

〜ばかりだ（限定・継続） N4

...bakari da: "only... / nothing but... / keep on..." — restrictive particle indicating exclusive continuation of a state

人は変われるばかりだ

"People can only change / People can do nothing but change" — The grammar of exclusive possibility.

Study tip: The grammar of The Courage to Be Disliked is the grammar of philosophical clarity — each construction chosen to deliver a precise conceptual operation. The definitional copula (wa... de aru / は...である) states universal truths with mathematical certainty; the definitional quotative (to wa... no koto de aru / とは...のことである) introduces concepts with pedagogical precision; the ownership question (no ga... de aru ka / のが...であるか) makes ethics into property law; the contrastive role-marker (to shite... zu / として...ず) reframes relationships in a single breath; the archaic imperative (se yo / せよ) delivers provocations with philosophical weight; the negative continuative (zu ni / ずに) makes absence into a positive practice; the withdrawal metaphor (kara... wo hiku / から...を引く) embodies respect as physical gesture; and the restrictive (bakari da / ばかりだ) limits possibility to liberate action. Pay attention to how these constructions work together to create a prose that sounds simple because it is precise — the voice of a philosopher who has spent decades learning to say exactly what he means, and a writer who has learned to make that precision feel like a conversation with a friend.
