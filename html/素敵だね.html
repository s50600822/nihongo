素敵だね

Isn't It Wonderful? — A full grammatical and lexical breakdown of Nobuo Uematsu's timeless ballad of transient beauty, where wind, clouds, moon, and stars mirror a love that knows it cannot last.

01 Verse 1

風が 寄せた 言葉に 泳いだ 心
Kaze ga yoseta kotoba ni, oyoida kokoro.
"To the words the wind brought near, a heart swam."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
風kazeNounwind
がgaSubject particlemarks the subject
寄せたyosetaVerb (attributive / past)brought near; drew close — past of 寄せる yoseru
言葉kotobaNounwords; speech; language
にniLocative particleto; toward; into
泳いだoyoidaVerb (attributive / past)swam — past of 泳ぐ oyogu
心kokoroNounheart; mind; spirit; feelings
Grammar: 寄せる means "to draw near; to bring close; to gather," and in this context suggests the wind carrying words toward the speaker, as if the breeze itself were a messenger. The past attributive 寄せた modifies 言葉: "words [that] the wind brought near." に marks the destination or target of the heart's movement: "toward [those] words." 泳いだ modifies 心: "a heart [that] swam." The image is delicate and unusual: the heart is not captured, struck, or moved by the words; it swims in them, as if the words were water. This intransitive use of 泳ぐ applied to 心 creates a sense of the heart floating, drifting, moving freely within the medium of language. The grammar is simple but the semantic choice is extraordinary: a heart that swims is a heart at play, unguarded, at ease. The entire line is a noun phrase (泳いだ心) in apposition to the preceding clause, a poetic compression where the main verb is implied rather than stated.
雲が 運ぶ 明日に 弾んだ 声
Kumo ga hakobu asu ni, hazunda koe.
"Toward the tomorrow the clouds carry, a voice that rang out [with excitement]."

Grammar: 運ぶ is the plain attributive form modifying 明日: "the tomorrow [that] clouds carry." Clouds traditionally symbolize transience and the passage of time in Japanese poetry (going back to the Man'yōshū and Heian waka), so "tomorrow carried by clouds" suggests a future that is airy, shifting, and not entirely within human control. に marks the direction of the voice's energy: "toward [that] tomorrow." 弾む means "to spring; to bounce; to rebound," and when applied to a voice it suggests a bright, lively, almost childlike tone: a voice that rings out with energy and excitement. 弾んだ modifies 声: "a voice [that] sprang [with joy]." The parallel structure with the previous line is exact: [nature] + が + [verb] + [abstract noun] + に, [verb] + [emotion noun]. Wind brings words; heart swims. Clouds carry tomorrow; voice springs. The grammar creates a world where natural elements (風, 雲) carry human experiences (言葉, 明日) toward emotional responses (心, 声).
月が 揺れる 鏡に 震えた 心
Tsuki ga yureru kagami ni, furueta kokoro.
"In the mirror where the moon sways, a trembling heart."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
月tsukiNounmoon; moonlight

揺れるyureruVerb (attributive)sways; trembles; flickers
鏡kagamiNounmirror; reflection; water surface

Grammar: 揺れる modifies 鏡: "the mirror [in which] the moon sways." The image is of moonlight reflected on water (a common poetic motif in Japanese literature, where water surfaces are often called "mirrors"). The moon's reflection shimmers and distorts on rippling water, creating an image of beauty that is unstable, fleeting, and slightly unreal. に marks the location where the heart's trembling takes place: "in [that] mirror [where the moon sways]." 震えた modifies 心: "a heart [that] trembled." The shift from 泳いだ (swam, playful) in the first stanza to 震えた (trembled, vulnerable) marks an emotional deepening. Where the heart swam freely in the wind's words, it now trembles at the moon's reflected instability. The grammar is identical to the first line of the song, but the emotional valence has shifted from joy to fragility. The moon-mirror is a classic symbol of beauty that cannot be grasped: you can see it, but if you touch the water, it shatters.
星が 流れ こぼれた 柔らかい 涙
Hoshi ga nagare, koboreta, yawarakai namida.
"Stars flow, [and] soft tears spilled."

Grammar: 流れ is the continuative form (連用形) of 流れる (to flow; to stream; to fall [of stars]), connecting to the following clause. 星が流れ = "stars flowing" = "shooting stars; falling stars." The continuative form here creates a temporal or causal connection: as the stars fall, tears spill. こぼれた modifies 涙: "tears [that] spilled." 柔らかい (soft; gentle) modifies 涙, creating an oxymoron: tears are usually associated with pain, but these are soft, almost comforting. The word 柔らかい carries connotations of warmth, pliability, and kindness; applied to tears, it suggests crying not from grief but from an overflow of gentle feeling. The parallel with the previous stanza's structure is again exact: [nature] + が + [verb] + [abstract noun]. But here the continuative 流れ creates a more explicit connection between the natural image and the emotional response: the falling stars cause, or coincide with, the spilling of soft tears. The grammar of continuity (流れ) makes emotion feel like a natural consequence of beauty, not a separate event.

02 Chorus 1

Grammar: 素敵だね is the song's title and emotional anchor: 素敵 (wonderful; lovely) + だ (copula) + ね (seeking agreement). The ね is crucial: it doesn't declare wonder, it invites shared recognition of it. "It's wonderful, isn't it?" The speaker is asking the beloved to agree, to share the feeling. 手をとり is the continuative form of 手を取る (to hold hands; to take hands): [te-form] + 取る = "taking hands." The continuative form connects to the conditional clause that follows. 歩けた is the past potential form of 歩く (to walk): "could walk." [Verb in past] + なら creates a hypothetical: "if [we] could walk." The conditional is built on capability, not certainty: the speaker is imagining a possibility, not stating a fact. 行きたいよ is the desiderative + emphatic particle: "I want to go [emphatically]." The list of destinations narrows from public to private: 街 (town) → 家 (home) → 腕の中 (inside your arms). The grammar of narrowing scope (broad place → intimate space) mirrors the emotional progression from public companionship to private embrace. 腕の中 is a set phrase meaning "in one's arms; in an embrace." The katakana キミ (instead of 君) is a stylistic choice that adds a slightly modern, stylized feel to an otherwise classical lyric.

"That chest, entrusting my body, blending into the evening, I dream."

Grammar: その胸 is the destination implied by the following verb: "[to] that chest." The particle を is omitted, a common poetic compression. からだあずけ writes 体 (body) in hiragana, creating a softer, more intimate visual texture. 預ける means "to entrust; to deposit; to leave in someone's care." The continuative form あずけ connects to the next phrase: "entrusting [my] body [and then] ~." 宵にまぎれ uses the continuative form of 紛れる (to blend in; to merge; to be lost among): "blending into the evening." 紛れる carries a shade of being hidden, of disappearing into something larger. 宵 is a literary word for evening, more poetic than 夜 or 夕方. 夢見る is written as a single compound verb: 夢 (dream) + 見る (to see) = "to dream." The entire line is a chain of continuative forms (あずけ, まぎれ) leading to the final verb 夢見る, creating a sense of continuous, flowing action: entrusting body to chest, blending into evening, dreaming. The grammar of continuation (連用形 chaining) makes the three actions feel simultaneous and inseparable, as if they are all part of one gesture of surrender to the moment.

03 Verse 2

風は 止まり 言葉は 優しい 幻
Kaze wa tomari, kotoba wa yasashii maboroshi.
"The wind stops. Words are a gentle illusion."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
風kazeNounwind
はwaTopic particleas for
止まりtomariVerb (continuative / noun)stopping — continuative of 止まる tomaru
言葉kotobaNounwords; speech; language
はwaTopic particleas for
優しいyasashiiI-adjective (attributive)gentle; kind; tender; soft
幻maboroshiNounillusion; phantom; dream; vision
Grammar: The second verse begins with a direct inversion of the first verse's opening. Where the first verse had 風が寄せた言葉に (toward the words the wind brought near), the second verse has 風は止まり (as for the wind, [it] stops). The shift from が (subject, active) to は (topic, descriptive) marks a shift from narrative to commentary: the wind is no longer doing something; it is being described as having stopped. 止まり is the continuative form of 止まる, which here functions as a nominalized verb or a sentence fragment: "the wind stopping" or "the wind stops." The comma creates a caesura, a pause that separates the two clauses. 言葉は優しい幻 is a complete copular sentence with the copula omitted, a common poetic device: "words [are] a gentle illusion." The shift from the first verse's 言葉に (words as destination) to the second verse's 言葉は (words as topic) is devastating: words have gone from something the heart swims toward to something revealed as illusory. The grammar of topic marking (は) makes this a statement of realization, not speculation. The wind that carried words has stopped, and the words are now seen for what they were: beautiful, gentle, and unreal.

Grammar: 破れ is the continuative form of 破れる (to tear; to be torn; to break), here used as a sentence fragment: "clouds tearing" or "clouds tear." Where the first verse had clouds carrying tomorrow (雲が運ぶ明日), the second verse has clouds tearing apart, and tomorrow becoming something distant and auditory rather than something approaching. 明日は遠くの声 is a copular sentence with the copula omitted: "tomorrow [is] a distant voice." The shift from に (toward) in the first verse to は (as for) in the second verse again marks a shift from anticipation to description. Tomorrow is no longer a destination; it is a voice that comes from far away, perhaps unreachable. The grammar of distance (遠くの) applied to 声 creates a poignant image: the future can be heard but not touched, can be recognized but not reached. The parallel with the first verse is exact in structure but inverted in emotional valence: where the first verse's clouds carried tomorrow toward a voice that sprang with excitement, the second verse's clouds tear and tomorrow becomes a voice from far away.
月が にじむ 鏡を 流れた 心
Tsuki ga nijimu, kagami wo, nagareta kokoro.
"The moon blurs. The mirror [that it blurs]. A heart that flowed."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
月tsukiNounmoon; moonlight
がgaSubject particlemarks the subject
にじむnijimuVerb (attributive)to blur; to run; to bleed [colors]
鏡kagamiNounmirror; reflection; water surface
をwoObject particlemarks the object
流れたnagaretaVerb (attributive / past)flowed; streamed — past of 流れる nagareru
心kokoroNounheart; mind; spirit; feelings
Grammar: にじむ means "to blur; to run [of colors]; to bleed [of ink]," the intransitive verb describing what happens when a reflection on water is disturbed or when wet ink spreads. The moon blurs on the mirror-water, just as the heart will blur and flow. 鏡を is an unusual use: the を marks the mirror as the space across which the moon blurs, or as the object of the heart's flowing. The grammar is compressed and slightly ambiguous, which is characteristic of poetic Japanese. One reading: "The moon blurs across the mirror; a heart flowed." Another: "[Toward] the mirror [where] the moon blurs, a heart flowed." The shift from the first verse's 月が揺れる鏡に (in the mirror where the moon sways) to the second verse's 月がにじむ鏡を (the mirror which the moon blurs across) is precise: に (in) becomes を (across/through), and 揺れる (sways) becomes にじむ (blurs). The heart has changed too: where it trembled (震えた) in the first verse, it now flows (流れた). Trembling suggests fear and fragility; flowing suggests surrender and dissolution. The grammar of motion (流れた) makes the heart into something liquid, something that cannot hold its shape, something that follows gravity and finds the lowest point.
星が 揺れて こぼれた 隠せない 涙
Hoshi ga yurete, koboreta, kakusenai namida.
"Stars swayed, [and] spilled [forth] tears that cannot be hidden."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
星hoshiNounstar(s)
がgaSubject particlemarks the subject
揺れてyureteVerb (te-form)swaying; trembling — te-form of 揺れる yureru

Grammar: 揺れて is the te-form of 揺れる (to sway; to tremble), connecting to the following clause: "stars swaying, [and then] ~." Where the first verse had stars flowing (流れ) and soft tears spilling, the second verse has stars swaying (揺れて) and tears that cannot be hidden. The shift from 流れ (continuative) to 揺れて (te-form) is subtle: the continuative suggests ongoing, simultaneous action, while the te-form suggests sequential or causal action. The stars sway, and THEN tears spill. The shift from 柔らかい (soft; gentle) to 隠せない (cannot be hidden) is the emotional core of the verse's transformation. Where the first verse's tears were gentle, almost welcomed, the second verse's tears are uncontrollable, impossible to conceal. 隠せない is the potential negative of 隠す (to hide; to conceal): "cannot hide." It modifies 涙: "tears [that] cannot be hidden." The grammar of incapacity (隠せない) replaces the grammar of quality (柔らかい): the tears are no longer defined by their texture but by the speaker's inability to control them. The parallel structure reveals the song's emotional arc: from beauty that is embraced to beauty that is overwhelming, from tears that are soft to tears that cannot be stopped.

Grammar: The second chorus is identical to the first chorus in its opening lines, creating exact structural repetition. The grammatical analysis is identical. However, the emotional weight has shifted because of what the second verse has revealed: the wind has stopped, words are illusions, clouds have torn, tomorrow is distant, the moon blurs, and tears can no longer be hidden. The same words — "It's wonderful, isn't it? If we could walk hand in hand, I want to go to your town, your home, into your arms" — now carry the weight of impossibility. The conditional 歩けたなら (if we could walk) feels more poignant after the second verse's dissolution of the natural world that carried the first verse's hope. The grammar of repetition here performs the persistence of desire in the face of fading beauty: even when everything else has blurred, torn, and drifted away, the wish remains exactly the same. The unchanged grammar is the point: love doesn't adapt or compromise; it stays exactly as it was, even when the world around it has transformed.

The structural parallel is exact: [body part/face] + [continuative verb phrase] + [time reference with に] + [verb] + 夢見る. But every element has shifted from night to dawn, from surrender to tenderness, from hiding to fading. そっと is an adverb of extreme gentleness: "softly; lightly; quietly; without pressure." It modifies 触れて, the te-form of 触れる (to touch; to brush against): "gently touching." 触れる is a lighter verb than 触る (sawaru): it suggests a brush, a graze, a barely-there contact. 朝に溶ける uses に as the destination of dissolution: "dissolving into morning." 溶ける means "to melt; to dissolve; to fade away," and applied to a dream or vision dissolving into morning light, it captures the exact moment of waking: the dream is still present but becoming transparent, losing substance, being replaced by daylight. The shift from 宵 (evening) to 朝 (morning) completes the song's temporal arc: evening was for hiding, for blending into darkness, for entrusting oneself to another; morning is for dissolution, for the dream ending, for the gentle touch that is the last contact before disappearance. The grammar of the outro is the grammar of farewell: every action is in the continuative or te-form, creating a chain of ongoing, simultaneous gestures that lead to the final verb 夢見る. The dream is not a destination; it is the container that holds all the other actions. Touching the face, dissolving into morning: these ARE the dream, and the dream is ending.

05 Key Grammar Patterns Summary

Attributive Fragments as Complete Poetic Statements
Past/attributive verbs modifying nouns function as complete utterances without copulas. 泳いだ心, 震えた心, 流れた心. The grammar of haiku and waka applied to song lyrics.
連用形 (Continuative Form) Chaining
Verbs in continuative form (i-form) connect actions into flowing sequences. 寄せた言葉に泳いだ心, からだあずけ宵にまぎれ夢見る. Creates simultaneity and continuity.
〜たなら (Past Conditional)
"If [we] could [verb]." Past potential + なら. 歩けたなら. Expresses hypothetical possibility, not certainty. The grammar of wishing.
Copula Omission
Complete sentences with the copula (だ/です) omitted for poetic compression. 優しい幻, 遠くの声. Common in classical poetry and waka; creates imagistic directness.
が vs は Shift
First verse uses が (subject, active, narrative). Second verse uses は (topic, descriptive, commentary). 風が寄せた → 風は止まり. The grammar marks the shift from hope to realization.
〜に (Locative/Directional)
に marks direction toward abstract destinations. 言葉に, 明日に, 鏡に, 宵に, 朝に. The grammar of the heart moving toward language, time, reflection, evening, and dawn.
〜て (Te-Form Continuity)
Te-form connects natural phenomena to emotional responses. 星が流れこぼれた, 星が揺れてこぼれた. Creates temporal and causal flow between outer world and inner feeling.
Nominal Compounds
Compound nouns and compound verbs create compressed meaning. 手を取る → 手とり, からだあずけ, 夢見る. The grammar of classical Japanese poetry's density.
