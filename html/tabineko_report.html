Note: The passages below are based on Tabineko Ripōto (旅猫リポート) by Arikawa Hiro, serialized in Shūkan Bunshun from October 2011 to April 2012 and published by Bungeishunjū in November 2012. The translations and commentary are original to this lesson.

Serialized in Shūkan Bunshun from October 2011 to April 2012 and published as a novel by Bungeishunjū in November 2012, Tabineko Ripōto (旅猫リポート, The Travelling Cat Chronicles) is one of the most beloved works in this lesson series: a road novel told from the perspective of a cat. Its author, Arikawa Hiro (有川浩, b. 1972), was born in Kōchi Prefecture, graduated from Sonoda Gakuen Women's University, and debuted in 2004 with Shio no Machi (塩の街), which won the Dengeki Novel Prize. She is best known for the Toshokan Sensō (図書館戦争, "Library War") series, a fusion of military SF and romantic comedy that won the 39th Seiun Award, and for Hankyū Densha (阪急電車), a bestselling novel of intersecting lives on a single train line. Where the Library War series asks what we would fight to protect and Hankyū Densha asks what a chance encounter on a commuter train can change, Tabineko Ripōto asks something quieter and more devastating: what does it mean to love something you know you will lose? The novel's narrator is Nana (ナナ), a proud, clever, "tsundere" male stray cat who was rescued by Satoru, a gentle young man with a secret. When Satoru can no longer keep Nana, the two embark on a journey in a silver station wagon to find Nana a new home among Satoru's old friends. Each visit reveals not just a candidate for adoption but a chapter of Satoru's hidden past, and Nana's voice — by turns arrogant, tender, and heartbroken — becomes the lens through which we learn what family means when blood ties have been severed and what love means when time is running out. The novel won the 4th Booklog Prize (Novel Category), was nominated for the 26th Yamamoto Shūgorō Prize and the 34th Yoshikawa Eiji Literary Newcomer Prize, and has been adapted into a stage play (2013, script by the author herself), a radio drama, a picture book, and a 2018 film starring Fukushi Sōta as Satoru with Takahata Mitsuki voicing Nana. It has sold millions of copies and been translated into more than a dozen languages, but its power remains rooted in the specific grammar of Japanese intimacy: the way a cat says "I" (吾輩, wagahai), the way a man asks for help (もらってくれませんか), the way loss is endured without being named.

Title Analysis

The Title: The Grammar of the Travelling Cat

The title contains its entire premise in five syllables.

What translation loses: The compound poetics of 旅猫

旅猫 (tabineko): "Travelling cat." The compound is simple — 旅 (tabi, journey) + 猫 (neko, cat) — but it carries the weight of Japanese literary tradition. The word 旅 (tabi) is not the casual travel of a vacation. It is the journey of the classical Japanese poetic imagination: the road that leads away from home, the michiyuki (道行き) of Noh drama and saigyō poetry, the wandering of Bashō. A 旅猫 is not merely a cat that rides in cars. It is a cat engaged in the specifically Japanese form of travel-as-self-discovery, where the road is not a means to an end but the story itself. The English "travelling cat" captures the literal meaning but loses the literary resonance. "Journeying cat" is closer but sounds archaic. "Road cat" sounds like a stray. The Japanese 旅猫 contains Bashō, Saigyō, and the entire tradition of Japanese road literature in two syllables.

リポート (ripōto): "Report." The English loanword makes the title into something between journalism and literature. A リポート is not a novel (小説, shōsetsu), not an essay (エッセイ), not a memoir (自伝). It is a report: factual, observational, delivered by someone who was there. By calling Nana's story a リポート, Arikawa gives the cat the authority of a journalist and the distance of a witness. Nana is not merely experiencing the journey; he is filing dispatches from it. The loanword also creates a charming tonal clash: the classical 旅猫 paired with the modern リポート, as if a Heian-period travelling poet had been given a press pass. This tonal clash is the key to the book's voice — Nana speaks with the archaic self-importance of a classical narrator and the casual irony of a contemporary observer. The grammar of the title makes the cat into a correspondent, and the journey into news that must be reported before it is too late.

Arikawa Hiro's career is a lesson in literary range. The woman who wrote military science fiction about censorship and armed library defense also wrote the gentlest novel about a cat's last journey.

What translation loses: The genre-hopping grammar of Arikawa's career

電撃ゲーム小説大賞 (Dengeki Gēmu Shōsetsu Taishō): "Dengeki Game Novel Prize." This is one of Japan's most prestigious awards for light novels (ライトノベル), the category of youth-oriented fiction that blends illustration, anime-style storytelling, and genre experimentation. Winning the Grand Prize with Shio no Machi (塩の街, "Salt Town"), a post-apocalyptic military SF novel, established Arikawa as a writer of serious genre fiction. The Library War series (2006–2008) confirmed her reputation: a dystopian romance about censorship and armed librarians that won the 39th Seiun Award (Japan's Hugo equivalent) and sold over a million copies. And then, from this writer of military SF and dystopian romance, came Tabineko Ripōto — a novel about a cat. The whiplash is deliberate. Arikawa has said in interviews that she wrote the novel after being deeply moved by her own experiences with cats, and the shift from battles to whiskers is not a departure but a deepening. The same narrative skills that made Library War thrilling — pacing, emotional withholding, the slow revelation of character through action — make Tabineko Ripōto devastating. The grammar of her career makes the cat novel into the logical destination of a writer who has spent her career asking what is worth protecting.

2019年、ペンネームを「有川浩」から「有川ひろ」に改める: "In 2019, changed pen name from 'Arikawa Hiro' (浩) to 'Arikawa Hiro' (ひろ)." This change is significant. The original 浩 (hiro) uses the kanji meaning "vast, great, mighty" — a strong, masculine-associated character. The new ひろ is written in hiragana, the syllabary used for grammatical particles and native Japanese words, which carries a softer, more gender-neutral, more approachable feeling. The change reflects Arikawa's evolution from a light novel writer in a male-dominated field to a general fiction author whose work appeals across demographics. The grammar of the name change is the grammar of a writer claiming her full range: she can write military SF and cat novels, and she does not need a "mighty" name to do either.

History

The Cat in Japanese Literature: From Sōseki to Nana

To understand Tabineko Ripōto, one must understand the lineage of the talking cat in Japanese literature, the boom in pet literature in the 2000s, and the specific cultural moment of 2011–2012 when the novel was written.

"Wagahai wa neko de aru. Namae wa mada nai."
"I am a cat. As yet I have no name."
— Natsume Sōseki, Wagahai wa Neko de Aru (1905), opening line

What translation loses: The Sōseki echo chamber

吾輩は猫である (Wagahai wa Neko de Aru): "I Am a Cat." Natsume Sōseki's 1905 novel is the most famous work of Japanese literature narrated by a cat, and Arikawa's opening — "吾輩は猫である。名前はまだない。昔の偉い猫は言った。" ("I am a cat. As yet I have no name. So said the great cat of old.") — is a direct, playful homage. But the homage is also a declaration of difference. Sōseki's cat is a philosopher, a detached observer of human folly, a Meiji-period intellectual who uses his feline perspective to satirize Japanese society's rush to modernize. Arikawa's Nana is something else entirely: not a philosopher but a roommate, not detached but deeply attached, not a satirist but a mourner. Where Sōseki's cat looks down on humans from the roof, Nana looks up at Satoru from the passenger seat of a silver station wagon. The grammar of the homage makes Nana into Sōseki's descendant, but the grammar of the story makes him into something Sōseki could not have imagined: a cat who loves a human so much that he would rather be homeless than be owned by anyone else.

The pet literature boom: The 2000s and 2010s saw an explosion of Japanese literature about cats, dogs, and the human-animal bond. Works like Hiraide Takashi's Neko no Kyaku (猫の客, "The Guest Cat," 2001), Itō Hideaki's Nana to Kurashite (ナナと暮らして), and dozens of " healing" (癒し系, iyashi-kei) novels about pets filled bookstore shelves. This boom reflected demographic shifts: Japan's aging population, declining birthrate, and increasing number of single-person households made pets into family members, companions, and grief objects. Tabineko Ripōto was written in the immediate aftermath of the March 11, 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami, a national trauma that made questions of loss, displacement, and the fragility of daily life urgently present. The novel's journey — a man and a cat driving through a landscape of memory, visiting old friends, preparing for an ending — resonates with the post-3.11 Japanese consciousness of impermanence. The grammar of the historical moment makes the cat novel into a national elegy, and Nana's voice into the voice of everyone who has ever had to say goodbye.

Structure

A Silver Station Wagon, Five Old Friends, and One Unspeakable Secret

Tabineko Ripōto is structured as a road novel with a frame narrative. The book opens with a Pre-Report ("Before We Set Out on Our Journey") in which Nana introduces himself and his relationship with Satoru. Then come four Reports, each centered on a visit to one of Satoru's friends from a different period of his life. Between the Reports, Nana's narration continues, deepening the reader's understanding of Satoru's secret. The novel closes with a Last-Report that delivers the emotional climax and the revelation that has been withheld throughout.

What translation loses: The chapter-naming grammar of the report

Report-01, Report-02, etc.: The chapter headings use the English loanword "Report" with a number, creating the effect of a filed dispatch, a travel log, or a scientific observation. But the content is deeply emotional, not scientific. This tension between the clinical form (report) and the sentimental content (a cat's love for his human) is the structural joke of the novel. Nana pretends to be objective — he calls himself "unparalleled in intelligence and beauty" — but he is writing an elegy. The grammar of the chapter names makes the novel into a series of official documents that record an unofficial love. The Pre-Report establishes the terms of the journey. The numbered Reports document each visit. Report-3.5 (最後の旅, "The Last Journey") breaks the numerical sequence to mark the turning point. And the Last-Report delivers the ending that the entire book has been withholding. The structure is not merely organizational; it is emotional architecture, with each Report adding a floor to the reader's understanding of who Satoru is and why he must give Nana away.

The Five Visits and What They Reveal

Each visit follows the same pattern: Satoru and Nana arrive, the friend is delighted to see Satoru, Nana evaluates the friend as a potential new owner, and through conversation and memory, a piece of Satoru's past is revealed. But the pattern is not repetitive; it is cumulative. Each visit adds a layer to the portrait of Satoru as a man who has been shaped by loss.

Report-01: Kōsuke (コースケ) — Satoru's elementary school friend, now the heir to a photo studio. Kōsuke's wife has left him, and his life is in disarray. Through Kōsuke's memories, we learn that Satoru lost his parents in sixth grade in a traffic accident and was raised by his aunt Noriko. We also learn about Hachi, the cat Satoru had as a child, and the origin of Satoru's belief that pets are family, not property. Kōsuke cannot take Nana; his life is too unstable. But the visit restores something in Kōsuke, a reminder of who he was before his marriage failed.

Report-02: Yoshimine (ヨシミネ) — Satoru's middle school friend, now a farmer. Yoshimine grew up in the chaos of his parents' divorce and was raised by his grandmother. His farm is remote, peaceful, and full of animals. He seems like an ideal candidate. But Yoshimine's life is too full, too rooted in his land and his work. He cannot take Nana either. Through Yoshimine, we learn about Satoru's adolescence: his kindness, his silence about his own pain, his capacity to make others feel seen.

Report-03: Sugi and Chikako (スギとチカコ) — Satoru's high school friends, now married and running a pet-friendly pension. They are the most obvious candidates: they love animals, they have space, they are stable and kind. But they have a dog, and Nana, being a proud cat with a tsundere personality, does not get along with dogs. The visit fails as an adoption, but it succeeds as revelation: through Sugi and Chikako, we learn about Satoru's young adulthood, his romantic failures, his decision not to pursue love because he knew something they did not.

Report-04: Noriko (ノリコ) — Satoru's aunt, the woman who raised him after his parents died. She is not a candidate for adoption; she is the destination. The visit to Noriko reveals what the reader has suspected: Satoru is dying. He has a terminal illness. The journey has never been about finding Nana a new owner. It has been about saying goodbye to the people who made Satoru's life bearable, and about finding Nana a home before Satoru can no longer care for him. Noriko takes Nana. The Last-Report is Nana's life after Satoru, a life of grief and memory and the slow, painful process of learning to love again.

The Unreliable Cat Narrator

Nana is an unreliable narrator in the most poignant sense: he does not know what Satoru knows. He suspects, he worries, he notices that Satoru is thinner, more tired, more tender than usual. But he does not understand the full truth until the end, and his gradual realization mirrors the reader's. This structural choice is brilliant: by filtering the story through a cat's limited understanding, Arikawa makes the reader feel the full force of the revelation when it comes. We have been laughing at Nana's arrogance, admiring his pride, feeling his love — and then we realize that every moment of the journey has been suffused with the knowledge of its ending. The grammar of the cat's perspective is the grammar of innocence confronting mortality.

Key Passages

These passages contain the novel's most precisely weighted sentences: the opening that echoes Sōseki, the request that launches the journey, the declaration of what was gained rather than lost, and the definition of family that the entire book exists to prove.

What translation loses: The pronoun shift from wagahai to boku

吾輩 (wagahai) → ボク (boku): The opening echoes Sōseki's famous first line using 吾輩, the pompous, archaic first-person pronoun that Sōseki's cat uses to establish his philosophical authority. But Nana immediately shifts to ボク (boku), the standard masculine first-person pronoun of a young man or boy. This shift is the key to Nana's voice. He is not Sōseki's philosopher-king. He is a contemporary guy who happens to be a cat. The ボク makes him approachable, casual, slightly immature — the perfect voice for a proud cat who thinks he's smarter than his human but loves him anyway. The English translation cannot reproduce this pronoun shift because English has only one "I." The reader of Japanese feels the tonal drop from classical grandeur to everyday masculinity in a single syllable, and that drop is the essence of Nana's character: he aspires to Sōseki's dignity but lives in the contemporary world of silver station wagons and convenience store stops.

類まれなる聡明さと美貌 (taguireru-naru sōmei-sa to bibō): "Unparalleled intelligence and beauty." The phrase 類まれなる is a literary adjectival form (連体形, rentaikei) of 類まれない, meaning "unparalleled, peerless, unprecedented." It is the kind of phrase found in classical literature, not in the self-description of a cat. Nana's use of it is absurd and endearing: he is a stray who has appointed himself aristocrat. The English "unparalleled" captures the meaning but loses the literary register. "Peerless" is closer but sounds like a knight. "Unprecedented intelligence and beauty" sounds like a press release. The Japanese phrase makes Nana sound like a Heian-period courtier describing himself in a diary, which is exactly the joke. He is a cat of impossible self-regard, and his language is the language of impossible self-regard.

What translation loses: The devastating politeness of もらってくれませんか

もらってくれませんか (moratte kuremasen ka): "Won't you take/receive [him]?" This is one of the most emotionally complex sentences in contemporary Japanese fiction, and it is devastating because of its politeness. The grammar combines もらう (to receive) with くれる (to give, directed toward the speaker) in the te-form, creating もらってくれる: "to give me the favor of receiving." The addition of ませんか makes it a polite request: "Won't you do me the favor of receiving?" The sentence is indirect, humble, and excruciating. Satoru is not saying "I need to give my cat away." He is saying "Would you do me the kindness of accepting him?" The grammar puts Satoru in the position of supplicant and the friend in the position of benefactor. It makes the giving of the cat into a gift that the receiver bestows on the giver. This is the opposite of how we usually think about pet surrender, which is framed as abandonment. Satoru's grammar reframes it as an act of grace that he is requesting. And because the reader knows what Satoru knows — that he is dying, that this is not a casual rehoming but a final arrangement — the politeness becomes almost unbearable. He is too gentle to say "I am dying and need to find my cat a home before I go." He says "Won't you take my cat?" and lets his friends fill in the rest.

What translation loses: The inversion grammar of 何も失わない

何も失わない (nani mo ushinawanai): "I lose nothing." The sentence is a radical inversion of the expected emotional response. When someone loses a home, a family, a person they love, we expect them to say they have lost everything. Nana says the opposite: he has lost nothing because what he had was a gift that he was never entitled to. The grammar of 何も失わない makes loss into a philosophical position. Nana is not denying his grief. He is reframing it: grief is the price of having had something worth losing, and he would rather have the grief than never have had Satoru. The English "I lose nothing" sounds like stoicism or denial. In Japanese, it sounds like wisdom — the wisdom of someone who has learned that love is not a debt to be collected but a grace to be grateful for. The particle も (mo) in 何も (nani mo) creates a total negation: "not one thing." The total negation of loss is the total affirmation of what was given.

得ただけ (eta dake): "Simply gained." The particle だけ (only, just) minimizes what Nana has received — "only the name Nana and five years" — but the context maximizes it. "Only five years" is an eternity when you are a cat. "Only the name Nana" is everything when you were born without one. The grammar of だけ creates the characteristic Japanese rhetorical move of humble magnification: by claiming to have received "only" something, the speaker makes that something appear infinitely precious. The English "simply gained" captures the minimization but loses the emotional expansion. "I only gained" sounds like regret. 得ただけ sounds like wonder.

Passage 4: The Definition of Family — The Grammar of What Pets Are

Report-01 (Kōsuke)
「ペットじゃないよ。ハチは家族だよ。」……「ハチは家族だ。家族なんだ。」
"Petto ja nai yo. Hachi wa kazoku da yo." ... "Hachi wa kazoku da. Kazoku na n da."
"He's not a pet. Hachi is family." ... "Hachi is family. Family."
— Young Satoru, Report-01

What translation loses: The repetition grammar of 家族なんだ

家族なんだ (kazoku na n da): "Is family." The repetition — "Hachi is family. Family." — is not mere emphasis. It is a child correcting an adult's category error. The first sentence (ペットじゃないよ。ハチは家族だよ) is a correction with the softening particle よ. The second sentence (ハチは家族だ。家族なんだ) removes the softening and adds the explanatory のだ (なんだ), which makes the statement into an explanation of a fundamental truth. The child Satoru is not merely disagreeing. He is revealing a truth about the world that adults have forgotten: the boundary between "pet" and "family" is a human invention, and children can see through it. The grammar of the repetition makes the second statement more certain than the first, as if Satoru is discovering the word "family" as he speaks it. The English translation can repeat "family" but cannot reproduce the grammatical intensification from だよ (gentle assertion) to なん (explanatory revelation). This small grammatical shift is the philosophical center of the entire novel: everything that follows exists to prove that Satoru was right when he said Hachi was family, and that Nana is family too.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Arikawa's world is built from words that carry the weight of literary tradition, the intimacy of the human-animal bond, and the unspeakable knowledge of endings.

1. Wagahai (吾輩): The Pronoun That Makes a Cat Aristocratic

What translation loses

吾輩 (wagahai): "I, me." This is one of the most famous first-person pronouns in Japanese literature, entirely because of Sōseki's cat. Before 1905, it was an archaic, pompous pronoun used by men of high status to refer to themselves with exaggerated humility that actually conveyed superiority. Sōseki's cat adopts it to establish his philosophical authority, and Arikawa's Nana adopts it to establish his literary pedigree. But Nana cannot sustain it. He drops 吾輩 after the opening sentence and reverts to ボク (boku), the pronoun of a contemporary young man. The drop is the joke: Nana aspires to Sōseki's grandeur but is too modern, too casual, too emotionally exposed to maintain the pose. English has no equivalent pronoun shift. "I" is "I" is "I." The reader of English knows that Nana is a cat; the reader of Japanese knows that Nana is a cat who has read Sōseki and cannot decide whether to be a philosopher or a boyfriend. The 吾輩 is a literary costume that Nana tries on and immediately discards, and that discarding is the essence of his character.

2. Kizuna (絆): The Bond That Is Not a Rope

What translation loses

絆 (kizuna): "Bond, tie, emotional connection." The word originally meant "hobble, tether, bond" — the physical rope that ties an animal's legs. Over centuries, it shifted to mean the emotional bond between people (or between people and animals). The original meaning is still present as a ghost: a 絆 is something that constrains as well as connects. When Arikawa writes about the 絆 between Satoru and Nana, she is invoking this double meaning: their bond is what makes them free and what will make their separation unbearable. The English "bond" captures the connection but loses the etymological shadow of the rope. "Tie" captures the constraint but sounds too physical. "Connection" is too neutral. The Japanese 絆 contains the paradox of love: it is what holds us together and what makes loss possible. Without the 絆, there would be no grief. With it, there is everything.

3. Tebanasu (手放す): The Verb That Means Both Release and Abandonment

What translation loses

手放す (tebanasu): "To let go of, to part with, to release from one's hands." The word combines 手 (te, hand) with 放す (hanasu, to release), creating an image of physically opening one's hands and letting something fall away. It is used for selling a house, giving up a hobby, and — crucially — surrendering a pet. The physicality of the word makes pet surrender into an embodied act: Satoru is not merely "giving Nana away." He is opening his hands and letting him go. The English "part with" is the closest equivalent but sounds archaic. "Let go" sounds like acceptance. "Give up" sounds like surrender. "Surrender" sounds like defeat. The Japanese 手放す contains all of these and none of them: it is simply the act of opening your hands, which is the most devastating thing a person who loves something can do. The grammar of the word makes the body responsible for the emotion.

4. Satō-oya (里親): The Word for the Person Who Becomes Family

What translation loses

里親 (sato-oya): "Adoptive parent, foster parent, new owner (of a pet)." The word combines 里 (sato, home, village, native place) with 親 (oya, parent), creating a compound that means "the parent who provides a home." It is used for pet adoption as well as child foster care, and this overlap is not accidental. In Japanese, the language of pet adoption borrows from the language of human family because the emotional structure is the same: someone who cannot keep a dependent finds someone who can, and the dependent's home is transferred from one set of hands to another. The English "new owner" sounds like property transfer. "Adoptive parent" sounds like human adoption. "Foster home" sounds like institutional care. The Japanese 里親 contains the village, the parent, and the act of taking someone in as a single concept. It is the word Satoru is looking for when he asks his friends もらってくれませんか, and it is the word that makes Nana's journey not a surrender but a search for a new 里 — a new village, a new home.

5. Yasashii (優しい): The Word That Contains All the World's Gentleness

What translation loses

優しい (yasashii): "Gentle, kind, tender, considerate, mild." This is the word most often used to describe Satoru, and it is impossible to translate with a single English equivalent. 優しい is not merely "kind." It is a quality of being that encompasses gentleness of manner, tenderness of heart, consideration for others, and a kind of emotional softness that makes people feel safe. A 優しい person does not raise their voice, does not impose, does not demand. They make space for others. They listen. They do not rush to judgment. Satoru's 優しさ is the reason Nana loves him, the reason his friends remember him, the reason his aunt raised him, and the reason he cannot bring himself to tell anyone he is dying. He is too 優しい to burden them. The English "gentle" captures the manner but not the moral quality. "Kind" captures the moral quality but not the texture. "Tender" sounds romantic. "Considerate" sounds calculating. The Japanese 優しい is a single word for a whole way of being in the world, and Satoru embodies it until it breaks your heart.

Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns from the language of proud cats, unspeakable requests, philosophical gains, and the definition of family.

〜てくれませんか (…te kuremasen ka)

"Won't you do [for me]...?" — The grammar of the devastatingly polite request. 僕の猫をもらってくれませんか (Won't you take my cat?). The te-form + くれる (to give, toward the speaker) + ませんか (polite negative question) creates a request so humble it sounds like a favor being asked rather than a pet being surrendered. N4–N3 level: combines benefactive くれる with polite invitation.

〜なくなる (…naku naru)

"To become unable to..." — The grammar of gradual incapacity. 飼えなくなっても (even if [he] becomes unable to keep me). The potential negative 飼えない + なる (to become) creates a euphemistic way of describing terminal illness without naming it. The grammar makes dying into a process of losing abilities one by one. N4 level.

〜だけ (…dake)

"Only, just" — The grammar of humble magnification. 五年を得ただけなんだ (I simply gained five years). The limiting particle だけ minimizes what was received, but the context makes the minimization into magnification. "Only five years" becomes "an eternity of grace." N5 level, but its rhetorical use is advanced.

〜なかったら (…nakattara)

"If [I] had not..." — The grammar of counterfactual gratitude. 出会わなかったら絶対に手に入らなかった (If I hadn't met him, I never could have gotten it). The conditional negative past + counterfactual result creates the grammatical structure of gratitude: the acknowledgment that what you have was contingent, unearned, and precious because it might not have happened. N4 level.

〜なんだ (…na n da)

"Is [emphatic explanatory]" — The grammar of revealed truth. 家族なんだ (Is family). The explanatory のだ (なんだ after a noun) makes a statement into a discovery, an explanation of a fundamental truth that the speaker has just recognized. N4 level, but its emotional force in this context is N2+.

〜も…ない (…mo…nai)

"Not even one..." — The grammar of total negation. 何も失わない (I lose nothing). The も particle after an interrogative (何, 誰, どこ) creates total negation when combined with a negative predicate. "Not one thing." The total negation of loss becomes the total affirmation of love. N4 level.

〜である (…de aru)

"Is [copula, formal/literary]" — The grammar of classical authority. 吾輩は猫である (I am a cat). The copula である is the formal, written-language equivalent of だ, used in literature, academic writing, and — crucially — the opening of Sōseki's novel. Nana's use of it is a costume he immediately removes. N4 level in form, N1 in literary resonance.

〜ても (…te mo)

"Even if..." — The grammar of concessive love. 飼えなくなっても、僕は何も失わない (Even if he can no longer keep me, I lose nothing). The te-form + も creates a concession: the first clause is admitted as true, and the second clause defies its expected consequence. Even if the worst happens, the grammar says, something remains. N4 level.
