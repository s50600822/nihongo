Silence
沈黙

A Japanese lesson through Endo Shusaku's most famous novel
The weight of silence · The swamp · The foot that tramples and is trampled

Published in 1966, Silence is Endo's masterpiece and one of the most important novels of the twentieth century. It tells the story of Father Rodrigues, a Portuguese Jesuit who travels to 17th-century Japan to find his mentor, Ferreira, who is rumored to have apostatized. Instead, Rodrigues finds a Japan that has systematically eradicated Christianity, and a God who seems to answer torture with nothing but silence. The novel asks: What does faith mean when God does not speak? What does apostasy mean when it is done to save others? And what kind of Christ can live in a country Endo calls a swamp?

1. The Title: 沈黙

Endo's title is not 静寂 (seijaku), the word for mere absence of noise. It is 沈黙 (chinmoku), a much heavier word.

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
沈黙chinmokusilence as a weight, a presence, a refusal to speak
静寂seijakustillness, quiet, absence of sound (neutral)
What translation loses: The sinking silence

沈 (chin) means to sink, to submerge, to be immersed in. 黙 (moku) means silence, muteness. Together, 沈黙 suggests a silence that is not empty but heavy, something that presses down on you like water. It is the silence of someone who could speak but chooses not to, or of a God who hears but does not answer. The English word "silence" can mean either "quiet" or "refusal to speak." 沈黙 leans decisively toward the latter. It is a willed, meaningful, almost aggressive silence.

In the novel, this silence belongs to God, to the inquisitors, to the martyred peasants, and finally to Rodrigues himself. When the title appears at the end of a chapter, it is not describing an acoustic condition. It is naming the central character of the book.

2. Japan as a Swamp

The most famous metaphor in the novel comes from the apostate Ferreira, explaining why Christianity failed in Japan.

「この国は沼だ。今までわしは、日本という国を沼とは考えなかった。今までわれわれが植えたものは、根を伸ばそうとしたが、やがて水に漬かって腐った」
"Kono kuni wa numa da. Ima made washi wa, Nihon to iu kuni wo numa to wa kangaenakatta. Ima made wareware ga ueta mono wa, ne wo nobasou to shita ga, yagate mizu ni tsukatte kusatta."
"This country is a swamp. Until now I had not thought of Japan as a swamp. Everything we planted tried to extend its roots, but soon it soaked in the water and rotted."
— Ferreira to Rodrigues

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
沼numaswamp, marsh, bog, morass
根neroot
伸ばすnobasuto extend, to stretch out (transitive)
漬かるtsukaruto be soaked in, to be immersed in
腐るkusaru / kusanuto rot, to decay, to decompose
What translation loses: The organic horror of 沼

沼 (numa): Not merely a swamp. A 沼 is stagnant water, shallow, filled with decaying vegetation, producing methane and bad air. It is the opposite of a river (河/川), which flows and renews itself. Ferreira is saying that Japan does not kill Christianity violently; it lets it sink into stagnant water and decompose from below. The word carries a sense of slow, organic, inevitable corruption. "Swamp" in English can be neutral (the Everglades) or political (drain the swamp). 沼 is simply rotten. It is nature in a state of unhealthy stasis.

腐った (kusatta): The plain past tense of 腐る. Ferreira uses no fancy theological language. He says the faith rotted. This is the language of a doctor describing gangrene, not a theologian describing heresy. The word is visceral and final. Something that rots does not merely die; it becomes unclean, unapproachable, something you do not touch.

The metaphor is devastating because Ferreira was once the most dedicated missionary. His vocabulary of rot is the vocabulary of a man who has watched something he loved die not by fire but by dampness.

3. The Warmth of the Heart

Rodrigues, watching the hidden Christians, comes to understand why the faith spread among peasants who had nothing.

我々の宗教がこの地方の農民に水の浸み入るように拡がっていったのは、ほかでもない、生れてはじめてこの連中が人の心のあたたかさを見たからです。人間として取り扱ってくれる者にあったからです
"Wareware no shuukyou ga kono chihou no noumin ni mizu no shimiiriru you ni hirogatte ita no wa, hoka demo nai, umarete hajimete kono renchuu ga hito no kokoro no atatakasa wo mita kara desu. Ningen to shite toriatsukatte kureru mono ni atta kara desu."
"The reason our religion spread among the peasants of this region like water soaking in was nothing other than this: for the first time in their lives, these people saw the warmth of the human heart. They met someone who treated them as human beings."
— Rodrigues's reflection

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
浸み入るshimiiriru / shimijimiruto soak in, to permeate, to penetrate gradually
拡がるhirogaruto spread, to extend (intransitive)
あたたかさatatakasawarmth (of heart, of feeling)
取り扱うtoriatsukauto treat, to handle, to deal with
What translation loses: Water soaking into dry ground

水の浸み入るように (mizu no shimiiriru you ni): This simile is almost too humble for the subject. Endo compares the spread of Christianity not to fire or wind or conquering armies, but to water soaking into soil. The grammar 〜ように makes it a simile of manner, not of appearance: it spread in the manner that water soaks in. This suggests something gradual, inevitable, and welcomed by the receiver. Dry ground does not resist water. These peasants did not resist the faith because it arrived not as doctrine but as 人の心のあたたかさ, the warmth of the human heart.

人の心のあたたかさ (hito no kokoro no atatakasa): The warmth of a person's heart. In English this sounds like a cliché. In Japanese, あたたかさ is a concrete physical word (warmth, as opposed to coldness) being used for emotional reality. The peasants had been treated as beasts of burden by the samurai class. When missionaries looked at them with kindness, they felt a warmth they had never known. The word suggests that Christianity spread in Japan not because of theology but because of something simpler and harder to sustain: human decency.

4. What Is Sin?

Rodrigues, watching guards laugh at the suffering of prisoners, has a sudden insight that redefines the novel's moral universe.

罪は、普通考えられるように、盗んだり、虚言をついたりすることではなかった。罪とは人がもう一人の人間の人生の上を通過しながら、自分がそこに残した痕跡を忘れることだった
"Tsumi wa, futsuu kangaerareru you ni, nusundari, kyogen wo tsuitari suru koto de wa nakatta. Tsumi to wa hito ga mou hitori no ningen no jinsei no ue wo tsuuka shinagara, jibun ga soko ni nokoshita konseki wo wasureru koto datta."
"Sin was not, as commonly thought, stealing or telling lies. Sin was passing over another person's life while forgetting the traces one left there."
— Rodrigues's interior monologue

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
罪tsumisin, guilt, crime (internal, moral)
虚言kyogenfalsehood, lie (literary/formal)
通過tsuukapassing through, transit
痕跡konsekitrace, mark, vestige
What translation loses: The geometry of sin

人生上を通過 (jinsei no ue wo tsuuka): To pass over a life. 通過 is a neutral word for transit, used for trains passing through stations without stopping. Rodrigues is saying that sin is treating other people's lives as places you pass through without stopping, leaving damage you do not remember and do not account for. The metaphor is spatial: a life as a landscape, and the sinner as a traveler who leaves footprints and forgets them. This is very different from the Western definition of sin as violation of divine law. Endo's definition is relational and Japanese: sin is failed memory, failed attention, the refusal to acknowledge that you have altered someone else's existence.

痕跡 (konseki): Not merely a "mark" or "trace." 痕跡 is a forensic word. It is what detectives look for at a crime scene. By using it for ordinary human interaction, Rodrigues is saying that every contact leaves evidence, and to forget that evidence is to commit a kind of spiritual vandalism. The word makes sin into something that could be photographed, measured, presented in court.

5. Ferreira's Despair

The moment Rodrigues has feared arrives: his former teacher, Ferreira, appears before him, not as a prisoner but as a Japanese man.

「わしが転んだのはな、いいか。聞きなさい。そのあとでここに入れられ耳にしたあの声に、神が何ひとつ、なさらなかったからだ。わしは必死で神に祈ったが、神は何もしなかったからだ」
"Washi ga koronda no wa na, ii ka. Kiki nasai. Sono ato de koko ni irerare mimi ni shita ano koe ni, kami ga nani hitotsu, nasaranakatta kara da. Washi wa hisshi de kami ni inotta ga, kami wa nani mo shinakatta kara da."
"I apostatized because, listen to me. After I was put in here and heard those voices, God did absolutely nothing. I prayed to God desperately, but God did nothing."
— Ferreira's confession

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
転ぶkorobu / korondato fall, to apostatize (in this context, to fall from faith)
必死hisshidesperate, with all one's might
なさるnasaruto do (honorific of する)
What translation loses: The honorific that accuses God

なさらなかった (nasaranakatta): This is the negative past of なさる, the honorific form of する (to do). Ferreira is a priest speaking about God. Even in his accusation, he uses honorific language. This creates a devastating tension: he is angry at God, he is saying God failed, and yet his grammar still bows. The honorific makes the accusation more personal, not less. It is the grammar of a son accusing a father, not a subject accusing a king. In English, "God did nothing" is flat and declarative. なさらなかった carries the weight of a relationship. Ferreira is not saying God is powerless. He is saying God chose not to act, and that choice is a betrayal.

転ぶ (korobu): The standard word for "to fall down." In Christian Japanese, it became the euphemism for apostasy: to fall from the faith. Ferreira uses this simple word instead of 棄教する (kikyousuru, "to apostatize formally"). He describes his apostasy not as a theological decision but as a physical collapse, like a man falling in the street. The word contains no dignity. It is the opposite of 殉教 (junkyou, martyrdom, "dying for the faith").

6. The Sound of the Sea

Throughout the novel, Rodrigues hears what he thinks is the sea, or the wind, or the cicadas. In fact, it is often the sound of Christians being tortured.

夜半、彼はうめき声で目を覚ました。それは海の音か、風の音かと思ったが、よく耳をすますと、それは人間の声であった
"Yonaka, kare wa umekigoe de me wo samashita. Sore wa umi no oto ka, kaze no oto ka to omotta ga, yoku mimi wo sumasu to, sore wa ningen no koe de atta."
"In the middle of the night he awoke to groaning. He thought it was the sound of the sea, or the wind, but when he listened carefully, it was a human voice."
— In the prison

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
夜半yonakamiddle of the night, midnight
うめき声umekigoegroan, moan (of pain, not pleasure)
耳をすますmimi wo sumasuto listen carefully, to prick up one's ears
What translation loses: The naturalization of torture

うめき声 (umekigoe): A groan of pain. The word うめく suggests an animal sound, something that escapes without words. It is the sound a wounded dog makes. By making Rodrigues mistake this for the sea or the wind, Endo accomplishes something terrible: he makes torture sound like nature. The groans become part of the landscape, like waves or wind, something you might not even notice if you were not paying attention. This is how the guards live with it. They have learned to hear うめき声 as background noise.

耳をすます (mimi wo sumasu): Literally "to clear one's ears." It means to listen with deliberate attention, to make an effort to hear what is not obvious. The phrase suggests that the truth is available to anyone who tries to hear it, but most people do not try. Rodrigues must actively clear his ears to recognize human suffering. Before he does, he hears only nature. This small phrase contains the novel's ethics: perception is a moral act. You must choose to hear.

7. The Fumie

The climax of the novel. Rodrigues is ordered to trample on the fumie, an image of Christ, to prove he has apostatized. He looks at the face of Christ, and the face seems to speak.

『踏むがいい。お前の足の痛さはこの私が一番よく知っている。踏むがいい。私はお前たちに踏まれるため、この世に生れ、お前たちの痛さを分つため十字架を背負ったのだ』
"Fumu ga ii. Omae no ashi no itasa wa kono watashi ga ichiban yoku shitte iru. Fumu ga ii. Watashi wa omaetachi ni fumareru tame, kono yo ni umare, omaetachi no itasa wo wakatsu tame juujika wo seotta no da."
"Trample! It was to be trampled on by you that I was born into this world. It was to share your pain that I bore the cross."
— The voice Rodrigues hears from the fumie

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
踏むfumuto step on, to trample
踏み絵fumiethe image one is ordered to trample (anti-Christian test)
痛さitasapain (nominalized from 痛い)
分つwakatsuto share, to divide, to partake of
背負うseouto bear on one's back, to carry (a burden)
What translation loses: The grammar of being-trampled

踏まれるため (fumareru tame): The passive form. "In order to be trampled." This is the theological shock of the passage. Christ says he was born not to conquer, not to judge, but to be trampled. The passive voice makes him into a victim by design, not by accident. In English, "to be trampled on by you" keeps the passive, but loses the deliberate grammatical construction 〜ため (in order to). In Japanese, Christ is saying his purpose, his telos, his reason for being born was to receive this specific violence from these specific people. It is not something that happens to him despite his mission. It is his mission.

痛さ (itasa): The nominalization of 痛い (painful). By turning the adjective into a noun, Endo makes pain into an object, a thing that can be known and shared. Christ says "your pain" (お前の足の痛さ) as if it were a measurable substance. And he claims to know it better than anyone. 痛さ is more intimate than 苦痛 (kutsuu, suffering); it is the pain of a specific foot on a specific face.

分つ (wakatsu): Usually means "to divide" or "to separate." Here it means "to share," as in dividing something so that each party has a portion. Christ says he bore the cross in order to 分つ (divide/share) their pain. The word suggests that pain is not eliminated but distributed. Christ does not take their pain away; he takes a share of it. This is the "God who suffers with" rather than the "God who rescues from." It is the central theological innovation of Endo's entire career.

Grammar: 〜がいい

Pattern: Verb + がいい = "You may as well [verb] / Go ahead and [verb]"

This pattern gives permission, but with a specific nuance: it is permission given when the speaker believes the action is inevitable or even desirable. When Christ says 踏むがいい, he is not merely saying "I allow you." He is saying "This is why I am here." The grammar of permission becomes the grammar of purpose. It transforms an act of apostasy into an act of communion.

8. The Rooster Crows

After Rodrigues apostatizes, the novel gives us a devastating echo of the Gospels. Kichijiro, the Judas figure, appears again.

鶏が鳴いた。キチジローは顔を上げた。そしてロドリゴを見た。その目には、涙が光っていた
"Niwatori ga naita. Kichijirou wa kao wo ageta. Soshite Rodorigo wo mita. Sono me ni wa, namida ga hikatte ita."
"The cock crowed. Kichijiro raised his face. And he looked at Rodrigues. In his eyes, tears shone."
— After the apostasy

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
鶏niwatorichicken, rooster, fowl
鳴くnakuto cry, to call, to crow (animal sound)
光るhikaruto shine, to gleam, to glisten
What translation loses: Peter and Judas in one gesture

鶏が鳴いた (niwatori ga naita): In the Gospels, the cock crows after Peter denies Christ three times. Here, it crows after Rodrigues apostatizes. But the person who hears it is not Rodrigues. It is Kichijiro, the serial apostate, the character who has betrayed Rodrigues repeatedly, the Judas figure. The tears in Kichijiro's eyes suggest that he recognizes Rodrigues as finally like himself: weak, broken, forgiven. The cock crow is for Kichijiro, not for the priest. It is the moment when the betrayer recognizes the betrayed as his brother in failure.

鳴く (naku): The word for animal cries. Crows 鳴く, dogs 鳴く, insects 鳴く. By using 鳴く instead of 啼く (the more dignified word for a rooster's crow), Endo keeps the sound in the realm of nature, of unglamorous farmyard fact. The cock does not make a triumphant clarion call. It simply 鳴くs, the way animals do. The mundanity of the word makes the biblical allusion more painful, not less.

9. Christ Died for the Miserable

One of the novel's most important theological passages comes early, when Rodrigues looks at the peasants and understands something about the nature of grace.

基督は美しいものや善いもののために死んだのではない。美しいものや善いもののために死ぬことはやさしいのだが、みじめなものや腐敗したものたちのために死ぬのはむつかしいと私はその時はっきりわかりました
"Kirisuto wa utsukushii mono ya yoi mono no tame ni shinda no de wa nai. Utsukushii mono ya yoi mono no tame ni shinu koto wa yasashii no da ga, mijime na mono ya fuhai shita monotachi no tame ni shinu no wa mutsukashii to watashi wa sono toki hakkiri wakarimashita."
"Christ did not die for the beautiful or the good. It is easy enough to die for the beautiful and the good; what is hard is to die for the miserable and corrupt. At that moment I understood this clearly."
— Rodrigues's reflection

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
みじめmijimemiserable, wretched, pitiful
腐敗fuhaicorruption, decay, rottenness
むつかしいmutsukashiidifficult, hard, complicated
はっきりhakkiriclearly, distinctly
What translation loses: The word for miserable

みじめ (mijime): This is a word of pity, not of condemnation. It describes someone who is so unfortunate that they evoke sympathy simply by existing. A beggar in rags is みじめ. A beaten dog is みじめ. It is different from 不幸 (fukou, unfortunate), which is factual. みじめ is emotional. When Rodrigues says Christ died for the みじめ, he is saying Christ died for those who make you uncomfortable with their need. The English "miserable" can mean "unhappy" or "contemptibly inadequate." みじめ cannot mean contemptible. It always carries compassion.

腐敗したものたち (fuhai shita monotachi): "Those who have rotted." Ferreira used 腐る for Christianity rotting in Japan. Here Rodrigues uses the same root for people. But the context transforms the word. Christ died for the rotten. Not despite their rottenness. For it. This is the scandal of Endo's theology, and it is stated in vocabulary that sounds medical rather than romantic. He does not say "sinners." He says "the rotten." The word refuses to beautify humanity. It insists that grace must be ugly enough to love actual people.

10. Kichijiro's Weakness

The most misunderstood character in the novel is Kichijiro, the Japanese guide who betrays Rodrigues, apostatizes repeatedly, and yet keeps returning to the faith. He speaks in the dialect of the weak.

「俺は生まれつき弱か。心の弱か者には、殉教さえできぬ。どうすればよか。ああ、なぜ、こげん世の中に俺は生まれあわせたか」
"Ore wa umaretsuki yowaka. Kokoro no yowaka mono ni wa, junkyou sae dekinu. Douse sureba yoka. Aa, naze, kogen yo no naka ni ore wa umareawaseta ka."
"I was born weak. Those with weak hearts cannot even become martyrs. What should I do? Ah, why was I born into such a world?"
— Kichijiro's confession

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
弱かyowakaweak (dialectal/older form of 弱い)
生まれつきumaretsukiby nature, from birth
殉教junkyoumartyrdom
こげんkogensuch (dialectal form of こんな)
生まれあわせたumareawasetawas born (humble/reflexive, dialectal)
What translation loses: The dialect of the unworthy

弱か (yowaka): An older/dialectal form of 弱い (weak). Kichijiro does not say 弱い. He says 弱か, which is humbler, more self-abasing. It is the speech of someone who has internalized his own inadequacy so deeply that his grammar has changed. The word sounds like a confession before it is a description. In English, "weak" is a statement of fact. 弱か is a statement of identity.

こげん (kogen): A dialectal form of こんな (such, like this). Kichijiro speaks in the Japanese of the rural poor, not the standard language of the priests. His dialect marks him as uneducated, marginal, unreliable. But it also gives his words an authenticity that Rodrigues's polished Latin prayers lack. When Kichijiro asks why he was born into こげん世の中, he is asking the novel's central question in the voice of those for whom the question is not theological but existential.

殉教さえできぬ (junkyou sae dekinu): "Cannot even become a martyr." The particle さえ means "even." Kichijiro's despair is precise: he is too weak for the highest form of Christian heroism. He cannot even manage the glorious death. His weakness excludes him from the one form of religious excellence that his culture recognizes. This is why he fascinates Endo, and why he is the secret hero of the novel: he represents the faith of those who cannot be heroes, which is, Endo suggests, almost everyone.

11. Summary: The Vocabulary of a Silent God

Key patterns

  • 〜がいい — permission that reveals purpose ("Go ahead and...")
  • Passive forms (〜られる) — victimhood as design, not accident
  • 〜ように — simile of manner, not appearance (soaking, not burning)
  • Honorifics in accusation (なさる) — anger that still bows

Words that carry the novel's weight

  • 沈黙 — silence as a presence, a weight, a refusal
  • 沼 — stagnant water that rots from below
  • 腐る — organic decay, not violent death
  • 罪 — not violation of law but failure of memory and attention
  • 痕跡 — forensic traces left on another's life
  • うめき声 — the groan that sounds like nature
  • 踏む — to trample; the central action of the novel
  • 痛さ — pain made into a shareable object
  • 分つ — to divide and share, not to eliminate
  • 弱か — weakness as identity, spoken in dialect
  • みじめ — the miserable who evoke pity merely by existing

The triptych complete

Where The Sea and Poison taught the vocabulary of moral failure (paralysis, poison, punishment, dryness), and Deep River taught the vocabulary of spiritual seeking (love, crossing, gathering, wrapping), Silence teaches the vocabulary of divine absence and unexpected presence. The same author, across three decades, uses the same physical, humble words to ask different questions: What does guilt feel like in a body? What does grace look like in a river? What does God sound like in silence?

In all three, the words that matter most are the small ones: 働き (working), 包む (wrapping), 麻痺 (paralysis), 踏む (trampling), 分つ (sharing), 沈黙 (silence). Endo's genius is to make these ordinary words bear the weight of theology. He writes as if he believes that Japanese, with its capacity for turning adjectives into nouns (痛さ), verbs into processes (浸み入る), and physical words into spiritual ones (沼), is a language uniquely suited to asking what it costs to love the weak.
