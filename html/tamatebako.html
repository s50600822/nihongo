Title Analysis

Title Grammar: 玉手箱

The title draws directly from the Urashima Taro folktale, where the tamatebako is the forbidden box given by Princess Otohime. Opening it releases white smoke and transforms Urashima into an old man. In Kotemari's hands, the box becomes a metaphor for a daughter whose inner world remains closed, and for the secrets women keep about their bodies, desires, and choices.

浦島太郎の玉手箱
Urashima Taro no Tamatebako
"The Jewel Casket of Urashima Taro"

A box given by Princess Otohime with the warning "never open it." When Urashima breaks the taboo, white smoke pours out and he ages instantly. The box contained his stolen time.

Title Breakdown

玉 tama "jewel, gem, sphere" — A prefix of beauty and preciousness. In classical Japanese, tama is an honorific prefix denoting something beautiful or precious.
手箱 tebako "hand box / casket" — A small traditional Japanese box for holding accessories, cosmetics, or valuables. Historically used by noblewomen to store combs and makeup.
玉手箱 tamatebako "jewel casket" — The compound fuses beauty (tama) with intimacy (tebako). It suggests something small, precious, and dangerously full of secrets.
Literary nuance: The title is written without any particles or verbs, creating a noun-phrase stillness that mirrors the sealed box itself. This is a classic Japanese titling strategy: the object stands alone as a symbol, inviting the reader to supply the action (open it? leave it closed?).

Title as Metaphor

In the book's title story, a woman who has suffered from infertility decides on surrogacy and finally obtains her "own" child. But the daughter's tamatebako — her inner self, her origins, her relationship to the woman who raised her — remains closed. The title thus operates on multiple levels:

  1. Folktale level: The forbidden box whose opening brings irreversible transformation
  2. Body level: The womb as a box that may or may not open, that may carry another's child
  3. Psychological level: The secrets we keep from those closest to us, and from ourselves
  4. Narrative level: Each story is itself a box the reader opens, releasing emotions the characters have contained
Author

小手鞠るい — Kotemari Rui

鞠

小手鞠るい

Kotemari Rui · b. 1956, Okayama

Kotemari Rui is a Japanese novelist, poet, and essayist who has lived in Woodstock, New York since 1992. She is one of Japan's most versatile contemporary writers, producing everything from adult romance novels to acclaimed children's books, while maintaining a poetic sensibility that runs through all her work.

  • 1956 Born in Bizen City, Okayama Prefecture. Birth name: Kawataki Kaori (川滝かおり).
  • ~1978 Graduated from Doshisha University, Faculty of Law. Worked as a publishing editor, cram school teacher, and bookstore clerk before becoming a freelance writer.
  • 1981 Won the 7th Sanrio "Shi to Meruhen" (Poetry and Fairy Tales) Prize. Published three poetry collections under her real name, Kawataki Kaori.
  • 1992 Moved to Woodstock, New York, where she has lived ever since.
  • 1993 Won the 12th Umigame New Literary Prize for Otogibanashi (おとぎ話 / "Fairy Tale"), a story about a Japanese woman's physical and emotional turmoil witnessing an American couple's divorce.
  • 1995 Published Tamatebako, her fiction debut as Kotemari Rui — a "re-departure" (sai-shuttatsu) as a novelist after beginning as a poet. The book was later described as a "phantom debut collection" (maboroshi no debut shosetsushu) after going out of print.
  • 2005 Won the 12th Shimase Love Literature Prize for Hoshii no wa, Anata Dake (欲しいのは、あなただけ / "All I Want Is You").
  • 2009 The picture book Ruu to Rinden — Tabi to Orusuban (ルウとリンデン 旅とおるすばん) won the Bologna International Children's Book Fair Prize.
  • 2019 Won the Shogakukan Children's Publishing Culture Prize for Aru Hareta Natsu no Asa (ある晴れた夏の朝 / "One Sunny Summer Morning").
Personal note: Kotemari has been married to Glenn Sullivan, an American, since her move to the United States. Her life straddling two cultures — the rural tranquility of Woodstock and the intense emotional landscapes of her Japanese fiction — gives her work a unique binocular vision. She has described her motivation simply: "I just love to write." This single feeling, she says, is the only thing she has, yet it is also what sets her free.
Contents

The Three Stories

Tamatebako is a short story collection containing three thematically linked works. Each explores the body, desire, and the gap between what women want and what society allows them to have.

Title Story

玉手箱 · Tamatebako

A woman tormented by infertility decides on surrogacy and finally obtains the child she has longed for. But her daughter's inner world — her "tamatebako" — remains closed. A story of maternal love, bodily alienation, and the question of what makes a mother a mother.

Themes: infertility, surrogacy, motherhood, bodily autonomy

Award-Winning

おとぎ話 · Otogibanashi

Winner of the 12th Umigame New Literary Prize. A Japanese woman in America witnesses an American couple's divorce and finds herself caught in the physical and emotional turmoil of their separation. Love and sexuality are depicted with unflinching, sensational honesty.

Themes: divorce, sexuality, cross-cultural encounter, bodily desire

Third Story

卵を忘れたカナリア · Tamago wo Wasureta Kanaria

"The Canary Who Forgot Her Eggs." A story about a woman who wants a child, capturing the trembling, contradictory emotions of maternal desire. The canary — a fragile bird traditionally used in coal mines to detect danger — becomes a metaphor for female vulnerability and reproductive anxiety.

Themes: childlessness, reproductive longing, female fragility, hope

Story

Synopsis

玉手箱 · Tamatebako

A woman has suffered deeply from infertility. After much anguish, she decides on surrogacy and finally obtains the child she has so desperately wanted. But the daughter's tamatebako — her inner world, her genetic origins, her relationship to the woman who carries and raises her — remains closed. The story traces the emotional entanglement of love and possession, asking: What makes a mother? Is it blood, gestation, or the act of raising? The "box" of the title is both the daughter's inaccessible psyche and the womb that could not, and then could, produce a child.

おとぎ話 · Otogibanashi (Fairy Tale)

A Japanese woman living in America becomes entangled in the divorce of an American couple she knows. The story depicts the raw physical and emotional chaos of marital dissolution with a directness that earned it the Umigame New Literary Prize. Love, sex, jealousy, and the body are laid bare without romantic varnish. The title is deeply ironic: there is nothing fairy-tale-like about the pain of separation, yet the phrase also hints at the archetypal nature of the drama — every divorce is both utterly unique and a story as old as marriage itself.

卵を忘れたカナリア · Tamago wo Wasureta Kanaria

A canary that has forgotten her eggs. The title evokes a bird that has lost its reproductive instinct, or perhaps a woman who fears she has forgotten how to become a mother. Canaries were historically used in coal mines to detect toxic gas — their fragility made them early warning systems. In this story, the canary becomes a symbol for the fragility of female reproductive hope, and for the way society places the burden of "warning" (of biological clocks, of declining fertility) on women's bodies.

Collection arc: Read together, the three stories form a triptych of female bodily experience: the body that cannot conceive (Tamatebako), the body caught in the crossfire of others' desires (Otogibanashi), and the body that fears it has forgotten its reproductive purpose (Tamago wo Wasureta Kanaria). Kotemari's poetic background is evident in the way each title is a compressed image rather than a declarative statement.
Text

Key Passages

These representative passages capture the book's tone, thematic concerns, and linguistic features. Note: The original text is not widely excerpted online; the passages below are reconstructed from the book's synopsis language and Kotemari's known stylistic tendencies, based on publisher descriptions and critical reception.

01
The Closed Box
不妊に苦しみ、代理出産を決意して得たわが子。だけど娘の玉手箱は閉じたままだった……。
Funin ni kurushimi, dairi-shussan wo ketsui shite eta wa ga ko. Dakedo musume no tamatebako wa tojita mama datta…….
"A child obtained after suffering from infertility and resolving upon surrogacy. But the daughter's jewel casket remained closed……"

Nuance Note

This is the book's core sentence, appearing in publisher descriptions. The phrase wa ga ko (わが子, "one's own child") carries immense weight in Japanese: wa is an archaic/literary first-person possessive, elevating the emotional stakes beyond casual watashi no ko. The ellipsis at the end is characteristic of Japanese book-copy style, inviting the reader to imagine what remains unsaid. The juxtaposition of eta (得た, obtained/acquired) with wa ga ko (one's own child) creates a subtle tension: is this child truly "obtained," or truly "one's own"?

02
Love's Entanglement
愛の葛藤を描き高い評価を得た初期傑作。
Ai no kattou wo egaki takai hyouka wo eta shoki kessaku.
"An early masterpiece that depicts the conflicted entanglements of love and won high acclaim."

Nuance Note

Kattou (葛藤) is stronger than "conflict" or "struggle." It implies vines tangled together, mutually constraining — an image of love as something that binds both parties inextricably. Shoki kessaku (初期傑作, "early masterpiece") is critical vocabulary indicating a work of mature artistry from the very beginning of a career. The sentence structure, with the verb egaku (描く, to depict) preceding the result takai hyouka wo eta (won high praise), is typical of Japanese promotional copy, building toward the evaluative climax.

03
Sensational Honesty
愛と性をセンセショナルに描いた「海燕」新人文学賞受賞作「おとぎ話」。
Ai to sei wo senseshonaru ni egaita "Umigame" Shinjin Bungaku-shou jushou-saku "Otogibanashi."
"Otogibanashi, winner of the Umigame New Literary Prize, sensationally depicts love and sexuality."

Nuance Note

The word senseshonaru ni (センセーショナルに, "sensationally") is a loanword deployed here with precision. In Japanese literary discourse, it signals that the work deals with sex and the body without euphemism or modesty — a departure from traditional Japanese reticence about female desire. The juxtaposition of this word with otogibanashi (おとぎ話, "fairy tale") creates a deliberate friction: fairy tales are supposed to be innocent, yet this one is sensational. The effect is subversive.

04
The Forgotten Eggs
子を望む女性の揺れる気持ちを描いた「卵を忘れたカナリア」。
Ko wo nozomu josei no yureru kimochi wo egaita "Tamago wo Wasureta Kanaria."
"The Canary Who Forgot Her Eggs, depicting the trembling emotions of a woman who desires a child."

Nuance Note

Yureru (揺れる) means "to sway, tremble, waver." Applied to kimochi (気持ち, feelings), it captures not indecision but the physical sensation of emotion in flux — like a tree in wind. The canary (kanaria) is a Western-imported symbol in Japan, associated with fragility, song, and (through the mining context) vulnerability to invisible danger. Tamago wo wasureru (卵を忘れた, "forgot her eggs") is an almost absurdly delicate way to describe infertility anxiety: not "cannot conceive" but "has forgotten" — as if motherhood were a memory that might return.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loses

Kotemari's prose is poetic, allusive, and emotionally compressed. Much of its power depends on cultural and literary references that do not travel easily into English.

1. Wa ga Ko (わが子) The Poetic Possessive

JP 代理出産を決意して得たわが子
EN "the child she obtained through surrogacy"
Loss Wa ga (わが) is an archaic/literary first-person possessive, closer to "my very own" than neutral watashi no. It carries the weight of poetry and prayer. A mother calling her child wa ga ko is making a claim that transcends biology — it is the voice of the Manyoshu, of classical waka, of maternal love as an almost sacred bond. English "her child" flattens this into mere possession.

2. Tamatebako as Cultural shorthand

JP 娘の玉手箱は閉じたままだった
EN "Her daughter's jewel casket remained closed"
Loss For Japanese readers, tamatebako instantly evokes the Urashima Taro folktale: the box that must not be opened, the secret that transforms everything once revealed. The English "jewel casket" sounds decorative; it lacks the taboo charge. A Japanese reader understands that the daughter's inner world is not merely "closed" but dangerously closed — that opening it would change the mother-daughter relationship irreversibly, just as opening Urashima's box changed him into an old man.

3. Otogibanashi (おとぎ話) as Irony

JP 愛と性をセンセーショナルに描いた「おとぎ話」
EN "A 'fairy tale' that sensationally depicts love and sexuality"
Loss Otogibanashi carries the warmth of childhood innocence. Applying it to a story of divorce, adulterous desire, and bodily turmoil is a calculated irony. The English "fairy tale" has been so overused by Disney that it has lost some of its innocence; in Japanese, otogibanashi still retains the flavor of the otogizoshi tradition — medieval illustrated storybooks for children. The gap between that innocence and the story's adult content is the point.

4. Kattou (葛藤) — Entangled Vines

JP 愛の葛藤を描き
EN "depicting the conflicts of love"
Loss Kattou is written with the characters for kuzu (葛, arrowroot vine) and fuji (藤, wisteria vine) — both climbing plants that tangle around whatever they touch. The word thus encodes a specific image: love not as battle (sentou) but as entanglement, two lives twined together so thoroughly that separation damages both. English "conflict" or "struggle" loses this botanical, organic quality.

5. Maboroshi no Debut Shosetsushu (幻のデビュー小説集)

JP 幻のデビュー小説集
EN "The legendary phantom debut short-story collection"
Loss Maboroshi (幻) means phantom, illusion, mirage — something that almost seems not to have existed because it was so hard to find. In Japanese publishing discourse, maboroshi no is a term of art for out-of-print works that acquire legendary status among collectors and devoted readers. The English "phantom" or "legendary" captures some of this, but misses the specific affect of Japanese book-collecting culture, where finding a maboroshi no book is a triumph.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns drawn from the book's synopsis language and thematic context, useful for intermediate to advanced learners.

〜に苦しむ N2

Ni kurushimu: to suffer from (a condition, emotion, or situation).

不妊に苦しみ……

"Suffering from infertility……" — Kurushimu implies ongoing, bodily suffering rather than temporary distress.

〜を決意して N2

Wo ketsui shite: having resolved to, having made up one's mind to.

代理出産を決意して得たわが子

"A child obtained after resolving upon surrogacy" — Ketsui (決意) is stronger than kimeru; it implies a solemn, life-altering decision.

〜たまま（継続） N4

Ta mama: remaining in a state; having done X and stayed that way.

閉じたままだった

"Remained closed" — The continuative mama emphasizes that the state of being closed has persisted over time, with a hint of regret or tension.

〜を描く N3-Literary

Wo egaku: to depict, portray, render (in art or literature).

愛の葛藤を描き高い評価を得た

"Depicted the entanglements of love and won high praise" — Egaku is the standard literary verb for what writers and painters do; it implies craft and intentionality.

〜を得る N3

Wo eru: to obtain, gain, acquire (slightly formal/literary).

高い評価を得た初期傑作

"An early masterpiece that obtained high praise" — Eru is more elevated than morau or te ni ireru; it suggests something earned or achieved.

〜に（様態・程度） N3

Ni as an adverbial marker: in a X manner, to a X degree.

センセーショナルに描いた

"Depicted sensationally" — The adverbial ni turns the loanword senseshonaru into a manner adverb describing how the depiction was done.

〜の（連体修飾） N5-N1

The attributive particle no, here in literary compound formation.

幻のデビュー小説集

"The phantom debut short-story collection" — Maboroshi no is a set literary phrase; the no links the abstract noun to the concrete one it modifies.

〜を望む N2

Wo nozomu: to desire, hope for, long for.

子を望む女の揺れる気持ち

"The trembling emotions of a woman who desires a child" — Nozomu is more elevated than hoshii; it carries a sense of yearning toward something that may be difficult to attain.

Study tip: Kotemari's prose, shaped by her background in poetry, uses elevated diction (wa ga, eru, nozomu) alongside modern grammatical structures. Reading her work trains sensitivity to register shifts in Japanese — the difference between poetic and prosaic ways of saying the same thing. Compare watashi no ko (my child, casual), watashi no kodomo (my child, neutral), and wa ga ko (my child, literary/poetic) to feel the gradations.
