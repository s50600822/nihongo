Grammar: どう is an interrogative adverb directed at the goat, asking for an evaluation of the gift: "How is it?" Its use with an animal is anthropomorphizing, treating the goat as a capable evaluator. どうぞ is the standard Japanese offering phrase: "Here you go; please [take it]." Its repetition (どうぞ。どうぞ。) creates an encouraging, insistent tone, as the vlogger tries to persuade the goat to accept the gift. うわあ is an exclamation of surprise at the goat's reaction, likely at the goat's unexpected behavior (perhaps running away or showing intense interest). サビちゃん uses the affectionate suffix ちゃん attached to the goat's name, a standard marker of intimacy in Japanese pet discourse. ちょっと、ちょっと待って uses the te-form imperative 待って (wait) with the diminutive ちょっと (a moment) repeated: "Wait, wait a moment." The repetition adds urgency and a sense of managing an unruly or excited animal. 行くぞ uses the assertive sentence-ending particle ぞ attached to 行く (to go): "Here we go; let's go." ぞ is a masculine, forceful particle that creates momentum and determination, appropriate for launching an action. なんか止まってしまった uses the colloquial pronoun なんか (somehow; something) + 止まる (to stop) + てしまった (regrettably ended up): "Somehow [it] stopped [moving]." The てしまう auxiliary adds a nuance of unintended result, as if the goat's stopping was an unexpected or slightly problematic outcome. いいね uses the adjective いい (good) + agreement-seeking particle ね: "Nice, right?" This is directed at the goat or the camera audience, framing the moment as a shared positive experience. 美味しいか uses the adjective 美味しい (delicious) + question particle か: "Is it delicious?" The direct question to the goat is another anthropomorphizing device, common in pet vlogs where animals are treated as conversational partners.

Grammar: タンポポがほらいっぱい uses the attention-directing interjection ほら (look; see) embedded in a noun phrase: "dandelions, look, [there are] lots." ほら is commonly used to draw attention to something visible, and its placement after the noun creates a pointing gesture in speech. 近い is a standalone adjective comment, likely noting that the dandelions are growing close to something (the goat, the house, or the path). 魔法を使ってこなくなります uses the pattern [verb te-form] + [negative stem] + なる (to become): "[I] will stop coming by using magic." This is a playful, nonsensical statement, likely referring to some pest (insects, perhaps) that the vlogger jokingly claims will be deterred by "magic." The grammar of [te-form] + [negative] + なる expresses a change of state from doing to not doing something. だいぶ入ったで uses the Kansai sentence-ending で (from だ) after the past verb 入った (went in): "[It] went in quite a bit, [didn't it]." The ておく auxiliary expresses doing something in preparation for future convenience, and its contraction to とく is standard in casual speech. Grammar: けよ is a dialectal/colloquial imperative form, possibly a variant of こう (let's do it) or a regional command form. This level of dialectal contraction (これ → け) is characteristic of very casual Kansai or rural speech. これが目に入らんのか uses the set phrase 目に入る (to enter one's eyes = to be visible; to notice) + Kansai negative 入らん (iran, from 入らない) + explanatory question のか: "Does this not enter [your] eyes? = Can't you see this?" The のか turns it into a rhetorical or genuine question of disbelief, expressing frustration that the goat (or someone) is not noticing something obvious. ほら、嬉しいな uses the attention-directing ほら + adjective 嬉しい (happy) + exclamatory な: "Look, [how] happy." The repetition with different endings creates a crescendo of realization, as the vlogger notices something unexpected moving.

Grammar: やった is the canonical success exclamation: "[I] did it." Its use here marks a small victory in the ongoing gift-giving process. え、これでも潰れそうじゃない? uses the interjection え (huh) + これでも (even this) + conjectural 潰れそう (seems [it will] get crushed) + negative tag じゃない: "Huh, this might get crushed too, mightn't it?" The でも (even; also) adds the sense that even this [gift/container] might be at risk, and the tag じゃない turns the statement into a worried question seeking confirmation. やばい is the ubiquitous Japanese slang exclamation for danger, trouble, or surprise: "[That's] bad; yikes." 外れたで uses the past verb 外れた (came off; came loose; missed [the mark]) + Kansai sentence-ending で: "[It] came off, [didn't it]." The で replaces standard だ/よ and adds a confirming, slightly exasperated tone. ちゃんとできてなかった uses the adverb ちゃんと (properly; correctly) + negative past progressive できてなかった (had not been able to do properly): "[I] didn't do [it] properly." The progressive negative frames the inadequacy as a state that persisted during the action. 違うだろ repeated three times uses the verb 違う (to differ; to be wrong) + presumptive/tag auxiliary だろ: "[That's] wrong, [isn't it]." The triple repetition creates a crescendo of correction or denial, directed at the goat or the situation. だろ is a masculine, assertive tag that seeks agreement while stating certainty. ブラシ行っても uses the concessive ても (even if) after the verb 行く (to go): "Even if [I] go [get] the brush..." The quotative って marks this as reported speech or observation, suggesting the goat (or the vlogger interpreting the goat's behavior) has indicated a preference for the human thigh over the brush.
