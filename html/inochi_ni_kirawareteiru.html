Title Analysis

Title Grammar: 命に嫌われている。

The title Inochi ni Kirawarete iru. (命に嫌われている。) is a study in Japanese passive psychology. At first glance, it appears to be a simple passive construction: "Life hates us" / "We are hated by life." But the grammar encodes a specifically Japanese way of experiencing the world — one in which suffering is not something we actively confront but something that happens to us, something we endure, something we are submerged in. The passive voice (-rare / 〜られ) transforms the speaker from an agent into a patient, from someone who acts upon the world into someone who is acted upon by it. The title does not say "I hate life" (active, individual, accusatory) or "Life is cruel" (objective, detached, philosophical). It says "We are hated by life" — a formulation that makes life the subject and the speaker its object, that distributes agency to the universe and leaves the human being in a position of radical passivity. This is the grammar of a generation that grew up with the sense that the world was already decided before they were born, that the economy, the climate, the social expectations were structures within which they must live but which they did not choose. The title is not a complaint; it is a diagnosis. And the period (。) at the end — the Japanese full stop maru (丸) — turns the title into a complete sentence, a statement of fact, an irrefutable condition. There is no question mark, no exclamation point, no appeal. Just the fact: we are hated by life. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese quality of the passive as a mode of existential description — the way that ni kirawarete iru (に嫌われている) makes being-hated-by-life not an event but a state, not a moment but a permanent condition.

Title Breakdown

命 inochi "life" — The word inochi (命) is one of the most emotionally charged in Japanese. Unlike seimei (生命), the biological term for "life" (as in "the phenomenon of living organisms"), or jinsei (人生), the biographical term for "one's life" (as in "my life story"), inochi refers to life as a precious, fragile, almost sacred substance — the thing that can be lost, saved, risked, or wasted. Inochi is the word used when a mother says she would give her inochi for her child; it is the word used when a soldier is told his inochi belongs to the emperor; it is the word used when a doctor says a patient's inochi cannot be saved. The choice of inochi over seimei or jinsei is crucial: Kanzaki is not talking about biological existence or personal biography but about life as a force that possesses us, that we are entrusted with, that can be taken away. The title makes this inochi — this precious, entrusted thing — into the agent of hatred. Life, which should be the thing we cherish most, is the thing that hates us. This is the central paradox of the song, and it is encoded in the very first word. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese emotional register of inochi as something that is given, entrusted, and can be lost — a concept closer to the English "soul" than to the English "life," though neither translation is adequate. The English "life" is accurate but flat; inochi carries the weight of everything that makes life worth living and everything that makes its loss unbearable.
に嫌われている ni kirawarete iru "is hated by / are hated by" — The construction ni kirawarete iru (に嫌われている) is the progressive-passive form of the verb kirau (嫌う, "to dislike / to hate / to detest"). The particle ni (に) marks the agent of the passive — the thing doing the hating — and the verb form kirawarete iru (嫌われている) combines the passive suffix -rare (〜られ) with the continuative -te iru (〜ている), creating a state of ongoing, continuous being-hated. This is not "Life hated us once" (a past event) or "Life will hate us" (a future prediction); it is "We are in a state of being hated by life" — a permanent, unchanging condition. The -te iru form is doing crucial work here: it transforms the passive from an event into a state, from something that happens into something that simply is. The Japanese speaker who hears ni kirawarete iru understands immediately that this is not a complaint about a specific injustice but a description of an existential condition. The English "hated by" can capture the passive but loses the progressive-state aspect; "is hating us" captures the ongoingness but loses the passive. What is lost is the specifically Japanese grammar of suffering-as-state — the way that -te iru makes being-hated-by-life into something like the weather, something you don't choose and can't change, something you simply exist within. The title is not an accusation; it is a weather report from the inside of despair.
。 maru The period (。) — The Japanese full stop maru (丸, literally "circle") at the end of the title transforms it from a phrase into a sentence, from a description into a declaration. In Japanese typography, the maru is a small circle (。) rather than a dot (.), and its visual roundness gives it a quality of finality that the Western period lacks — a sense of completion, of enclosure, of the sentence having come full circle. The title with a maru is not asking a question, not making an appeal, not expressing surprise. It is stating a fact. The maru says: this is how things are. Deal with it. In a song about the suffocating pressure of "positive" messages ("Don't say you want to die," "Don't give up, keep living"), the maru is the grammatical equivalent of the song's defiant tone: it refuses to plead, refuses to apologize, refuses to soften its claim with a question mark or an exclamation point. The statement is what it is. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese visual and emotional weight of the maru as a mark of quiet, absolute finality — the small circle that closes the sentence like a lid on a coffin, like the period at the end of a life.
Official English title: "Hated by Life Itself" — The official English title adds "Itself" to specify that it is life-in-itself, life as a force or principle, that does the hating. The Japanese title leaves this implicit: inochi (命) is both "life" and "Life" — the individual life and the principle of life combined in a single word. The English "Itself" makes explicit what Japanese leaves resonantly ambiguous.
Author / Composer

カンザキイオリ — Kanzaki Iori (1997–)

神

カンザキイオリ / Kanzaki Iori

Vocaloid Producer · Composer · Lyricist · Novelist · Born March 11, 1997, Iwate Prefecture

Kanzaki Iori was born on March 11, 1997, in Iwate Prefecture — the rural, snow-bound northeastern region of Japan's main island, a place of harsh winters and stark beauty, of depopulation and quiet despair. He debuted as a Vocaloid producer in January 2014 under the name Kurogaki (黒柿, "Black Persimmon"), a name that evokes both the dark wood of the persimmon tree and the bitter fruit that must ripen before it can be eaten. In 2015, he changed his name to Kanzaki Iori (カンザキイオリ) — a name whose kanji (神崎 伊織) suggests "divine cape" and "woven thread," as if his art were a weaving of threads at the edge of the sacred. The name change marked a shift from amateur experimentation to professional ambition: the boy who had made songs in his bedroom was becoming the artist who would speak for a generation.

In 2017, at the age of 20, Kanzaki moved from Iwate to Tokyo — the classic trajectory of the Japanese artist from the provinces, the journey from the periphery to the center that has defined Japanese cultural history from Bashō to Murakami. It was in the immediate aftermath of this move, living alone in the vast anonymity of the capital, that he wrote "Inochi ni Kirawarete iru." In his own words (from the liner notes to his album Hakushi / 白紙, 2019): "This song was made when I had just come to Tokyo from my hometown, in a half-runaway kind of way, with 'my own view of life and death' as the theme." The song was written, he later revealed, for "someone I didn't want to die" — a confession that transforms the song from a work of nihilism into a work of desperate love, from a song about death into a song about the impossibility of letting someone go.

Kanzaki's artistic identity is defined by a triple vocation: Vocaloid producer, composer for virtual singers, and novelist. Since 2018, he has been affiliated with KAMITSUBAKI STUDIO, for which he composes all original songs for the virtual singer Kafu (花譜) — one of the most successful virtual-idol projects in contemporary Japan. His work with Kafu extends the aesthetic world of "Inochi ni Kirawarete iru." into a sustained meditation on the relationship between voice, identity, and technology: what does it mean to sing through a synthesized voice? What does it mean to feel emotion for a singer who does not exist in flesh? The Vocaloid voice — Hatsune Miku's, Kafu's — becomes in Kanzaki's work a metaphor for the voiceless-ness of the young generation, the sense that their words are spoken through technologies they did not create, in a world they did not choose.

As a novelist, Kanzaki has published Kemono (獣, 2020), Ano Natsu ga Hōwa suru. (あの夏が飽和する。, 2020 — a revised and retitled version of Kemono that became a bestseller with over 110,000 copies sold), and in June 2026, a novel titled Inochi ni Kirawarete iru. (命に嫌われている。, Kawade Shobo Shinsha, 192 pages, ISBN 9784309032603) — a literary expansion of the song's themes into prose narrative. His total book sales exceed 480,000 copies. The novel's subtitle — "A story of life and death based on truth, confronting a question to which the answer is still not visible" — suggests that Kanzaki has spent nearly a decade since the song's release continuing to interrogate the same question: why is life so hard, and why do we keep living it anyway?

Kanzaki Iori's significance extends beyond his individual works. He is, in many ways, the defining artistic voice of Japan's " Reiwa-era youth" — the generation that came of age after the 2011 triple disaster (earthquake, tsunami, nuclear meltdown on March 11, 2011 — Kanzaki's 14th birthday), that entered adulthood during the COVID-19 pandemic, that faces a future of climate crisis, economic precarity, and social isolation. His work speaks to a generation that has been told, from every direction, to "keep living" (ikiro / 生きろ) — by parents, by teachers, by pop songs, by government campaigns — and has found in that imperative not comfort but pressure, not hope but coercion. "Inochi ni Kirawarete iru." is the song that names this pressure and refuses it, that says what no one is supposed to say: that the command to live can feel like hatred, that life itself can feel like an adversary, that the bravest thing is not to pretend everything is fine but to admit that it isn't — and to keep living anyway.

History

Vocaloid, Youth Suicide, and the Culture of "Keep Living"

The Vocaloid Revolution: Voice Without a Body

Vocaloid is a singing voice synthesis software developed by Yamaha and first released in 2004. The most famous Vocaloid character is Hatsune Miku (初音ミク, "First Sound of the Future"), developed by Crypton Future Media and released in 2007. Miku is not a person; she is a piece of software, a voicebank, a character design — a turquoise-haired, twin-tailed avatar who exists only on screens and stages (where she appears as a hologram projection). And yet, she is also the most successful "singer" in contemporary Japan, with millions of fans worldwide, sold-out concerts, and a vast ecosystem of producers, illustrators, and cover artists who create music "for" her.

The significance of Vocaloid for Japanese youth culture cannot be overstated. For a generation that grew up with the internet, with social media, with the sense that their own voices were drowned out by the noise of the digital world, Vocaloid offered something unprecedented: the ability to make a "professional" song without being a professional singer, without a record label, without any of the gatekeepers that had controlled Japanese music for decades. A teenager in a rural town could download Vocaloid software, write lyrics on their phone, compose a melody on a cheap keyboard, and upload the result to Niconico or YouTube — and if the song was good, millions would listen. The voice that sang was not their own; it was Miku's, or Rin's, or Len's, or IA's. But the words, the melody, the emotion — those were entirely theirs.

Kanzaki Iori's choice to have Hatsune Miku sing "Inochi ni Kirawarete iru." is artistically crucial. Miku's voice — synthesized, slightly metallic, capable of expressing emotion without the "authenticity" of a human singer — becomes the perfect vehicle for a song about the voicelessness of the young generation. When Miku sings "We are hated by life," she sings it not as a person who has suffered but as a voice that exists precisely because someone needed to speak and had no other way to do so. The Vocaloid voice is the voice of the generation that has been told to be quiet, to endure, to keep living without complaining — and that has found, in technology, a way to scream. The fact that the song was later covered by human singers (Mafumafu, Kafu, Kobasolo, majiko, and dozens of others) does not diminish the power of the original Vocaloid version; it confirms it. The song was so true that even human voices wanted to sing it.

Youth Suicide and the Pressure to "Keep Living"

Japan has one of the highest suicide rates among developed nations, and youth suicide has been a particular concern. In the years leading up to 2017, Japan saw a series of high-profile youth suicides that were linked to bullying (ijime / いじめ), academic pressure, and social media harassment. The government's response has typically been to promote "suicide prevention" campaigns that emphasize the value of life, the importance of talking to someone, and the message that "things will get better." Pop culture has echoed this message: countless Japanese songs, dramas, and anime tell young people to "not give up," to "keep living," to "believe in tomorrow."

"Inochi ni Kirawarete iru." opens with a direct attack on this culture of compulsory positivity. The first lines "Shinitai nante iu na yo" "Akirame na de ikiro yo" / Sonna uta ga tadashii nante bakageteru yo na (「死にたいなんて言うなよ」「諦めないで生きろよ」／そんな歌が正しいなんて馬鹿げてるよな, "'Don't say you want to die' 'Don't give up, keep living' / The idea that songs like that are correct is absurd") — name the exact rhetoric that young Japanese people hear every day and that the song will spend the next four minutes dismantling. Kanzaki is not saying that life is not worth living; he is saying that the command to keep living, delivered without understanding, without acknowledging the reality of suffering, is itself a form of violence. The person who says "don't give up" without knowing why someone wants to give up is not helping; they are adding to the pressure. The song's genius is to recognize that the well-meaning imperative to live can feel, to someone who is suffering, like hatred — like life itself reaching out to crush them with its demands.

The song also engages with a specific Japanese cultural context: the phenomenon of "suicide contagion" (shussan / 自殺の連鎖) through media. The lyrics describe a chain of events: someone dies, someone sings about it, a boy is influenced by the song and runs with a knife. This is a reference to real concerns in Japan about the relationship between media coverage of suicide and copycat behavior. But Kanzaki's treatment is not moralistic; it is descriptive. He is not saying "don't sing about suicide because it causes copycats." He is saying: "We are already singing about it. We are already influenced by it. The question is not whether to speak but how to speak — whether our words add to the pressure or relieve it." The song itself, by naming the problem with such precision, becomes a form of relief: it says what the listener has been unable to say, and in saying it, creates a space where the unspeakable can be spoken.

From Niconico to Kohaku: The Song's Trajectory

"Inochi ni Kirawarete iru." was first posted to Niconico on August 6, 2017, and to YouTube on September 30, 2017. On Niconico, it achieved "Vocaloid Hall of Fame" (VOCALOID dendōiri / VOCALOID殿堂入り) status — awarded when a Vocaloid song reaches 100,000 views — marking Kanzaki's first entry into this prestigious category. The song's success on Niconico was driven by the platform's unique culture: users could leave "comment subtitles" (danmaku / 弾幕, literally "bullet curtain") that scrolled across the video in real time, creating a collective viewing experience in which thousands of listeners shared their reactions simultaneously. For a song about isolation and voicelessness, the danmaku became a paradoxical form of community: strangers typing "I feel the same" and "This saved me" and "I'm crying" across the screen, creating a visible record of shared suffering that transformed the song from a solitary experience into a collective one.

The song's reach expanded dramatically when it was covered by utaite (歌い手, "cover singers") — amateur and semi-professional singers who record their own versions of Vocaloid songs and post them online. The most famous cover is by Mafumafu (まふまふ), whose version has accumulated over 51 million views on YouTube. Mafumafu's cover transformed the song from a niche Vocaloid hit into a mainstream phenomenon, introducing it to audiences who had never heard of Vocaloid and who encountered the lyrics not as a synthesized voice but as a human one. On December 31, 2021, Mafumafu performed the song on NHK's Kōhaku Uta Gassen (紅白歌合戦, "Red and White Song Battle") — the most prestigious music program in Japan, broadcast annually on New Year's Eve since 1951. The performance, with Mafumafu singing in Hatsune Miku's key with a high, androgynous voice, was one of the most talked-about moments of the 72nd Kohaku. A Vocaloid song about wanting to die, performed on the nation's most celebratory broadcast, in the voice of a singer who had himself been open about his mental health struggles: this was the moment when "Inochi ni Kirawarete iru." ceased to be a subcultural phenomenon and became a national anthem of sorts — the song that Japan needed to hear but had been afraid to sing.

Structure

Lyrics & Song Structure

The Architecture of Despair

The song follows a structure of escalating intensity: it begins with a quiet, almost spoken-word reflection on the hypocrisy of "positive" messages, builds through a series of increasingly specific observations about contemporary Japanese life, explodes in a chorus that names the central paradox ("We are hated by life"), and resolves — if it can be called a resolution — in a final section that refuses easy comfort while insisting on the necessity of continuing. The structure mirrors the experience of depression: the gradual accumulation of observations that something is wrong, the sudden crystallization of the feeling into a name, and the long, uncertain aftermath in which one must decide, moment by moment, whether to continue.

Verse 1: The song opens with the rejection of compulsory positivity. The speaker hears the messages — "Don't say you want to die," "Don't give up, keep living" — and recognizes them as absurd (bakageteru / 馬鹿げてる). The insight that follows is devastating in its honesty: the speaker admits that they don't actually care if they die, that what they care about is the sadness of those around them, and that this "caring" is itself a form of ego — "because I wouldn't like it." The verse then broadens to a social critique: we don't care if strangers live, hating someone is just fashion, and yet we say "let's live in peace" as if it were a lovely thing. The tone is cynical, detached, analytical — the voice of someone who has thought too much about things that are not supposed to be thought about.

Pre-chorus: The song shifts from social observation to media critique. "On the other side of the screen, someone is dying / Someone sings about it in grief / A boy, influenced by it, runs with a knife." This is the chain of media influence: death → song → imitation → more death. The observation is not moralistic; it is descriptive. The screen (gamen / 画面) the smartphone, the computer, the television — is the medium through which we experience other people's deaths, and through which our own deaths may be experienced by others. The boy with the knife is not condemned; he is simply the next link in the chain. The pre-chorus creates a sense of inevitability: this is how things are, this is how they will continue to be, unless something changes.

Chorus: The chorus — Bokura wa inochi ni kirawarete iru (僕らは命に嫌われている, "We are hated by life") — is the song's thesis and its emotional peak. It repeats three times, each with slight variation, creating a mantra-like effect. The first iteration names the condition; the second adds the specific accusation ("We easily broadcast songs that could kill someone over the airwaves"); the third adds the self-accusation ("We who lightly say we want to die, who lightly look at life — we are hated by life"). The chorus transforms the individual despair of the verses into a collective condition: it is not "I" who am hated by life but "we" (bokura / 僕ら). This shift from first-person singular to first-person plural is crucial: it creates community in suffering, transforming isolation into solidarity. The listener who feels alone in their despair hears "we" and understands that they are not alone — that the song itself is proof that someone else has felt this way, has thought these thoughts, has found the words to say what they could not.

Verse 2: The second verse descends into the specifics of a life without meaning. "With no money, today too I while away the day in lazy sleep / Unable to find meaning in living, aware of the waste, I breathe." This is the voice of the NEET (Not in Education, Employment, or Training), the hikikomori (引きこもり, social recluse), the young Japanese person who cannot find a place in the economy and has retreated into a life of apparent idleness. The speaker's question — can the word "lonely" (sabishii / 寂しい) even express this wound? — acknowledges the inadequacy of language, the way that ordinary words fail to capture extraordinary pain. The verse ends with the image of the speaker going to sleep alone, carrying only their stubbornness (iji / 意地) — the refusal to give up, or perhaps the refusal to admit defeat, that keeps them alive.

Bridge: The bridge offers the song's only moment of temporal hope, and even this is laced with mortality. "We who were boys will someday become young men / Growing old, someday, like withered leaves, we will rot away unknown to anyone." The passage of time is inevitable; aging is inevitable; death is inevitable. The "immortal body" (fujimi no karada / 不死身の身体) that the speaker imagines obtaining is a fantasy — the fantasy of the young person who cannot imagine their own death, or the fantasy of the depressed person who wishes they were already dead and therefore, in a sense, immortal. The bridge does not promise that things will get better; it promises only that things will change, that the boy will become a young man, that the young man will become an old man, that the old man will die. This is not optimism; it is fatalism. But fatalism, in Kanzaki's hands, becomes a strange kind of hope: if things will change, then the present suffering is not eternal. If we will age, then we must first live. If we will die, then we are still alive.

Final Chorus & Outro: The final section of the song is its most controversial and most misunderstood. After repeating the chorus one last time, the song shifts to a series of imperatives: "Since someday we will die / Since someday we will return to nothing / Why don't we struggle to the end / Why don't we struggle to stay alive." These lines have been read as a conventional "don't give up" message — the very message the song seemed to be rejecting. But the context transforms their meaning. The speaker is not saying "life is beautiful, so keep living"; they are saying "since we will die anyway, since we will return to nothing, we might as well struggle." The "struggle" (agaku / 足掻く) is not joyful; it is desperate, painful, undignified — the word literally means "to flounder, to struggle, to writhe." The song ends not with a promise of happiness but with a command to continue the struggle, not because life is good but because death is final and we are, for now, still alive. This is the song's radical honesty: it refuses to lie about the difficulty of living, and it refuses to give up on living anyway.

Text

Key Passages

These passages capture the philosophical precision, emotional devastation, and grammatical subtlety of Kanzaki Iori's lyrics — a song that speaks for a generation that has been told to keep living without being told why.

01
The Opening Refusal
「死にたいなんて言うなよ」
「諦めないで生きろよ」
そんな歌が正しいなんて馬鹿げてるよな
"Shinitai nante iu na yo"
"Akirame na de ikiro yo"
Sonna uta ga tadashii nante bakageteru yo na
"'Don't say you want to die' / 'Don't give up, keep living' / The idea that songs like that are correct is absurd."

Nuance Note

This opening is a declaration of war against the culture of compulsory positivity. The two quoted phrases — shinitai nante iu na yo (死にたいなんて言うなよ, "Don't say you want to die") and akirame na de ikiro yo (諦めないで生きろよ, "Don't give up, keep living") — are not invented by Kanzaki; they are the exact phrases that young Japanese people hear from parents, teachers, counselors, and pop songs. The quotation marks (「」) frame them as reported speech, as things that are said to the speaker rather than by the speaker. The imperative iu na (言うな, "don't say") is a command to silence; the imperative ikiro (生きろ, "live") is a command to continue. Both are delivered without asking why the person wants to die or why they want to give up. The final line — sonna uta ga tadashii nante bakageteru yo na (そんな歌が正しいなんて馬鹿げてるよな) — uses tadashii (正しい, "correct / right / proper") rather than yoi (良い, "good") or suteki (素敵, "wonderful"), which transforms the critique from aesthetic to moral: the problem is not that these songs are bad art but that they are wrong, incorrect, based on a false understanding of what suffering is. Bakageteru (馬鹿げてる, "absurd / ridiculous / foolish") is a strong word — the verb form of baka (馬鹿, "fool") — and its use here is deliberately provocative: the speaker is calling the dominant cultural message foolish, and by extension, calling those who deliver it fools. The final particle yo na (よな) softens the assertion slightly, turning it from a declaration into a shared observation: "it's absurd, isn't it?" — an appeal to the listener's own experience of having heard these messages and found them hollow. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese quality of ikiro as an imperative that carries the full weight of social expectation — the command to live not as a choice but as a duty, not as a desire but as an obligation. The English "keep living" captures the meaning but loses the imperative force; "live on" captures the continuation but loses the command. The Japanese ikiro is what a parent says to a child, what a teacher says to a student, what a society says to its young: live, because we demand it.

02
The Ego of Living
実際自分は死んでもよくて
周りが死んだら悲しくて
「それが嫌だから」っていうエゴなんです
"Jissai jibun wa shinde mo yokute
Mawari ga shindara kanashikute
'Sore ga iya da kara' tte iu ego nan desu"
"Honestly, I don't care if I die / But I'd be sad if the people around me died / It's an ego that says 'because I wouldn't like that.'"

Nuance Note

This is the song's most devastating insight, and it is delivered with the calm precision of a philosophical argument. The speaker begins with a confession that violates every social taboo: jissai jibun wa shinde mo yokute (実際自分は死んでもよくて, "honestly, I don't care if I die / actually, it would be fine if I died"). The phrase shinde mo ii (死んでもいい, "it's okay to die") is the exact opposite of the cultural imperative ikite mo ii (生きていい, "it's okay to live"); it is the unspeakable made speakable. The te mo ii (てもいい) construction — "it's okay even if..." — is a permission-granting form, and its use here is shocking: the speaker is granting themselves permission to die, as if the only thing preventing death were social convention rather than personal desire. The second line — mawari ga shindara kanashikute (周りが死んだら悲しくて, "but I'd be sad if the people around me died") — introduces the twist: the speaker does care about death, but only the death of others. The word mawari (周り, "surroundings / people around") is deliberately impersonal — not "my family" or "my friends" but "the people around me," as if the speaker's social world were a geographical feature rather than a network of relationships. The final line delivers the philosophical payload: "sore ga iya da kara" tte iu ego nan desu (「それが嫌だから」っていうエゴなんです, "It's an ego that says 'because I wouldn't like that'"). The word ego (エゴ, from English "ego" but used in Japanese with the full Freudian/Nietzschean weight of self-interest, self-preservation, selfishness) transforms the conventional "reason to live" into a form of self-interest: I don't kill myself not because life is worth living but because I wouldn't like the sadness of others' deaths. The nan desu (なんです) ending — the explanatory/emphatic copula — gives the statement the tone of a revelation, a discovery that the speaker has just made and is sharing with the listener. What is lost in English is the devastating honesty of this sequence: in three lines, Kanzaki dismantles the entire edifice of "reasons to live" and reveals that the most common reason — not wanting to hurt others — is itself a form of ego. This is not nihilism; it is radical honesty. And radical honesty, in a culture of compulsory positivity, is itself a form of resistance.

03
The Chorus
僕らは命に嫌われている
価値観もエゴも押し付けて
いつも誰かを殺したい歌を
簡単に電波で流した
"Bokura wa inochi ni kirawarete iru
Kachikan mo ego mo oshitsukete
Itsu mo dareka wo koroshitai uta wo
Kantan ni denpa de nagashita"
"We are hated by life / Imposing our values and our ego / We easily broadcast over the airwaves / Songs that could kill someone."

Nuance Note

The chorus is the song's thesis, and every word has been chosen for maximum weight. Bokura (僕ら, "we") — the first-person plural pronoun — transforms the song from a personal confession into a collective accusation. The speaker is not saying "I am hated by life" but "we are hated by life," implicating the listener, the culture, the generation. Bokura is the informal masculine "we" (as opposed to the more formal watashitachi / 私たち), and its use creates a sense of solidarity among the young, the informal, the not-yet-fully-adult. The phrase kachikan mo ego mo oshitsukete (価値観もエゴも押し付けて, "imposing our values and our ego") uses oshitsukeru (押し付ける, "to force upon / to impose"), a verb of violence: we do not share our values; we force them on others. The word kachikan (価値観, "values / value system") is a modern, almost sociological term — the kind of word used in discussions of cultural difference and generational conflict — and its use here elevates the personal to the structural: the problem is not individual meanness but a whole system of values that demands conformity. Itsu mo dareka wo koroshitai uta (いつも誰かを殺したい歌, "songs that could kill someone / songs that want to kill someone") is deliberately ambiguous: koroshitai (殺したい) can mean "wanting to kill" (the songs want to kill) or "capable of killing" (the songs can kill). The ambiguity is the point: the songs that tell us to keep living are, in their pressure, in their refusal to acknowledge suffering, potentially lethal. The final line — kantan ni denpa de nagashita (簡単に電波で流した, "we easily broadcast over the airwaves") — uses denpa (電波, "radio waves / airwaves"), a word that evokes both the physical medium of broadcast and the metaphor of transmission: our values, our ego, our songs travel through the air, invisible, omnipresent, impossible to escape. Kantan ni (簡単に, "easily / simply") is the accusation: we do this without thinking, without effort, as if it were nothing. What is lost in English is the full weight of oshitsukeru as a verb of physical force — the image of pressing something onto someone, of holding them down and forcing them to accept what they do not want. The English "impose" captures the meaning but loses the physicality; "force upon" captures the violence but loses the everyday quality of the act. The Japanese oshitsukeru is what a parent does when they make a child eat something they don't want, what a teacher does when they make a student conform, what a society does when it makes its young live by its values: it presses, it forces, it leaves no room for refusal.

Nuance Note

The final section of the song is its most complex and most often misunderstood. It begins with a statement of mortality — itsuka shinde shimau kara (いつか死んでしまうから, "since someday we will die") — that functions not as despair but as a premise. The shimau (しまう) auxiliary verb adds a sense of finality and inevitability: death is not something we choose but something that will happen to us, something that will be completed without our consent. The second line — itsuka chippoke na hone to hai ni modoru kara (いつかちっぽけな骨と灰に戻るから, "since someday we will return to tiny bones and ash") — uses chippoke na (ちっぽけな, "tiny / insignificant / puny"), a diminutive adjective that carries both tenderness and contempt: our bones are tiny, our ash is nothing, and yet this is what we will become. The word modoru (戻る, "to return") suggests that bones and ash are our original state, that life is a temporary departure from nothingness and death is a return to it. The crucial turn comes in the third line: saigo made mo agaki agaite ikiyou (最後までもがき足掻いて生きよう, "let's struggle and flounder to the end and live"). The verb agaku (足掻く, "to struggle / to flounder / to writhe") is the emotional center of the entire song. Agaku is not a noble struggle; it is the desperate, undignified movement of a drowning person, of an insect on its back, of someone who cannot escape but refuses to stop moving. The reduplication mo agaki agaite (もがき足掻いて) intensifies the image: struggle upon struggle, flounder upon flounder, a continuous, exhausting effort. And the volitional form ikiyou (生きよう, "let's live") transforms this struggle into a choice — not "we must live" but "let's live," a proposal, an invitation, a shared decision. The final line — saigo made inochi ni agaite iyou (最後まで命に足掻いていよう, "let's struggle against life to the very end") — is the song's ultimate paradox: we struggle not for life but against it, not to embrace existence but to resist its hatred. The particle ni (に) marks inochi as the object of struggle — we struggle against life, we flounder in the face of life, we refuse to let life's hatred defeat us. What is lost in English is the full visceral quality of agaku — a word that evokes not heroism but desperation, not triumph but endurance. The English "struggle" is too dignified; "flounder" is too aquatic; "writhe" is too physical. The Japanese agaku contains all of these and something more: the image of a creature that cannot win but will not surrender, that has no hope of victory but continues to move because stopping would be a kind of death before death. This is the song's final message: not "life is beautiful" but "we are still alive, and while we are alive, we will struggle." Not because struggle is noble, but because the alternative — surrender to life's hatred — is unbearable. The song ends not with a promise but with a posture: the posture of the drowning person who keeps moving their arms, not because they believe they will be saved, but because stopping is not yet possible.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Kanzaki Iori's lyrics are a masterclass in the Japanese language's capacity for emotional precision. Every word has been chosen to carry maximum weight with maximum economy, and many of these choices are impossible to fully reproduce in English.

1. Inochi (命) vs. "Life"

JP 命に嫌われている
EN "Hated by Life" / "Hated by Life Itself"
Loss Japanese has at least three common words for "life": inochi (命), seimei (生命), and jinsei (人生). Seimei is the biological term — the phenomenon of living organisms, the opposite of death. Jinsei is the biographical term — one's life story, the narrative arc from birth to death. Inochi is something else entirely: it is life as a precious substance, as something entrusted to us, as something that can be lost, saved, risked, or wasted. A mother gives her inochi for her child; a doctor saves a patient's inochi; a soldier risks his inochi for his country. Inochi is not merely existence; it is the quality of existence that makes it worth preserving. The choice of inochi over seimei or jinsei is the song's foundational decision: it makes "life" not a biological fact or a narrative structure but a force that possesses us, that we are responsible for, that can turn against us. The English "Life" cannot capture this range. "Life Itself" (the official translation) comes closer by suggesting life as a principle or force, but it loses the emotional intimacy of inochi — the sense that this is not an abstract concept but the very thing that makes each breath meaningful. The Japanese listener who hears inochi feels the weight of everything that word has meant in their culture — parental sacrifice, medical emergency, religious reverence — before they hear a single note of the melody. The English listener sees a one-syllable word and must build its resonance from context.

2. Ni Kirawarete Iru (に嫌われている) as Existential Weather

JP 命に嫌われている
EN "hated by life" / "life hates us"
Loss The construction ni kirawarete iru (に嫌われている) is the progressive-passive form of kirau (嫌う), and it encodes a specifically Japanese way of experiencing adversity. The passive voice (-rare / 〜られ) removes the speaker from the position of agent: life is not something we engage with actively but something that happens to us. The progressive aspect (-te iru / 〜ている) transforms the passive from an event into a state: we are not "hated" at a particular moment but in a continuous, ongoing condition of being-hated. The particle ni (に) marks the agent of the passive — the thing doing the hating — and in this case, that agent is inochi, life itself. The result is a grammatical structure that says: we exist in a state of being hated by the very thing that makes existence possible. This is not "life is difficult" (a statement about conditions) or "I hate my life" (a statement about feeling) but "we are the object of life's hatred" (a statement about our existential position). English "hated by" captures the passive but loses the progressive-state aspect; "life hates us" captures the ongoingness but loses the passive and makes the speaker an equal participant in a relationship rather than a patient in a condition. What is lost is the specifically Japanese grammar of suffering as a weather system — something you don't choose, can't control, and must simply exist within. The Japanese speaker who hears ni kirawarete iru understands immediately that this is not a complaint about a specific injustice but a description of an atmospheric condition, a climate of the soul.

3. Ikiro (生きろ) as Imperative Violence

JP 諦めないで生きろよ
EN "Don't give up, keep living" / "live on"
Loss The imperative ikiro (生きろ) is the raw, unsoftened command form of ikiru (生きる, "to live"). In Japanese, imperatives can be softened with various suffixes (-nasai / 〜なさい for polite command, -te / 〜て for request), but ikiro is the plain imperative — the form a parent uses with a child, a commander with a soldier, a society with its young. It is not "please live" or "I hope you live" or "it would be good if you lived"; it is "LIVE" — a command that brooks no refusal. The final particle yo (よ) adds emphasis and a sense of shared understanding: "Live, okay?" — as if the speaker and listener both know that living is not easy but that the command must be given anyway. The song's critique is directed precisely at this imperative: the culture that commands young people to live without asking why they want to die, that delivers ikiro as a slogan rather than an invitation. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese cultural weight of ikiro as a social command. In Japan, ikiro is what you see on suicide prevention posters, what counselors say to at-risk youth, what pop songs shout at their listeners. It is the word of the ikigai (生き甲斐, "reason for living") industry, the wellness culture, the compulsory positivity machine. The English "keep living" or "live on" captures the meaning but loses the imperative force and the cultural specificity. "Live!" with an exclamation point comes closer to the command but adds an enthusiasm that the Japanese ikiro yo does not have. The Japanese imperative is flat, exhausted, delivered from one tired person to another — the command of a society that has run out of reasons but not of expectations.

4. Agaku (足掻く) as Undignified Struggle

JP 最後までもがき足掻いて生きよう
EN "let's struggle and flounder to the end and live"
Loss The verb agaku (足掻く) is one of the most viscerally specific words in Japanese, and it has no exact English equivalent. Agaku means to struggle desperately, to flounder, to writhe — specifically, the kind of struggle that is undignified, unbeautiful, and probably futile. It is what a drowning person does, what a fish does on land, what an insect does when it is on its back. It is not the noble struggle of the hero (tatakau / 戦う); it is the desperate movement of the creature that cannot escape but refuses to stop moving. The reduplication mo agaki agaite (もがき足掻いて) — combining mogaku (もがく, "to struggle / to squirm") and agaku (足掻く) — creates an image of continuous, exhausting, repetitive effort: struggle upon struggle, flounder upon flounder, with no end in sight. The song's final imperative — saigo made mo agaki agaite ikiyou (最後までもがき足掻いて生きよう, "let's struggle and flounder to the end and live") — is not a call to heroic resistance; it is an acknowledgment that living is, for some people, a continuous process of undignified struggle, and that the only choice is whether to continue that struggle or to stop. What is lost in English is the full physicality of agaku — the image of a body in distress, moving not from strength but from refusal to surrender. English "struggle" is too dignified, too heroic; "flounder" is too aquatic and comical; "writhe" is too physical and sexual. None of these captures the specific quality of agaku as the movement of a being that is losing but has not yet lost. The Japanese listener who hears agaku feels in their body the image of the drowning person, the insect on its back — and understands that the song is saying: this is what living feels like, for some of us. Not climbing a mountain. Not sailing toward a horizon. Just moving your arms and legs because stopping is not yet possible.

5. Bokura (僕ら) as the Voice of a Generation

JP 僕らは命に嫌われている
EN "We are hated by life"
Loss The pronoun bokura (僕ら) is the informal, masculine first-person plural — "we" as spoken by a young man to his peers. It is softer than ore-ra (俺ら, rough masculine "we") and less formal than watashitachi (私たち, neutral/formal "we"). Bokura carries connotations of youth, of male friendship, of the "we" that excludes adults and includes only those who are still figuring things out. The choice of bokura over watashitachi is crucial: it makes the "we" of the song specifically generational and specifically gendered (though the song's appeal transcends gender). Bokura is the "we" of the young Japanese man who feels the pressure of expectations — to succeed, to conform, to keep living — and who finds in the song a voice for what he cannot say. But bokura is also inclusive in its own way: it is the "we" of shared experience, of "we who grew up with the internet," "we who came of age after 3/11," "we who have been told to keep living without being told why." The English "we" cannot capture this specificity. "We" is universal; bokura is particular. The Japanese listener who hears bokura knows immediately that this is a song for the young, for the informal, for those who exist in the space between childhood and full adulthood — the space where the pressure is greatest and the reasons are fewest. What is lost in English is the generational identification encoded in a single pronoun — the way that bokura says, in one syllable, "this is for us, not for them."
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns from the language of existential crisis, compulsory positivity, and the struggle to live in a world that demands your survival without offering reasons.

〜に〜られている（受け身＋継続） N4

...ni...rarete iru: passive + progressive — "is/are being [verb]ed by..." — transforms an action into a continuous state.

命に嫌われている

"We are hated by life" — not a momentary event but a permanent existential condition.

〜なんて言うなよ（禁止＋軽視） N4

...nante iu na yo: prohibitive imperative + nante (dismissive) + yo (emphasis) — "Don't say things like..."

死にたいなんて言うなよ

"Don't say things like you want to die" — nante dismisses the content; na commands silence; yo insists on shared understanding.

〜なんて馬鹿げている（軽視＋状態） N3

...nante bakagete iru: nante (dismissive) + bakagete iru (progressive of "to be absurd") — "the idea that... is absurd."

そんな歌が正しいなんて馬鹿げてるよな

"The idea that songs like that are correct is absurd, isn't it?" — yo na softens the assertion into a shared observation.

〜てもよい（許可・可能性） N5

...te mo ii: "it's okay even if... / it's fine to..." — permission-granting construction that can be used for shocking admissions.

自分は死んでもよくて

"I don't care if I die / It would be fine if I died" — the permission-granting form used to express indifference to one's own death.

〜っていうエゴなんです（引用＋説明） N4

...tte iu ego nan desu: quotative tte + ego + explanatory nan desu — "It's an ego that says '...'"

「それが嫌だから」っていうエゴなんです

"It's an ego that says 'because I wouldn't like that'" — nan desu gives the statement the tone of a philosophical revelation.

〜て流した（他動詞＋完了） N4

...te nagashita: -te continuative + nagashita (past of "to broadcast / to flow") — "broadcast [something] by [doing]."

簡単に電波で流した

"We easily broadcast over the airwaves" — kantan ni (easily) modifies the whole action, adding the accusation of thoughtlessness.

〜が〜て（原因・理由） N4

...ga...te: subject + ga + -te form — causal connection between events, often with emotional weight.

周りが死んだら悲しくて

"[I'd be] sad if the people around me died" — the te form connects the condition to the emotion without a full stop.

〜よう（意志・勧誘） N4

...you: volitional form — "let's..." — transforms command into invitation, obligation into choice.

最後までもがき足掻いて生きよう

"Let's struggle and flounder to the end and live" — not "we must live" but "let's live," a proposal rather than a command.

Study tip: Kanzaki Iori's grammar is the grammar of a generation that has been commanded to live without being given reasons. Pay attention to how the passive voice (-rarete iru) transforms the speaker from agent to patient, how the imperative (ikiro) encodes social pressure as linguistic force, how te mo ii grants permission for the unspeakable, and how the volitional (ikiyou) transforms command into choice. The grammar is as precise as the despair it describes — and as carefully constructed as the hope that emerges from it.
