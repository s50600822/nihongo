The Na-Adjective (ナ形容詞 / 形容動詞)

にぎやか is a na-adjective (形容動詞, keiyodoshi). Unlike i-adjectives, which inflect directly, na-adjectives require the copular particle だ (predicative) or な (attributive) to modify a noun.

Key pattern: Na-adjective + な + Noun = attributive modification

にぎやかな未来 = "a lively future" (modifying the noun directly)

未来はにぎやかだ = "the future is lively" (predicative, ending the sentence)

The Ironic Undertone

にぎやか is typically a positive word: it evokes festivals, crowded streets, children playing. But in the title story, the "liveliness" is the cacophony of an advertising-saturated dystopia. The title is therefore bitterly ironic: what sounds cheerful is actually oppressive. This tonal dissonance is central to Tsutsui's black humor.

Orthographic Note: 賑やか vs. にぎやか

The title uses hiragana にぎやか rather than the kanji 賑やか. This softens the visual impact and creates a playful, almost childlike surface that contrasts with the dystopian content. Tsutsui often uses hiragana for effect, especially in his nonsense and slapstick modes.

02 About the Author: 筒井康隆

Tsutsui Yasutaka is one of Japan's most versatile and influential writers, a shape-shifter who moved between SF, slapstick comedy, black humor, metafiction, and literary fiction with apparent ease.

Japanese name 筒井 康隆（つつい やすたか）
Born September 24, 1934, Osaka
Education Doshisha University, Faculty of Letters (Aesthetics & Art)
Debut 1960, "Otasuke" (お助け) in SF coterie magazine NULL
Major awards Izumi Kyoka Prize (1981), Tanizaki Prize (1987), Kawabata Prize (1989), Japan SF Grand Prize (1992), Yomiuri Prize (2000)
Honors Medal with Purple Ribbon (2002), Japan Art Academy member

Career Trajectory

Tsutsui founded the SF coterie magazine NULL in 1960 with his three younger brothers. His story お助け (Otasuke) was spotted by Edogawa Ranpo and reprinted in the commercial magazine Houseki, launching his professional career. In the 1960s and early 1970s, he produced a flood of short-short stories (ショートショート) that combined SF premises with slapstick and black humor. This collection, Nigiyaka na Mirai, gathers the best of that early period.

In the 1980s, Tsutsui shifted toward more experimental and literary work (虚人たち, 夢の木坂分岐点). In 1993, he issued a famous 断筆宣言 (declaration that he would stop writing) after a controversy over his story 無人警察 being removed from a high-school textbook. He resumed writing in 1996 and has continued publishing into his late 80s.

Tsutsui's Literary DNA

  • SF御三家: Along with Komatsu Sakyo and Hoshi Shinichi, Tsutsui formed the "Big Three" of early Japanese SF.
  • Black humor: He finds comedy in cruelty, bureaucracy, and bodily functions.
  • Metafiction: He frequently breaks the fourth wall and plays with narrative convention.
  • Osaka sensibility: His comedy carries the fast-talking, irreverent energy of Kansai humor.
  • Prophetic SF: Many 1960s premises (ad saturation, surveillance, time manipulation) feel contemporary.

The Three Highlighted Stories

お助け (Otasuke / "Help!") — Tsutsui's 1960 debut. An astronaut trainee finds his own time moving faster and faster relative to the world around him. He can perform pranks in "frozen time," but the ending is terrifying: he lies down in traffic and awakens to find a truck wheel slowly crushing him, screaming "Help!" in a world where no one can perceive his speed. A perfect introduction to Tsutsui's blend of SF premise and cruel punchline.

にぎやかな未来 (Nigiyaka na Mirai / "A Lively Future") — The title story. In a future saturated with advertising, silence itself becomes a luxury commodity. A man buys a "silent record" (無音のレコード) only to discover that true silence has been outlawed or made impossible. The story is a prescient satire of attention economics, written decades before smartphones and YouTube ads.

無人警察 (Mujin Keisatsu / "Unmanned Police") — Robot police officers monitor citizens for pre-crime signals, including abnormal brainwaves associated with epilepsy. The story became infamous in 1993 when the Japan Epilepsy Association protested its inclusion in a high school textbook, leading to Tsutsui's writing strike. It remains a sharp satire of surveillance and algorithmic policing.

05 What Translation Loses

A. にぎやか (nigiyaka)

Cheerful Noise vs. Oppressive Clutter

にぎやか carries warmth: children's laughter, festival crowds, a lively classroom. It is almost always positive. When Tsutsui applies it to a future of inescapable advertising, the word becomes poisoned. English "bustling" or "lively" cannot convey this betrayal of a normally happy word. The closest might be "obnoxiously lively," but that over-explains what Japanese leaves implicit.

B. ノロノロ (noronoro)

Onomatopoeia as Emotional State

ノロノロ is an adverbial onomatopoeia for slow, sluggish movement. But it also carries irritation: the speaker is annoyed by the slowness. English "slowly" is neutral. "Sluggishly" is clinical. "At a snail's pace" is idiomatic but too specific. The Japanese word compresses perception and emotion into one phonetic gesture.

C. ぶちまける (buchimakeru)

The Violence of Venting

ぶちまける means to pour out, to vent, to spill everything. The prefix ぶち～ carries a sense of explosive release (related to 打つ, to strike). "Vent" is close but too calm. "Dump on someone" captures the aggression but is too slangy. The Japanese word suggests that grievances are not gently shared but violently discharged.

D. 相槌 (aizuchi)

The Untranslatable Art of Listening

相槌 refers to the interjections ("uh-huh," "I see," "really?") that a listener makes to show engagement. There is no single English word for this. "Nods" is physical, not verbal. "Interjections" is too broad. The concept is deeply embedded in Japanese conversational etiquette: the listener is expected to actively 打つ (strike) these sounds back at the speaker.

E. お助け (otasuke)

Childish Cry in Adult Terror

お助け is an old-fashioned, almost childlike cry for help. The honorific prefix お～ softens it, making it sound like something from a period drama or a child's mouth. When the protagonist screams this at the end, trapped under a slowly descending truck wheel in a world frozen by his own accelerated time, the word's childishness makes the moment grotesquely pathetic. "Help!" is too generic. "Save me!" is too dramatic. The Japanese title word carries a specific cultural register of helplessness.

F. 無音 (muon) / 音のレコード

Silence as Luxury Commodity

無音 is not merely "silence" (静寂, seijaku). It is the absence of sound as a technical, almost scientific condition. A 無音のレコード is a record that plays nothing, a product designed to deliver silence in a world where silence no longer exists naturally. The oxymoron is sharper in Japanese because レコード (record) implies content by definition. English "silent record" sounds like a category error; in Japanese it sounds like a desperate invention.

G. ひたひた (hitahita)

The Sound of Unstoppable Flow

ひたひた suggests a steady, relentless flow, often of water approaching or overflowing. It carries a hint of menace: something is coming, continuously, without pause. "Steadily" is too neutral. "Trickling" is too small. The word makes the urine flow sound almost like rising floodwaters, turning a bodily function into something quietly horrifying.

06 Grammar Points

Na-adjective + な + Noun (attributive)

Na-adjectives modify nouns using な, not い. This is the attributive form (連体形) of the na-adjective copula.

にぎやかな未来 = a lively future

奇妙なこと = a strange thing

透明な水 = transparent water

Contrast with i-adjectives: 高い山 (takai yama) uses the adjective's inherent attributive form, with no extra particle.

～しながらも (shinagara mo) — Concessive "although"

イライラしながらも、どうにか相槌を打っていた

～ながらも combines the continuative ながら (while) with the emphatic/concessive も. It means "although / even though." The subject holds two contradictory states simultaneously.

Pattern: Verb-stem + ながらも + [contradictory clause]

Distinction from simple ながら: ながら alone means "while doing X, (also) do Y." ながらも means "despite X, (still) Y" with a sense of overcoming or enduring.

～ようとしない — Negative intention / refusal

離れようとしない = "refuses to move away" / "won't step away"

～ようとする means "to try to do." The negative ～ようとしない means "to not even try to do" or "to refuse to do." It implies stubborn persistence in not doing the action.

Formation: Volitional form (意向形) + とする → negative

English "won't" captures some of this, but ようとしない suggests a more active, almost willful refusal.

～らしい — Hearsay / Inference

チェックされらしい = "apparently they are flagged" / "I hear they get checked"

～らしい has two main uses: (1) hearsay ("I hear that...") and (2) inference from evidence ("it seems that..."). In this passage, the protagonist is reporting something he has heard or surmised about the system.

Distinction:

  • 雨が降るらしい (hearsay: "They say it will rain")
  • 雨が降ったらしい (inference: "It seems to have rained")
～てしまう — Completion / Regrettable result

腸そのものが消失してしまった = "his intestines have (regrettably/completely) disappeared"

～てしまう (colloquially ～ちゃう) indicates completion, often with a sense of irreversibility or regret. It can also suggest something happening against one's will.

In this context, it turns a medical anomaly into a personal catastrophe. The intestines didn't just disappear; they have gone and disappeared, with all the finality that implies.

～のに — Contradictory "although / and yet"

何も悪いことしていないのに = "even though I haven't done anything wrong"

～のに marks a proposition that contradicts the expected outcome. The speaker has done nothing wrong; and yet the robot police follow him.

Emotional load: のに often carries a tone of grievance or injustice. It is stronger than English "although" because it implies the speaker feels wronged by the contradiction.

～だけが — Exclusive focus / "only"

小便だけが、透明な水のように、ひたひたと流れ出る

だけが isolates one element in contrast to everything else. Here, urine is the only thing that still functions; the intestines are gone, the feces absent, but urine alone continues to flow.

The construction creates a haunting rhythm: everything else has failed; only this one thing persists, almost mockingly.

07 Cultural & Literary Context

The Short-Short Boom

In the 1960s, Japanese magazines popularized the ショートショート: stories of 2–4 pages with a twist ending. Hoshi Shinichi was the master of the form; Tsutsui brought to it a wilder, more transgressive energy. The brevity demands precision: every word must earn its place.

SF as Social Satire

Tsutsui's SF is not primarily concerned with scientific accuracy. It uses speculative premises to expose human folly. The ad-saturated future of the title story, written in the 1960s, anticipates:

  • YouTube pre-roll ads and unskippable commercials
  • Algorithmic surveillance and predictive policing
  • The commodification of attention and silence
  • Social credit systems and pre-crime detection

Kansai Humor and the Grotesque

Tsutsui's Osaka roots give his comedy a rapid, irreverent rhythm. But beneath the slapstick is a fascination with the grotesque body: intestines that vanish, time that accelerates, brainwaves that betray you. His humor is not comforting; it is the laughter of recognition at how absurd and fragile human existence is.

The 1993 Controversy: 無人警察 and the Danpitsu Sengen

In 1993, 無人警察 was selected for a high school textbook. The Japan Epilepsy Association protested that the story's depiction of epilepsy was discriminatory. Kadokawa (the publisher) removed the story without consulting Tsutsui. Enraged by what he saw as censorship and cowardice, Tsutsui issued his 断筆宣言 (declaration that he would stop writing). The case became a landmark in debates over freedom of expression, disability representation, and editorial responsibility in Japan. Tsutsui resumed writing in 1996, but the episode cemented his reputation as a principled (if combative) defender of artistic freedom.

For language learners: Tsutsui's early stories are excellent for intermediate readers. The sentences are short, the vocabulary is concrete, and the plots are gripping. But beware: his humor often depends on register shifts (sudden moves from clinical language to slang, from polite to vulgar) that are easy to miss without cultural context.

08 Study Guide

Reading Strategies

  • Read one story per session. The short-short format is designed for single sittings.
  • Pay attention to the last sentence of each story. Tsutsui's punchlines often flip the entire narrative.
  • Track his use of onomatopoeia: ノロノロ, ひたひた, ぎゅっと. These are not decorative; they carry emotional and sensory information.
  • Notice how he shifts between だ/である (literary/narrative) and だ/～のだ (colloquial/character voice) within a single story.

Discussion Questions

  1. Why does Tsutsui use the word にぎやか (normally positive) for a dystopian future? What does this irony reveal about his view of consumer society?
  2. In "Otasuke," time acceleration begins during a domestic argument. What is the relationship between the protagonist's emotional state and his supernatural condition?
  3. How does 無人警察 use the grammar of surveillance (チェックされる, 離れようとしない) to create paranoia? Can you rewrite the passage in polite Japanese, and does it change the tone?
  4. Tsutsui's body humor (腸はどこへいった) has been called "vulgar" and "brilliant." How does the clinical vocabulary (排便, 消失) interact with the absurd premise?
  5. Compare Tsutsui's SF with Western dystopian fiction (Bradbury, Orwell, Vonnegut). What is distinctively Japanese about his approach?
