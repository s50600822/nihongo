01 Prologue

その日、人類はい出した
Sono hi, jinrui wa omoidashita.
"On that day, humanity remembered."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
そのsonoDeterminerthat; the
日hiNounday
人類jinruiNounhumanity; mankind
はwaTopic particleas for
思い出したomoidashitaVerb (past)remembered; recalled — past of 思い出す omoidasu
Grammar: 思い出す is a compound verb: 思う (to think) + 出す (to bring out). Together: "to recall; to remember" (literally: "to bring [a memory] out of thought"). The past tense 思い出した places the action at a specific historical moment. The line is the most famous opening in anime history: "that day" refers to the fall of Wall Maria, when humanity's century of false peace was shattered. The grammar is simple but monumental; the weight is carried by the vocabulary and the context, not by complex syntax.
奴らに支配されていた恐怖
Yatsura ni shihaisarete ita kyoufu.
"The terror of being dominated by them."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
奴らyatsuraPronounthey; them — rough, contemptuous plural
にniAgentive particleby (marks the agent of passive)
支配されていたshihaisarete itaVerb (passive progressive past)had been dominated — passive progressive past of 支配する shihaisuru
恐怖kyoufuNounterror; fear; dread
Grammar: 支配されていた is the past progressive form of the passive voice: [suru-verb stem] + される (passive) + ていた (progressive past). It describes a state that had been ongoing: "had been being dominated." The passive に marks the agent: 奴らに = "by them." 奴ら is a deliberately coarse, dehumanizing pronoun, fitting for how humanity views the Titans. The line is a noun phrase with the copula omitted, common in dramatic narration: "[It was] the terror of having been dominated by them."
鳥かごの中に囚われていた、屈辱
Torikago no naka ni torawarete ita, kutsujoku.
"The humiliation of having been trapped inside a birdcage."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
鳥かごtorikagoNounbirdcage — 鳥 (bird) + 籠 (cage)

囚われていたtorawarete itaVerb (passive progressive past)had been imprisoned — passive progressive past of 囚われる torawareru

Grammar: 囚われていた is the past progressive of 囚われる, the passive form of 囚う (to imprison; to capture). The progressive ていた emphasizes duration: "had been [continuously] imprisoned." The structure mirrors the previous line exactly: [passive progressive past clause], [abstract noun]. This parallel structure creates a rhythmic, incantatory quality. 鳥かご (birdcage) is the central metaphor for the walled city; the walls that protected humanity also made them livestock. The comma before 屈辱 is unusual in Japanese; it creates a dramatic pause, as if the word must be spoken separately, like a verdict.
放たれた弓矢よ、壁を越え海まで飛んで行け！

"O bow and arrow that have been loosed: cross the walls and fly to the sea!"
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: The vocative particle よ after a noun directly addresses it, like "O [noun]" in English poetry. 飛んで行け combines 飛ぶ (to fly) in te-form with 行く (to go) in imperative form: "fly [and] go!" = "fly away!" The continuative form 越え connects the two actions: crossing the walls, then flying to the sea. まで marks the extent or destination: "as far as the sea." The line is a direct address to the weapon of liberation, personifying the bow and arrow as the speaker's proxy in the quest for freedom. The imperative grammar makes it a battle cry, not a wish.

02 German Refrain

Sind Sie das Essen? Nein, wir sind der Jäger!
"Are they the food? No, we are the hunters!"
Note: These German lines are among the most famous in anime music history, though they are frequently misheard. The official lyrics ask "Sind Sie das Essen?" (Are they the prey/food?), answered with "Nein, wir sind der Jäger!" (No, we are the hunter!). The reversal of the predator-prey relationship is the song's central declaration: humanity, long the livestock of the Titans, reclaims agency. The grammar is simple German, but its placement in a Japanese song creates a striking multilingual effect. Jäger (hunter) becomes the song's key motif, appearing in kanji as 狩人 (kariudo) later in the lyrics.
Feuerroter Pfeil und Bogen
"Fire-red arrow and bow" — the direct German translation of 紅蓮の弓矢.
Note: Feuerroter Pfeil und Bogen literally means "fire-red arrow and bow." 紅蓮 (guren) refers to the red lotus, but in Buddhist imagery it also represents the fires of hell, particularly the 紅蓮地獄 (Guren Jigoku), one of the eight hot hells where the cold causes the skin to crack like a red lotus blossom. The title thus carries connotations of both beauty and infernal suffering. The German translation strips away the Buddhist symbolism for directness: fire, arrow, bow, the instruments of war.

03 Verse 1

踏まれた花の 名前も知らずに
Fumareta hana no namae mo shirazu ni.
"Without even knowing the name of the flower that was trampled."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

のnoGenitive particleof

Grammar: [Verb in negative adverbial] + に means "without [verb]ing." 知らずに = "without knowing." The particle も with a negative verb creates a universal negative: 名前も知らずに = "without even knowing the name." 踏まれた is the passive past of 踏む (to step on; to trample), modifying 花: "the flower that was trampled." The flower is a classic metaphor for the innocent victims of violence, those destroyed without even being acknowledged. The grammar of ignorance (知らずに) layered over the grammar of victimization (踏まれた) creates a devastating indictment of casual cruelty.
地に墜ちたちた鳥は 風を待ちわびる

"The bird that fell, crashed to the ground, longs for the wind."

にniLocative particleto; onto

Grammar: 墜ちた and 落ちた are near-synonyms both meaning "fell," but 墜ちる carries a sense of crashing or plummeting (often used for aircraft or celestial bodies), while 落ちる is more general. Their juxtaposition creates emphasis through repetition with variation. 待ちわびる is a compound verb: 待つ (to wait) + わびる (to be weary; to long for). Together: "to wait impatiently; to long for." The bird that fell to earth yearns for the wind that would let it fly again, a metaphor for humanity trapped behind the walls, longing for freedom. The topic particle は marks the bird as the subject of this emotional state.
祈ったところで 何も変わらない
Inotta tokoro de, nani mo kawaranai.
"Even if we pray, nothing changes."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
祈ったinottaVerb (past)prayed — past of 祈る inoru

何naniInterrogative pronounwhat
もmoInclusive particleeven; also — with negative: "nothing"
変わらないkawaranaiVerb (negative)doesn't change — negative of 変わる kawaru
Grammar: [Verb in past] + ところで is a concessive pattern meaning "even if [verb]; even though [verb]." It expresses that the action is futile or insufficient to produce a result. 祈ったところで = "even if [we] prayed." 何も with a negative verb creates a universal negative: 何も変わらない = "nothing changes." The line rejects passive hope in favor of action, a core theme of the series. The grammar of concession (ところで) makes prayer a hypothetical that is immediately dismissed; the negative (変わらない) closes the door on it completely.

"What changes the unwilling present is the resolve to fight."

Grammar: [Verb] + のは nominalizes the verb phrase and topicalizes it: "What [does X] is..." 変えるのは = "What changes [it] is..." 戦う is the attributive form of the verb, modifying 覚悟: "the resolve [to] fight." The line is a cleft-like sentence structure that emphasizes the answer: not prayer, not hope, not waiting, but 覚悟 (resolve / preparedness) is what changes the world. 覚悟 is a weighty word in Japanese; it implies acceptance of consequences, including death. The brackets 《》 around 不本意な現状 mark it as a conceptual or emphasized phrase.

04 Pre-Chorus

屍踏み越えて
Shikabane fumikoete.
"Stepping over corpses."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
屍shikabaneNouncorpse; dead body — literary/archaic
踏み越えてfumikoeteCompound verb (te-form)stepping over — 踏む + 越える
Grammar: 踏み越える is a compound verb: 踏む (to step on) + 越える (to cross over; to surpass). The te-form 踏み越えて creates a participial or continuative sense: "stepping over [corpses]." The line is syntactically incomplete, serving as a fragment that connects to the following line. 屍 (shikabane) is the same literary, elevated word for corpse used in 悪魔の子, suggesting these songs share a thematic and lexical universe. The image is brutal: progress requires treading on the dead. The te-form keeps the action ongoing, as if the speaker is in the middle of this terrible march.
進む意思を 嗤う豚よ
Susumu ishi wo, warau buta yo.
"O pig who sneers at the will to advance."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
進むsusumuVerb (attributive)to advance; to proceed

をwoObject particlemarks the object

Grammar: 嗤う is a literary/archaic variant of 笑う, specifically meaning "to sneer; to scoff at; to laugh mockingly." The attributive form modifies the noun 豚: "the pig [that] sneers at." 進む意思 is a noun phrase: "the will [to] advance." The vocative よ directly addresses the contemptible figure. The pig metaphor targets those who accept captivity, who mock the desire for freedom. In the world of the walls, these are the complacent citizens who prefer the safety of livestock to the danger of hunters. The grammar of direct address (よ) makes it an accusation, not merely a description.
家畜の安寧 虚偽の繁栄
Kachiku no annei, kyogi no han'ei.
"The peace of livestock, the false prosperity."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

のnoGenitive particleof

虚偽kyogiNounfalsehood; lie; deception
のnoGenitive particleof

Grammar: Two parallel noun phrases in apposition, with the copula omitted for poetic compression. 家畜の安寧 = "the peace of livestock" — a peace that comes from being fed and protected, not from true freedom. 虚偽の繁栄 = "false prosperity" — the illusion of wealth and stability built on a lie (the walls, the king, the history that was hidden). The parallel structure creates a rhythmic indictment. Both phrases describe the same condition from different angles: the comfortable prison that humanity mistook for civilization. No verb is needed; the juxtaposition itself is the accusation.
死せる餓狼の「自由」を！
Shiseru garou no "jiyuu" wo!
"[Give us] the 'freedom' of the starving wolf brought back from death!"
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

のnoGenitive particleof
「自由」"jiyuu"Noun (quoted)"freedom" — in quotation marks
をwoObject particlemarks the object
Grammar: 死せる is the classical attributive form of 死ぬ (to die), surviving in modern Japanese in literary and poetic contexts. It means "dead; deceased; the [noun] that has died." 餓狼 (starving wolf) is a compound noun of striking ferocity. The object particle を with no explicit verb creates an exclamatory demand: "[Give us] the 'freedom' of the dead starving wolf!" The quotation marks around 自由 signal that this is not ordinary freedom; it is a specific, violent, predatory kind of freedom. The classical grammar (死せる) elevates the diction, giving the line the feel of a war chant or a curse.

"The humiliation of being imprisoned is the signal fire of counterattack."

Grammar: 嚆 (kou) is an abbreviated form of 嚆矢 (koushi) or 嚆火 (kouka), meaning "signal arrow" or "signal fire" used in ancient warfare to signal the start of a battle. The passive 囚われた modifies 屈辱, specifying which humiliation: the humiliation of having been imprisoned. This is the song's thesis: suffering transforms into the fuel for revolution.

07 Bridge

獲物を殺すのは 「凶器」でも 技術でもない
Emono wo korosu no wa, "kyouki" demo, gijutsu demo nai.
"What kills the prey is neither 'weapon' nor technique."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
獲物emonoNounprey; game; quarry
をwoObject particlemarks the object
殺すkorosuVerbto kill
のはno waNominalizer + topicwhat kills ~ (topic)
凶器kyoukiNounweapon; lethal weapon
でもdemoAdverbial particleeven; or — "neither ~ nor" with も
技術gijutsuNountechnique; skill; technology
でもないdemo naiCopula (negative)is not either; is neither — でも + ない
Grammar: [Noun A] + でも + [Noun B] + でもない = "is neither A nor B." でも is the emphatic/inclusive particle: "even; also." Combined with the negative copula ない, it creates a categorical denial of both options. The nominalization のは topicalizes the question: "What kills the prey is neither weapon nor technique." The quotation marks around 凶器 again create distance, as if the speaker is interrogating the category itself. The line sets up the revelation that follows: the true weapon is internal, not external.
研ぎ澄まされた お前自身の殺意だ
Togisumasareta, omae jishin no satsui da.
"It is your own killing intent, honed to a razor's edge."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: 研ぎ澄ます is a compound verb: 研ぐ (to sharpen; to polish) + 澄ます (to clear; to purify). Together: "to hone to a fine edge; to sharpen to clarity." The passive past 研ぎ澄まされ modifies 殺意: "killing intent [that has been] honed." お前自身 combines the rough second-person pronoun お前 with the emphatic reflexive 自身: "your very own self." The directness of お前 is confrontational; this is not gentle advice but a challenge. The line completes the bridge's argument: tools and skill are nothing without the will to kill. The true weapon is the self's own sharpened hatred.

08 German Bridge

Wir sind die Jäger...
"We are the hunters — hot as flame, cold as ice, with ourselves as the arrow, piercing through all!"
Note: The four German lines form a powerful refrain: "We are the hunters, hot as flame! We are the hunters, cold as ice! We are the hunters, with ourselves as the arrow! We are the hunters, pierce through everything and advance!" The contrast between flame (passion, rage) and ice (calculation, ruthlessness) defines the hunter's dual nature. 己を矢に込めて (with ourselves loaded into the arrow) is a striking metaphor: the hunters do not merely fire weapons; they become the weapon. The imperative 征け (yuke, "advance; conquer") at the end transforms declaration into command. The German and Japanese together create a multilingual war cry that transcends linguistic boundaries.
Angriff auf die Titanen... Bald eines Tages wird er dem Schicksal die Zähne zeigen.
"Attack on the Titans... The boy of once will soon seize the sword... Hatred and anger are a double-edged blade... Soon, one day, he will bare his teeth at fate."
Note: This German passage tells the story of Eren in miniature. Der Junge von einst (the boy of once / the boy from before) will seize first zum Schwert (the sword) and then das schwarze Schwert (the black sword), symbolizing his transformation from victim to warrior to something darker. Hass und Zorn sind eine zweischneidige Klinge (hatred and anger are a double-edged blade) is a multilingual pun: the German zweischneidig (double-edged) directly parallels the Japanese 両刃 (moroha), and both mean something that cuts both ways, harming the wielder as well as the enemy. The final line, wird er dem Schicksal die Zähne zeigen (he will show his teeth to fate), renders defiance in bodily terms: not arguing with fate, but snarling at it like an animal.

09 Verse 3

何かを変える事が出来るのは 何かを捨てる事が出来るもの
Nanika wo kaeru koto ga dekiru no wa, nanika wo suteru koto ga dekiru mono.
"What can change something is one who can discard something."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
何かnanikaIndefinite pronounsomething
をwoObject particlemarks the object
変えるkaeruVerbto change; to alter
事kotoNominalizerthe act of ~ing
がgaSubject particlemarks the subject
出来るdekiruVerb (potential)can do; is able to
のはno waNominalizer + topicwhat can ~ (topic)
捨てるsuteruVerbto discard; to throw away; to abandon
ものmonoPronounone who; those who; person
Grammar: This is a cleft sentence of striking philosophical clarity. [Verb] + 事が出来る nominalizes and potentializes: "the act of being able to change." のは topicalizes this: "What can change something is..." The predicate is 何かを捨てる事が出来るもの: "one who can discard something." もの as a pronominal noun refers to people in a generalized, philosophical sense: "one who ~; those who ~." The line states a principle of sacrifice: change requires loss. The parallel structure (何かを変える / 何かを捨てる) creates a mirror effect, as if change and discarding are two sides of the same action. This line directly echoes a key theme of 悪魔の子, linking the two songs thematically.
何ひとつ「危険性」等 背負わないままで 何かが叶う等
Nani hitotsu "kikensei" nado, seowanai mama de, nanika ga kanau nado.
"That anything could be achieved without bearing even a single 'risk,' and the like."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: 何ひとつ with a negative verb means "not even one; not a single." 背負わないままで = "while remaining in a state of not bearing." まま means "as it is; in the state of" and ままで adds the particle で to create a continuative: "while [remaining] ~." 等 (nado) is a dismissive particle: "and the like; such things." Its use twice in one line creates a tone of contempt. The entire line is a noun phrase describing a foolish belief: "[the idea] that something could come true without bearing even a single risk, and the like." The grammar of dismissal (等) makes it clear the speaker rejects this belief utterly.
暗愚の想定 唯の幻影
Angu no soutei, tada no gen'ei.
"The assumption of a fool, merely an illusion."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss

Grammar: Two parallel noun phrases with the copula omitted. 暗愚 is a rare compound: 暗 (dark; ignorant) + 愚 (foolish), meaning "a dark fool; an ignorant fool." 想定 means "assumption; supposition," a more formal, almost technical word that contrasts with the emotional intensity around it. 唯の means "merely; only; simply," reducing whatever follows to insignificance. 幻影 (illusion; phantom) completes the dismissal: the belief that one can achieve anything without risk is not just wrong but illusory, a mirage. The elevated diction (暗愚, 想定, 幻影) gives the line the authority of a verdict.
今は無謀な勇気も 「自由」の尖兵 賭けの攻勢
Ima wa mubou na yuuki mo, "jiyuu" no senpei, kake no kousei.
"Now, even reckless courage [is] the vanguard of 'freedom,' a gambit offensive."

Grammar: The copula is omitted, leaving three noun phrases in apposition: "reckless courage" = "the vanguard of freedom" = "a gambit offensive." The inclusive particle も (even) suggests that even something as seemingly negative as "reckless courage" has become valuable in this context. 尖兵 (senpei) and 攻勢 (kousei) are both military terms, reinforcing the song's martial register. The quotation marks around 自由 again signal that this is a specific, contested concept of freedom, not an abstract ideal. The line transforms recklessness from a flaw into a weapon; in war, what seems foolish may be necessary.
奔る奴隷に勝利を！
Hashiru dorei ni shouri wo!
"Victory to the slaves who run!"
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
奔るhashiruVerb (attributive)running; rushing — literary form
奴隷doreiNounslave
にniDative particleto; for
勝利shouriNounvictory; triumph
をwoObject particlemarks the object
Grammar: 奔る is a literary variant of 走る (to run), used here for elevated diction. The attributive form modifies 奴隷: "the slaves [who] run." The dative particle に marks the recipient of the wished-for victory: "to the slaves." The object particle を with no explicit verb creates an exclamatory wish or blessing, like "Victory [be] to the running slaves!" The line is a battle cry and a benediction combined. The slaves who run are those who refuse their condition; running is the first act of rebellion. The grammar of blessing (に〜を) makes victory a gift bestowed on those brave enough to flee their chains.

10 Final Chorus & Outro

Chorus
架せられた不条理は 進撃の嚆だ
Kasarereta fujouri wa, shingeki no kouka da.
"The absurdity forced upon us is the signal fire of the advance."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
架せられたkakareretaVerb (passive past)forced upon; imposed — passive past of 架する kakusuru

Grammar: 架せられた is the passive past of 架する (to impose; to place upon), a literary/suru-verb form. 不条理 (absurdity; unreason) is a philosophical term, stronger than simple "injustice" — it refers to the fundamental meaninglessness of suffering. The line mirrors the first chorus exactly in structure: [Passive past noun]は[Counterattack noun]の嚆だ. Where the first chorus said "imprisoned humiliation is the signal of counterattack," this final chorus says "imposed absurdity is the signal of the advance." The progression from 反撃 (counterattack) to 進撃 (advance/charge) shows the shift from reactive to proactive, from defending to attacking. The title of the series, 進撃の巨人 (Shingeki no Kyojin), is embedded in this line.
Outro

"The horizon that was stolen, the 'boy' of that day who wished for 'freedom.'"

Grammar: 奪われた modifies 地平: "the horizon [that was] stolen." 望む is the attributive form modifying 少年: "the boy [who] wished for." The structure is two noun phrases linked by their shared context: the stolen horizon and the boy who wished for freedom are the same wound viewed from outside and inside. The quotation marks around both 自由 and 少年 create distance and irony: freedom was always a contested concept, and the boy who wished for it is now someone else, transformed by what he lost and what he became. The classical 其の and the brackets 《》 elevate the line to mythic status, as if describing a figure from legend rather than a living person.

Grammar: 止めどなき is a classical adjectival form: 止めど (a stopping) + なき (without; lacking). In modern Japanese this would be 止むことのない or 止まない. The classical grammar elevates the line to epic register. 侵されながら is the passive form of 侵す (to invade; to violate) combined with ながら (while): "while being invaded." The killing intent is treated as an external force that invades the body, not an internal emotion. 宵闇 (yoiyami) is a poetic word for the darkness just after sunset. The color 紫 (purple/violet) contrasts with the earlier 緋 (crimson/scarlet): where the first chorus pierced crimson through twilight, this outro carries purple into the deeper dark. Purple is the color of the underworld, of bruising, of the transition from life to death. The line is the song's most visually and grammatically complex, ending on the verb 運ぶ (to carry), as if the action is still ongoing, still moving into the darkness.
Outro
冥府の弓矢
Meifu no yumiya.
"The bow and arrow of the underworld."
JapaneseRomajiPart of SpeechGloss
冥府meifuNoununderworld; Hades; realm of the dead

Grammar: A simple noun phrase with the copula omitted, mirroring the opening line's structure but transforming the bow and arrow from crimson (紅蓮) to underworld (冥府). 冥府 refers to the land of the dead across multiple mythologies: Hades, Yomi, the Christian hell. The transformation is complete: what began as a weapon of liberation (紅蓮の弓矢) ends as a weapon of death (冥府の弓矢). The bow and arrow that crossed the walls to reach the sea have become the instrument that carries the speaker into darkness. The parallel structure (紅蓮の弓矢 / 冥府の弓矢) creates a frame for the entire song, showing the full arc from hope to damnation in a single changed word.

11 Key Grammar Patterns Summary

〜のは〜だ
Nominalization + topic/copula: "What ~ is ~." Used for philosophical definitions and cleft sentences throughout the song.
〜ても / 〜でも / 何〜も〜ない
Universal negatives and concessives: "not even; neither ~ nor; nothing." Creates categorical, absolute statements.
〜ながら / 〜つつ
"While ~ing." ながら is modern; つつ is classical. Both mark simultaneous action, used for burning, invading, moving through states.
〜まで
Extent: "until; to; as far as." Marks the limit of an action: "to the limit," "until death."
〜ところで
Concessive: "even if ~ing." Expresses futility: "even if we pray, nothing changes."
〜ずに / 〜ままで
"Without ~ing" and "while remaining." Both describe states of non-action that the song condemns as illusory.
Classical / Literary Forms
死せる, 其の, 嗤う, 止めどなき, つつ. The song deliberately mixes classical and modern grammar to create an epic, martial register.
Omission of Copula
Poetic deletion of だ/です for compression and impact. Common in lyrics; creates stark, declarative force.
