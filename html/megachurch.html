Title Analysis

Title Grammar: When English Meets Japanese to Name a New Religion

The title In za Megachāchi (イン・ザ・メガチャーチ, "In the Megachurch") is a linguistic hybrid that performs the very phenomenon it describes: the importation of foreign concepts into Japanese cultural space, their katakana transformation, and their application to realities that the original language never imagined. In za (イン・ザ) is the katakana transcription of the English prepositional phrase "in the," and its use here is deliberately jarring — Japanese has its own perfectly good locative expressions (no naka de / の中で, no naka ni / の中に), but the author chooses English, rendered in the angular phonetics of katakana, to signal that this is not a traditional Japanese space but a globalized, mediated, imported phenomenon. Megachurch (メガチャーチ) is not a Japanese word; it is an English term from American evangelical Christianity, referring to a Protestant church with a sustained average attendance of 2,000 or more worshippers. By using this term as the title of a novel about Japanese idol fandom, Asai Ryō performs an act of metaphorical translation as radical as anything in the book: he says, in effect, that the fan communities surrounding idols in contemporary Japan are not merely "like" megachurches — they are megachurches, operating with the same emotional architecture, the same economic logic, the same mechanisms of belonging and exclusion, the same fusion of spectacle and community, the same promise of transcendence through collective devotion. The title's grammar is thus the grammar of equivalence: not "like a megachurch" but "in the megachurch" — the definite article the (ザ) presupposing that the reader already knows what is being referred to, already lives inside it, is already a congregant whether they know it or not. The title does not describe the novel's setting; it names the air we breathe.

Title Breakdown

イン・ザ in za / "in the" "in the" — The katakana transcription of the English prepositional phrase "in the," written as イン・ザ with the interpunct (nakaguro / 中黒) separating the two elements. The choice of English over Japanese locative expressions is strategic: it signals that the space being described is not indigenous to Japan but imported, globalized, mediated through foreign cultural forms. The katakana script itself carries associations of modernity, foreignness, and commercial branding — it is the script of "McDonald's" (マクドナルド), "Starbucks" (スターバックス), and "Instagram" (インスタグラム). By writing "in the" in katakana, Asai makes the title feel like a brand, a franchise, a global product — which is precisely how megachurches and idol fan communities operate, as replicable formats that can be deployed anywhere culture and capitalism meet. The definite article za (ザ, "the") is particularly significant: it presupposes familiarity, assuming that the reader already knows what "the megachurch" is, already recognizes it as a category of contemporary experience. This is not "a megachurch" (indefinite, one among many possible) but "the megachurch" (definite, the phenomenon itself, the air we breathe). The title thus performs an act of recognition before the book has even been opened: you are already inside it.
メガチャーチ megachāchi / "megachurch" "megachurch" — The katakana rendering of the English term "megachurch," referring to a large Protestant church with 2,000+ regular attendees, characterized by contemporary worship music, professional media production, small-group community structures, and a charismatic senior pastor. The term is not metaphorical in the original English context; it is an industry term used by sociologists of religion, church planners, and evangelical leaders. By importing this term into Japanese and applying it to idol fandom, Asai performs what the novel itself describes: the use of "story" (monogatari / 物語) to make people believe, act, and belong. The megachurch is the perfect structural analogy for the contemporary fan community because both operate on the same principles: a charismatic focal point (the pastor / the idol), a narrative of personal transformation (salvation / "my life changed when I discovered ___"), a community of the similarly transformed (the congregation / the fandom), economic practices that bind the community to the focal point (tithing / merchandise, concert tickets, streaming), and a missionary imperative to spread the good news (evangelism / "let me tell you about my oshi"). The katakana rendering megachāchi (メガチャーチ) makes the term feel slightly alien even to Japanese readers — most Japanese people have heard of megachurches only in news reports about American Christianity — and this alienation effect is deliberate. It forces the reader to pause, to ask "what is a megachurch?" and in asking, to begin the process of recognition that the novel demands: the realization that the strange foreign thing being described is actually the reader's own daily environment, seen from a distance that makes its structures visible. The mega (メガ) prefix — from Greek megas, "great / large" — also carries associations of scale, excess, and corporate power in Japanese (megabank / メガバンク, megastore / メガストア), making the term feel like a commercial category as much as a religious one. Which, the novel argues, is exactly what it is.

Author

朝井リョウ — Asai Ryō (b. 1989)

朝

朝井リョウ / Asai Ryō

First Heisei-born Naoki Prize winner · chronicler of Japanese youth · architect of the multi-perspective modular novel

Asai Ryō was born in 1989 in Gifu Prefecture, Japan — the year the Heisei era began, making him the first writer in Japanese literary history to win the prestigious Naoki Prize (直木賞) while born in the Heisei period. (He would later joke that this fact, which appears in every article about him, is the most boring possible biographical detail.) He made his debut in 2009 while still a student at Waseda University, winning the Shōsetsu Subaru New Writer's Award (小説すばる新人賞) for Kirishima, Bukatsu Yamerutte yo (桐島、部活やめるってよ, "Kirishima, Bukatsu Yamerutte yo" / "Kirishima Quit the Club"), a multi-perspective novel about the ripple effects when a popular high-school student suddenly quits his club. The book became a cultural phenomenon, was adapted into a hit film by director Yoshida Daihachi, and established the "Asai method": a modular narrative structure in which multiple viewpoints gradually assemble a picture of a social ecosystem, with each perspective revealing what the others cannot see.

Asai's subsequent career has been a systematic expansion of this method across different social fields. Shūmatsu no Mārudein (週末のマルデイン, 2013) explored youth culture and part-time work; Nanimono (何者, 2016), which won the Naoki Prize, dissected the psychology of job-hunting and social-media self-presentation among university students; Chihōshimap (チハラファン, 2018) examined regional idol culture; Seiyoku (正欲, 2021) won the Shibata Renzaburō Prize and explored sexuality, disability, and the gap between public tolerance and private acceptance; and Seishokuki (生殖記, 2022) investigated reproductive technology and the commodification of childbirth. Throughout, Asai has demonstrated an almost sociological gift for identifying the structures of feeling that define each Japanese generation — the anxieties, desires, and self-deceptions that circulate through specific social worlds — and for rendering those structures visible through the accumulated detail of individual consciousnesses.

In the Megachurch is his 15th-anniversary work, and it represents both a culmination and a deepening. The "multi-perspective modular" structure that he pioneered in Kirishima is here applied to a subject of genuine national importance: the fan economy (fandomu keizai / ファンダム経済) that has become one of the defining features of contemporary Japanese culture. Where his earlier works examined relatively contained social worlds — a high school, a university, a regional city — this novel engages with a phenomenon that operates at the scale of national culture and global media. The result is his most ambitious work: a novel that asks not merely what it feels like to be young in Japan today but what it means to live in a society where the mechanisms of belief, belonging, and manipulation have been industrialized and distributed across digital networks, where the old anchors of identity (work, family, religion, nation) have weakened and been replaced by new forms of collective devotion that are at once more intense and more volatile than anything that preceded them. Asai has said that he wants to write novels that make him think "oh no, they're reading it" — books whose accuracy about contemporary life produces a kind of vertigo in both author and reader. In the Megachurch achieves this with a precision that is almost frightening.

History

Fandom Economy, the Decline of Religion, and the New Devotion

The Megachurch as Global Phenomenon and Japanese Metaphor

The term "megachurch" refers to a specific phenomenon in American evangelical Christianity: Protestant congregations with sustained average weekly attendance of 2,000 or more, characterized by contemporary worship music, professional media production, small-group community structures, and a charismatic senior pastor whose personal brand is inseparable from the church's identity. The phenomenon emerged in the 1970s, grew explosively in the 1980s and 1990s, and has since spread globally through missionary networks and media distribution. In the United States, there are now over 1,700 megachurches; in South Korea, the Yoido Full Gospel Church claims over 800,000 members; in Nigeria, the Redeemed Christian Church of God operates a stadium-sized auditorium. The megachurch is not merely a large church; it is a specific organizational form that combines religious content with corporate management techniques, entertainment industry production values, and network marketing community structures. It sells not just salvation but belonging, purpose, identity, and community — the very things that contemporary societies, with their weakening traditional bonds, have increasingly failed to provide.

Japan, by contrast, has very few actual megachurches. Christianity has never been a majority religion in Japan (Christians constitute less than 1% of the population), and the Protestant churches that do exist tend to be small, denominational, and culturally marginal. The megachurch as a lived religious reality is almost unknown to most Japanese people. This is precisely why Asai's metaphor is so powerful: he is not describing something Japanese readers already know; he is showing them that something they do know — idol fandom, "oshi-katsu" (推し活), the fan communities that surround actors, musicians, and virtual performers — has the same structure as a phenomenon they associate with foreign religion. The historical context for this insight is Japan's accelerating religious decline combined with the explosive growth of fan culture. Religious affiliation and practice have been dropping in Japan for decades; temples and shrines struggle to attract young people; the traditional "seasonal festival" model of Japanese religiosity has weakened as urbanization and digital media have transformed daily life. At the same time, the fan economy has grown into a major sector of Japanese consumer culture: idol groups, anime franchises, voice actors, YouTubers, and virtual performers command billions of yen in merchandise, ticket sales, streaming revenue, and fan-organized events. The novel's historical significance is to name this transition: what Japanese people have lost in traditional religion, they have regained (or replaced) in fan devotion — and the replacement is not merely analogous to religion but structurally identical to it, operating with the same psychological mechanisms, the same economic logics, and the same potential for both genuine community and systematic exploitation.

The "Oshi-katsu" Boom and the Industrialization of Devotion

The 2020s in Japan have been defined by what the media calls the "oshi-katsu boom" (推し活ブーム) — the mainstreaming of fan activities that were once considered the domain of "otaku" subculture into a normalized, even expected, mode of cultural participation. "Oshi" (推し, "the one I push / support / recommend") originally referred to one's favorite member of an idol group, the one you would "push" to others as the best. By the early 2020s, the term had expanded to encompass any object of intense, performative affection: a musician, an actor, a voice actor, a character, a sports team, even a corporate mascot. "Oshi-katsu" (推し活, "oshi activities") refers to the full range of practices through which this affection is expressed and performed: attending concerts and events, purchasing merchandise, participating in online fan communities, creating fan content, organizing support projects, and — crucially — spending money in ways that are visible to both the object of devotion and the fan community. The economic scale of these activities is staggering: the idol industry alone generates hundreds of billions of yen annually, and when extended to anime, games, and character goods, the "content industry" approaches 10% of Japan's consumer economy. What distinguishes "oshi-katsu" from earlier forms of fandom is its industrial organization: fan activities are no longer merely tolerated by content producers but actively cultivated, measured, and monetized. Idol groups hold "handshake events" where fans pay per minute of physical contact; streaming platforms sell "digital tickets" that grant access to exclusive content; fan clubs operate tiered membership systems that reward higher spending with greater proximity to the idol. The novel's "fandom economy" (fandomu keizai / ファンダム経済) is not a metaphor but a technical description of this system — and Asai's insight is that this system has learned, consciously or unconsciously, from the most successful organizational form in the history of voluntary human association: the church.

The Newspaper Novel in the Age of Digital Fandom

In the Megachurch was originally serialized in the evening edition of the Nihon Keizai Shimbun (日本経済新聞夕刊) from April 2023 to March 2024 — a "newspaper novel" (shinbun shōsetsu / 新聞小説) in the tradition that stretches from Natsume Sōseki's Michikusa (1915, Asahi Shimbun) through Matsumoto Seichō's Points and Lines (1958, Yomiuri Shimbun) to more recent works by Kirino Natsuo and others. The newspaper novel is a distinctively Japanese form: a work of fiction distributed in daily installments alongside news, weather, and stock prices, read by commuters on trains and by families at dinner tables, creating a shared national reading experience that bridges regional and generational divides. The form had been considered moribund in the digital age — who reads fiction in newspapers when they can stream video on their phones? — but Asai's serialization and subsequent triumph at the Booksellers' Award demonstrated its continuing power. The novel's newspaper origins are significant in several ways. First, the Nihon Keizai Shimbun is Japan's leading business newspaper, read primarily by middle-aged professionals and business leaders — precisely the demographic represented by Kubota Yoshihiko, the 47-year-old record company accountant who is one of the novel's three protagonists. Second, the daily installment format (roughly 1,500 characters per episode) forced Asai to construct the novel as a series of discrete scenes, each with its own dramatic arc, which contributes to the book's relentless forward momentum. Third, the newspaper's implicit claim to report "what is happening in Japan today" gave the novel a documentary authority: this is not merely fiction but a report from the front lines of contemporary culture, delivered through the same medium that reports on GDP and election results. The fact that a newspaper novel won the Booksellers' Award for the first time in the prize's 23-year history is itself a historical event: it signals that the traditional forms of Japanese literary culture can still engage with the most urgent contemporary realities, and that the shared experience of reading — even in an age of algorithmic personalization — still has collective power.

Structure

Three Souls, One Machine for Making Belief

The Architecture: Fandom as Triple-Helix

The novel is structured around three protagonists whose lives intersect through the male idol group "Bloome" (ブルーミー) and its surrounding ecosystem of fans, producers, and media. This three-perspective structure — what Asai has called the "triple-helix" (toripuru herikusu / トリプルヘリックス) in interviews — is his most refined deployment of the multi-perspective modular form he pioneered in Kirishima. Each protagonist occupies a different position in relation to the fandom economy: one who builds it, one who is consumed by it, and one who has survived it. Their alternating chapters create a narrative centrifuge that separates the components of fan devotion — narrative, community, commerce, identity — and reveals how each operates at the level of individual consciousness. The structure is not merely formal but thematic: the novel argues that to understand fandom, you must see it simultaneously from inside and outside, from above and below, from the perspective of those who profit, those who suffer, and those who merely pass through.

Kubota Yoshihiko: The Man Who Builds the Church

Kubota Yoshihiko (久保田慶彦), 47, works in the accounting and finance department of a major record company — a position far removed from the glamorous world of artist management he dreamed of as a young man. Once the passionate coordinator of the company's Western music division, he has been sidelined to a department where music is merely numbers on a spreadsheet. Divorced for seven years, his only meaningful human connection is a monthly video call with his daughter Sumika, who lives with his ex-wife and has taken her mother's surname, Mutō. His life is a study in mid-career emptiness: too old to reinvent himself, too young to retire, too conscious of his own failures to find comfort in routine. When his former classmate Hashimoto — now a successful producer for a popular idol group — invites him to join the "story team" for a new male idol group called "Bloome," Kubota sees a last chance to enter the world he once dreamed of. The "story team" is not responsible for music or choreography; its job is to construct the narrative that will make fans believe — the backstories, the character arcs, the interpersonal dynamics, the "lore" that transforms five young men into a universe worth devoting one's life to. Kubota's chapters trace his gradual initiation into the logic of fandom engineering: the discovery that what moves people is not talent or beauty but story, the carefully constructed illusion of authenticity that makes fans feel they are witnessing something real rather than consuming something designed. His education is the novel's most chilling thread, because it reveals the mechanics of manipulation with documentary clarity — and because Kubota, despite his growing awareness of what he is doing, cannot bring himself to stop. The story he builds becomes his own last chance for meaning, and he is as trapped by it as any fan.

Mutō Sumika: The Girl Who Falls Into the Abyss

Mutō Sumika (武藤澄香), Kubota's daughter, is a university student in Kyushu — far from Tokyo's entertainment centers, studying at a school with a large international student population. She is introverted, sensitive, and exhausted by the accumulated psychological labor of navigating a world that feels increasingly hostile to her temperament. Her father is a monthly video call; her mother is present but not close; her university peers are friendly but not friends. She is, in other words, precisely the kind of young person that the fandom economy is designed to capture: intelligent enough to feel the emptiness of her surroundings, lonely enough to seek connection, and young enough to believe that intensity of feeling is the same as authenticity of experience. When she encounters Dōya — a member of Bloome, or perhaps a figure adjacent to the group (the novel carefully maintains the ambiguity of whether her devotion is "real" or manufactured) — she "falls into the swamp" (numaru / 沼る), the Japanese fan term for becoming deeply, irretrievably obsessed with an idol. Her chapters trace the progression of this obsession with a clinical precision that never condescends: the first tentative search, the discovery of the fan community, the gradual surrender of time and money, the replacement of her own narrative with the group's narrative, the transformation of her loneliness into a form of passionate belonging that is simultaneously genuine and exploited. Sumika's story is the novel's emotional center, and Asai's rendering of her consciousness — its vulnerability, its intelligence, its capacity for self-deception — is what makes the book more than a sociological exposé. We do not merely observe her being manipulated; we understand why she wants to be manipulated, why the story being sold to her feels like salvation rather than exploitation, why the community that consumes her money and energy feels like the first real home she has ever had.

Sumikawa Ayako: The Woman Who Survived and Remembers

Sumikawa Ayako (隅川絢子), 35, is a contract office worker with a take-home pay of less than ¥200,000 per month — the kind of invisible, precarious labor that sustains Japanese corporate life without ever being acknowledged by it. She was once a devoted member of a fandom community for a young stage actor, part of a close-knit group of women who found in their shared devotion a friendship and purpose that their actual lives could not provide. Then a news report changed everything: a scandal, a revelation, a piece of information that made continued devotion impossible — not because the object of devotion was revealed as unworthy (the novel is too sophisticated for such moral simplicity) but because the story that had sustained the community was revealed as constructed, and the community itself turned on her when she tried to speak what she knew. Ayako's chapters are the novel's retrospective consciousness, the voice of someone who has been inside the megachurch and emerged, damaged but clear-eyed, able to see the structure because she has survived its collapse. Her perspective provides the novel's moral compass — not because she is righteous but because she is experienced, because she knows that the warmth of community and the coldness of commerce are not opposites but twins, because she has learned that the stories that save us are the same stories that exploit us, and that the only honest response is to remember, to speak, and to refuse the easy comfort of forgetting. Her presence in the novel transforms it from a story about fandom into a story about survival — about what remains when the story ends, when the community dissolves, when the idol moves on or disappears. What remains, Asai suggests, is the person who was always there beneath the devotion: fragile, contradictory, and finally, stubbornly real.

Text

Key Passages

These passages capture the novel's devastating clarity about how stories move people, how communities form around devotion, and how the mechanisms of belief operate in a country that has replaced God with narrative.

01
"In a Country Without God, the Best Way to Manipulate People Is to Use 'Story'"
神がいないこの国で人を操るには、“物語”を使うのが一番いいんですよ。
"Kami ga inai kono kuni de hito wo ayatsuru ni wa, 'monogatari' wo tsukau no ga ichiban ii n desu yo."
"In a country without God, the best way to manipulate people is to use 'story.'"

Nuance Note

This sentence — the novel's most quoted line, printed on its obi (帯) and repeated in countless reviews — is a masterpiece of compressed cultural analysis, and its grammar encodes the entire argument of the book in a single breath. Kami ga inai (神がいない, "God does not exist / there is no God") uses the existential negative ga inai (がいない, "does not exist / is not present") with kami (神, "God / deity / divine being") as the subject. The specificity of kami — not "religion" (shūkyō / 宗教) or "faith" (shinkō / 信仰) but the monotheistic capital-G God — is crucial. Japan is not a country without religion; it is a country without God, meaning without the single, omnipotent, transcendent deity of Abrahamic tradition. Japan has Buddhas, kami (in the Shinto sense of spirits and ancestors), and countless local deities, but it does not have the kind of God who issues commandments, demands exclusive worship, and provides a single narrative of cosmic meaning. This absence, the sentence suggests, is not merely a demographic fact but a structural condition: in a country without God, people still need what God provides — meaning, belonging, transcendence, moral orientation — and they will find substitutes. Kono kuni de (この国で, "in this country") uses the demonstrative kono (この, "this") to make the statement specific and immediate — not "in countries without God" (abstract, general) but "in this country" (the one we are living in, the one you are reading this in). The locative particle de (で, "in / at") makes the country into a container, a space within which something happens. Hito wo ayatsuru ni wa (人を操るには, "to manipulate people / in order to manipulate people") uses the object marker wo (を) with ayatsuru (操る, "to manipulate / to control / to operate"), a verb that carries associations of puppetry and mechanical control — to ayatsuru is not merely to persuade but to make someone move as you direct them, as a puppeteer controls marionette strings. The purposive ni wa (には, "in order to / for the purpose of") frames what follows as instrumental advice: if your goal is manipulation, here is the best method. "Monogatari" wo tsukau no ga ichiban ii ("物語"を使うのが一番いい, "using 'story' is the best") uses the nominalized verb tsukau no (使うの, "using / the act of using") as the subject of the adjectival predicate ichiban ii (一番いい, "best / number one good"). The quotation marks around monogatari (物語, "story / narrative / tale") are essential: they mark the word as a concept being examined, a tool being named, not merely a casual reference. The sentence is thus not "stories are nice" but "'story' as a technology is optimal." The final n desu yo (んですよ, explanatory assertion with emphatic particle) gives the sentence the quality of insider knowledge being shared — this is not a public announcement but a confidence, a piece of professional wisdom delivered from someone who knows how the machine works. What is lost in English is the full theological weight of kami (神) in this specific construction. "God" captures the referent but loses the contrast with Japan's actual religious landscape; "a god" would be more accurate to Japanese religious pluralism but would lose the monotheistic specificity that makes the sentence provocative. "Manipulate" captures the denotation of ayatsuru but loses the puppetry association; "control" is too neutral; "operate" too mechanical. The English sentence conveys the argument but cannot reproduce the shock of encountering this analysis in Japanese — a language in which the word kami appears in Shinto shrines and anime titles alike, and in which the absence of the Abrahamic God has rarely been named so bluntly as a social condition.

02
"The Sinking Archipelago, Where 'Communities' Boil"
沈みゆく列島で、“界隈”は沸騰する。
"Shizumi yuku rettō de, 'kaiwai' wa futtō suru."
"In the sinking archipelago, 'communities' boil over."

Nuance Note

This opening line of the novel's promotional copy is a study in apocalyptic compression, and its grammar creates a counterpoint between national decline and subcultural intensity. Shizumi yuku rettō de (沈みゆく列島で, "in the sinking archipelago / in the archipelago that is sinking") uses the continuative form shizumi yuku (沈みく, "sinking / going under") of shizumu (沈む, "to sink / to submerge"), with the auxiliary yuku (ゆく, "to go") giving the sinking a sense of ongoing, perhaps irreversible process. Rettō (列島, "archipelago / chain of islands") is the standard geographical term for the Japanese islands, but in this context it carries unmistakable echoes of national decline discourse — the "sinking" Japan of economic stagnation, demographic collapse, and geopolitical marginalization. The locative de (で, "in / at") establishes this sinking as the background, the atmospheric condition within which the main action occurs. "Kaiwai" wa futtō suru ("界隈"は沸騰する, "'communities' boil over / 'scenes' are in an uproar") uses the topic marker wa (は) to make kaiwai (界隈, "community / scene / circle / neighborhood") the subject of discourse. The quotation marks around kaiwai are significant: they mark the word as a contemporary internet-age term, a piece of slang that has migrated from online communities to general usage. Kaiwai originally meant "vicinity / neighborhood" but has been repurposed by Japanese internet culture to mean something like "scene" or "community" — the group of people who share a specific interest, aesthetic, or devotion. It is the perfect word for the novel's subject because it is simultaneously local and networked, organic and self-conscious, a "neighborhood" that exists in servers and timelines rather than streets. Futtō suru (沸騰する, "to boil / to boil over / to be in an uproar") is a powerful verb: futsu (沸) means "to boil" (water), and tō (騰) means "to rise / to surge," so futtō (沸騰) literally means "boiling and surging." The contrast between shizumi yuku (sinking) and futtō suru (boiling) creates the novel's central tension: the nation sinks while its subcultures boil. The macro-level decline (demographic, economic, geopolitical) and the micro-level intensity (fan devotion, community formation, narrative investment) are not contradictory but complementary — as the large structures weaken, the small intensities strengthen, drawing energy from the very collapse they seem to ignore. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese resonance of rettō (列島). "Archipelago" is accurate but clinical; "the Japanese islands" is more specific but loses the poetic compression; "Japan" is too flat. The word rettō carries in Japanese a sense of fragility, of land suspended between sea and sky, that "archipelago" cannot fully convey. Similarly, kaiwai (界隈) has no perfect English equivalent — "scene" is too cool, "community" too earnest, "fandom" too specific, "circle" too small. The word's very untranslatability is part of its power: it names something that exists between all these categories, a form of belonging that is too new and too Japanese to have an English name. The English translation can convey the meaning but loses the sense of naming something that has not been named before — which is precisely what the novel does.

03
"Life Is Less Painful with an Addiction"
中毒症状があるほうが苦しくないのだ、人生は。
"Chūdoku shōjō ga aru hō ga kurushikunai no da, jinsei wa."
"Life is less painful when you have an addiction — that is what life is."

Nuance Note

This sentence — from Sumika's interior monologue as she sinks deeper into idol devotion — is one of the novel's most quietly devastating observations, and its grammar encodes a theory of contemporary suffering that challenges conventional moralism about addiction and obsession. Chūdoku shōjō ga aru hō ga (中毒症状があるほうが, "the side that has addiction symptoms / having an addiction [subject]") uses the comparative hō (方, "side / direction") construction to set up a comparison between two states: having an addiction and not having one. Chūdoku shōjō (中毒症状, "addiction symptoms / withdrawal symptoms / toxicosis") is clinical language — the term a doctor would use, not a fan — and its use here creates a deliberate collision between medical discourse and emotional reality. The topic marker ga (が) on hō marks this side as the subject of the coming predicate, with a sense of new information or emphasis. Kurushikunai no da (苦しくないのだ, "is not painful / is less painful / the fact is that it is not painful") uses the negative of kurushii (苦しい, "painful / agonizing / difficult"), a word of intense physical and emotional suffering, with the explanatory nominalizer no da (のだ) that makes the statement into a discovered truth, a realization that has just arrived. The no da is crucial: it transforms the sentence from a description into an explanation, from "having an addiction is less painful" into "the fact is, life is less painful when you're addicted — and this is why." Jinsei wa (人生は, "life [topic]") comes at the end as a topical afterthought, a redefinition of the subject: not "addiction is less painful" but "life is less painful when addicted" — the entire phenomenon of living, re-evaluated through the lens of chemical and narrative dependency. The word order (predicate before topic) is unusual and powerful: it makes the sentence feel like an aftershock, a conclusion that rewrites its own premise. What is lost in English is the full force of chūdoku shōjō (中毒症状) as clinical terminology applied to fan devotion. "Addiction" captures the meaning but loses the medical specificity; "withdrawal symptoms" would be more literal but would mislead; "toxicosis" is too technical. The Japanese deliberately uses the language of pathology to describe something that mainstream culture celebrates as "passion" or "dedication," and this lexical choice is the sentence's moral core. The English translation must choose between accuracy and impact, between "addiction" (accessible but general) and "withdrawal symptoms" (precise but confusing). The no da construction is also difficult: "the fact is" adds words that aren't in the original; omitting it loses the sense of discovery. The Japanese sentence feels like a thought that has just emerged from experience, unfiltered by social propriety; the English translation, however careful, will feel more composed, more literary, less raw.

04
"What Moves People in This Era Is Not God, Not the Nation, Not the Company — It's Story"
今の時代、人を動かすものは神でも国でも会社でもなく、物語なんだ。
"Ima no jidai, hito wo ugokasu mono wa kami demo kuni demo kaisha demo naku, monogatari na n da."
"In this era, what moves people is not God, not the nation, not the company — it's story."

Nuance Note

This sentence — spoken by Kunitomi Makoto, the entertainment IT company's marketing leader who becomes Kubota's tutor in the logic of fandom — is the novel's thesis statement, and its grammar builds a historical argument through negation and substitution. Ima no jidai (今の時代, "this era / the present age") uses the demonstrative ima (今, "now / present") with the nominalizer no (の) to create a temporal specification — not "always" or "in every age" but "in this era," the one we happen to be living through. Hito wo ugokasu mono wa (人を動かすものは, "what moves people / the thing that moves people [topic]") uses the relative clause hito wo ugokasu (人を動かす, "moves people / causes people to act") to modify mono (もの, "thing / what"), with the topic marker wa (は) making "what moves people" the established subject of the coming predicate. The verb ugokasu (動かす, "to move / to stir / to mobilize") is causative and active — it describes not merely emotional influence but the production of action, the making of people do things. Kami demo kuni demo kaisha demo naku (神でも国でも会社でもなく, "not God, not the nation, not the company") uses the exhaustive listing construction ...demo...demo...demo (...でも...でも...でも, "whether...or...or") with the continuative negative naku (なく, "not being / without being") to enumerate and dismiss the three great mobilizing forces of modern Japanese history. Kami (神, "God") represents premodern and Western religious authority; kuni (国, "nation / country") represents the imperial and postwar state; kaisha (会社, "company / corporation") represents the postwar corporate system that organized Japanese identity through lifetime employment and corporate loyalty. These three — religion, nation, corporation — were the successive anchors of Japanese collective identity, and the sentence declares, with historical sweep, that all three have lost their mobilizing power. Monogatari na n da (物語なんだ, "it's story / it's narrative") uses the nominal copula na n da (なんだ, "is / that's what it is") to make the substitution: not those three, but this one. The na n da carries a sense of finality and revelation — this is the answer, the thing that has been hidden, the truth that explains what we are seeing. What is lost in English is the historical specificity of the three negated terms. "God, nation, company" captures the meaning but loses the Japanese historical sequence: kami (神) evokes the Christian missions and premodern faith; kuni (国) evokes the imperial period and its aftermath; kaisha (会社) evokes the postwar "company man" (kaishain / 会社員) system. Each term carries a century of Japanese history, and their enumeration creates a miniature historical narrative: from religion to nationalism to corporatism to... story. The English "company" also fails to capture the full weight of kaisha (会社) in postwar Japan, where it was not merely a place of employment but a primary identity, a family substitute, a source of meaning and belonging. "Corporation" would be more accurate to the economic reality but less resonant with Japanese social history. The sentence's power in Japanese comes from this historical weight, from the sense that an entire civilization's mobilizing forces have been tried and found wanting, leaving only monogatari (物語) — the word that Japanese uses for everything from ancient myths to advertising copy to personal life narratives. The English translation can convey the argument but cannot reproduce the sense of historical exhaustion and replacement that makes the sentence feel like a diagnosis of civilization itself.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Asai writes with the precision of a sociologist who has learned that the most devastating analyses are delivered in the calmest prose — and the translation of his most culturally specific novel raises urgent questions about how to carry structural insight across linguistic boundaries without flattening it.

1. Kaiwai (界隈): The Untranslatable Community

JP 界隈 — a community/scene that exists in digital space, simultaneously local and networked, organic and self-conscious; the "neighborhood" of shared obsession
EN "community" / "scene" / "circle" / "fandom" — each captures one dimension but loses the others
Loss The word kaiwai (界隈) is one of the most important and most untranslatable terms in contemporary Japanese internet culture, and its centrality to In the Megachurch makes it a crucial site of translation loss. Originally meaning "vicinity / neighborhood / surrounding area," kaiwai has been repurposed by Japanese online communities to mean something like "the group of people who share a specific interest, aesthetic, or devotion" — but with crucial differences from English equivalents. "Community" is too warm and voluntary; kaiwai can be accidental, even unwelcome (one finds oneself in a kaiwai without having chosen it). "Scene" is too cool and performative; kaiwai includes people who never perform, who merely consume. "Circle" is too small and intentional; kaiwai can encompass thousands of strangers. "Fandom" is too specific; kaiwai extends to political groups, aesthetic movements, and lifestyle clusters that would never call themselves "fans." The word's original meaning — "vicinity" — carries a sense of spatial proximity that survives in its internet-age usage: a kaiwai is a "neighborhood" you inhabit online, a place where you see the same faces, recognize the same references, share the same temporal rhythms (release dates, event schedules, anniversaries). This neighborhood-quality is essential to the novel's argument, because it explains why kaiwai membership feels like belonging rather than mere consumption — it provides the spatial metaphors (being "in" a place, having "neighbors") that make abstract networks feel like communities. The English translator must choose one approximation and sacrifice the others, or use a phrase ("community/scene") that feels clumsy and analytical. Either way, the word's unique fusion of local and global, organic and digital, is lost — and with it, a key to understanding how contemporary Japanese belonging actually works.

2. Oshi (推し) and Oshi-katsu (推し活): The Grammar of Devotion

JP 推し／推し活 — "the one I push/support" and "activities of supporting"; a grammar of performative, commercialized affection
EN "favorite" / "bias" / "stan" / "fave" / "support activities" — each captures one register but loses the commercial and performative dimensions
Loss The term oshi (推し) derives from the verb osu (推す, "to push / to recommend / to support"), and its grammar is essential to its meaning. An oshi is not merely someone you "like" — they are someone you push, someone whose success you actively promote, someone whose value you help create through your attention and expenditure. The verb-origin makes oshi inherently active and performative: to have an oshi is to do something, not merely to feel something. Oshi-katsu (推し活, "oshi activities") extends this performativity into a full lifestyle: the concerts attended, the merchandise purchased, the social media posts composed, the community events organized — all the practices through which devotion is made visible and measurable. English "favorite" is too passive; "bias" (K-pop fan terminology) is too specific to one subculture; "stan" (from the Eminem song, now general internet slang) carries associations of unhealthy obsession that oshi does not necessarily have; "fave" is too casual. None of these can capture the commercial dimension of oshi-katsu — the fact that these "activities" are also expenditures, that the fan is simultaneously a devotee and a customer, a believer and a revenue source. The Japanese term holds these contradictions in suspension: oshi-katsu is fun, meaningful, community-building, and economically exploitative, all at once. English translations must either sanitize this complexity ("fan activities") or emphasize the exploitation ("consumer behavior"), but they cannot hold both dimensions simultaneously as the Japanese term does. The loss is thus not merely lexical but structural: without oshi and oshi-katsu, the novel's central phenomenon becomes either romanticized or pathologized, losing the precise social analysis that makes Asai's work important.

3. Kami (神) in a Country Without God

JP 神がいないこの国 — "a country without God" where God means the monotheistic, transcendent, command-issuing deity of Abrahamic tradition
EN "a country without God" — accurate but loses the contrast with Japan's actual religious landscape
Loss The phrase kami ga inai kono kuni (神がいないこの国, "this country without God") is the novel's most theologically charged expression, and its translation raises profound questions about religious specificity. In Japanese, kami (神) is a complex word: it refers to the singular God of Christianity (Kami-sama / 神様), the multiple kami of Shinto (八百万の神, "eight million gods"), the Buddhist divine beings, and even the "gods" of popular culture (アニメの神, "the god of anime"). When Asai writes kami ga inai (神がいない, "God does not exist"), he is using kami in the specifically monotheistic sense — the capital-G God who creates, commands, and judges. Japan is not "without kami" in the broader sense; it is full of kami of various kinds. What it lacks is the specific kind of kami that has organized Western civilization for two millennia: the transcendent, omnipotent, exclusive deity whose absence creates a specific kind of existential vacuum. The English "a country without God" captures this meaning for readers familiar with Christian theology, but it cannot reproduce the shock that Japanese readers feel when they encounter this phrase — because for Japanese readers, the sentence forces a recognition: "Oh, he's talking about that God, the Western one, the one we don't have." This recognition is the first step in the novel's argument: to see what is missing is to begin to understand what has replaced it. The English reader, living in a culture where "God" is still a live option, may not feel this recognition; they may simply agree ("Yes, Japan is secular") without understanding the specific shape of the absence. The translation is accurate but loses the dialectical movement of the original: the Japanese reader is led from the recognition of absence to the discovery of substitution, while the English reader may simply confirm a pre-existing stereotype about Japanese secularism.

4. Numaru (沼る): The Slang of Irreversible Devotion

JP 沼る — "to fall into the swamp"; fan slang for becoming irretrievably obsessed; implies sinking, stuckness, and the impossibility of escape
EN "to fall into the swamp" / "to get sucked in" / "to become obsessed" — literal translation is comprehensible but loses the slang authenticity
Loss The verb numaru (沼る, "to fall into the swamp") is fan-community slang that has entered general Japanese usage in the 2020s, and it is the perfect word for what the novel describes. Derived from numa (沼, "swamp / marsh / bog"), it describes the moment when casual interest becomes irreversible obsession — when the fan realizes, often with a mixture of delight and resignation, that they can no longer leave, that the object of devotion has become a permanent feature of their mental landscape. The metaphor is precise: a swamp is not a cliff (you don't fall suddenly) nor a whirlpool (you don't spin dizzily) but a gradual sinking, each step deeper than the last, the ground becoming softer, the exit becoming less visible, until you realize that the swamp is now your environment. This gradualism is essential to the novel's portrayal of Sumika's descent: she does not suddenly "become a fan"; she slowly numaru, each small investment of time and emotion making the next one easier, until the swamp has become her natural habitat. The English "fall into the swamp" is comprehensible but sounds like a cautionary tale, a warning against danger; numaru in Japanese fan usage is often affectionate, even celebratory — fans congratulate each other on having numaru-ed, as if acquiring a new home. "Get sucked in" captures the irreversibility but loses the environmental metaphor; "become obsessed" is too clinical; "fall down the rabbit hole" is close in meaning but carries Alice-in-Wonderland associations of wonder rather than the sticky, slightly unpleasant physicality of a swamp. The loss is tonal and metaphorical: the English translations can convey the meaning but cannot reproduce the sense of community-specific language being used to describe a community-specific experience. Numaru is a word that only exists because the phenomenon exists; it is the linguistic evidence of a social reality that has not yet been named by official discourse. To translate it is to risk either domesticating it into generic English or leaving it as a foreign term that requires explanation — and either choice diminishes the word's power as insider language that has become general because the phenomenon has become general.

5. Monogatari (物語): The Word That Contains Everything

JP 物語 — story; narrative; tale; myth; the fundamental unit of Japanese meaning-making, from ancient chronicles to advertising copy
EN "story" / "narrative" — accurate but loses the historical and cultural weight
Loss The word monogatari (物語) is one of the most culturally loaded in the Japanese language, and its use as the novel's central concept creates translation challenges that are ultimately insurmountable. Etymologically, monogatari combines mono (物, "thing / matter / substance") and katari (語り, "telling / narration / recitation"), meaning literally "the telling of things" — but this literal meaning barely scratches the surface. Monogatari is the word used for Japan's earliest prose classics (Tale of Genji / 源氏物語); for folktales and myths; for news reports (nyūsu no monogatari / ニュースの物語); for personal life stories (jibun no monogatari / 自分の物語); for advertising narratives; for the "lore" of fictional universes; and for the constructed backstories of idol groups. This range is not accidental: Japanese culture has historically understood reality itself as monogatari, as something that becomes meaningful only when told, narrated, shaped into story-form. The novel's claim that "what moves people is story" is thus not merely a statement about marketing or propaganda; it is a claim about the fundamental structure of Japanese meaning-making, rooted in a literary tradition that stretches back a thousand years. The English "story" cannot carry this weight. "Story" is a casual word, used for children's books and newspaper articles alike; it does not evoke Genji or Heike. "Narrative" is more formal and analytical, but it loses the warmth and accessibility of monogatari, its sense of something shared around a fire or a screen. "Tale" is archaic; "myth" is too specific. The novel's quotation marks around monogatari ("物語") mark it as a concept under examination, a tool being analyzed — but the English translation's quotation marks around "story" cannot reproduce the cultural resonance of the Japanese original. The loss is civilizational: the novel is making an argument about Japanese culture using a word that is itself a monument of Japanese culture, and the translation can convey the argument but not the monument.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns from the language of sinking nations and boiling communities, of manipulative stories and addictive devotions, of what moves people when God has left the building.

〜のが一番いい（最上級の助言） N4

...no ga ichiban ii: "[doing X] is the best / optimal" — superlative recommendation stating the best method among alternatives

“物語”を使うのが一番いいんですよ

"Using 'story' is the best way" — The grammar of instrumental advice.

〜には〜のが一番いい（目的＋手段） N3

...ni wa... no ga ichiban ii: "in order to [goal], [method] is best" — purposive construction framing means-end reasoning

人を操るには、“物語”を使うのが一番いい

"To manipulate people, using 'story' is best" — The grammar of cynical expertise.

〜のだ（説明・発見） N4

...no da: "the fact is that / it is that" — explanatory nominalizer marking a realization, discovery, or explanation

中毒症状があるほうが苦しくないのだ

"The fact is, having an addiction is less painful" — The grammar of discovered truth.

〜でも〜でも〜でもなく（列挙否定） N4

...demo...demo...demo naku: "not X, not Y, not Z" — exhaustive listing negation dismissing multiple alternatives

神でも国でも会社でもなく、物語なんだ

"Not God, not nation, not company — it's story" — The grammar of historical replacement.

〜ほうが〜（比較） N5

...hō ga...: "the side of / rather / more" — comparative construction privileging one state over another

中毒症状があるほうが苦しくない

"The side with addiction symptoms is less painful" — The grammar of paradoxical comparison.

〜ゆく（継続・進行） N2

...yuku: "[doing] onward / continuing to [do]" auxiliary verb indicating ongoing, often irreversible process

沈みゆく列島

"the sinking archipelago / the archipelago that continues to sink" — The grammar of ongoing decline.

〜わ…〜んだ（対比的話題提示） N3

...wa...n da: "[topic] is [explanation]" — contrastive topic marking with explanatory predicate, often used for redefinition

人生は…中毒症状があるほうが苦しくないのだ

"Life is... the side with addiction is less painful" — The grammar of topical redefinition.

〜的（性質・属性） N3

...-teki: "-like / -ical / in terms of" — suffix forming adjectival nouns indicating quality or attribute

内向的で繊細な気質

"introverted and sensitive temperament" — The grammar of psychological characterization.

Study tip: The grammar of In the Megachurch is the grammar of historical diagnosis delivered as casual observation. The superlative recommendation (no ga ichiban ii) presents manipulation as technical advice; the purposive means-end construction (ni wa... no ga ichiban ii) frames cynicism as expertise; the explanatory nominalizer (no da) transforms addiction from pathology into discovered truth; the exhaustive negation (demo...demo...demo naku) dismisses centuries of mobilizing forces in a single breath; the comparative (hō ga) makes addiction paradoxically preferable; the continuative auxiliary (yuku) turns national decline into ongoing process; the contrastive topic (wa...n da) redefines life itself through the lens of dependency; and the adjectival suffix (-teki) characterizes souls with clinical precision. Pay attention to how these constructions work together to create a prose that sounds like reportage while delivering analysis at the depth of theology — the voice of a novelist who has learned that the most disturbing truths are best delivered in the grammar of everyday speech.
