Title Analysis

Title Grammar: 韓国嫁入り日記

The title Kankoku Yomeiri Nikki (韓国嫁入り日記) is a compound noun that compresses an entire life transformation into five kanji. Kankoku (韓国) is "Korea / South Korea" — the destination, the foreign country, the place where Japanese common sense no longer applies. Yomeiri (嫁入り) is one of the most culturally specific words in Japanese: it does not simply mean "marriage" (kekkon / 結婚) but specifically "marrying into one's husband's family" — the traditional practice whereby a bride leaves her natal home and enters her husband's household as a daughter-in-law. The word carries the weight of patrilocal marriage, of filial obligation, of becoming yome (嫁, "bride / daughter-in-law") to a mother-in-law (shutome / 姑 or omma / オンマ). Nikki (日記) is "diary / journal" — the daily record, the accumulation of small observations that becomes, over time, a portrait of a life. The grammar is simple: three nouns in apposition, [country] + [marriage-type] + [diary]. But the simplicity is deceptive. The title announces not merely a memoir of marrying a Korean man but a specific kind of memoir: the diary of someone who became a yome in Korea — someone who entered a Korean family not as an equal partner but as a daughter-in-law, with all the cultural expectations, linguistic challenges, and domestic negotiations that role entails. The title's grammar performs the book's central experience: the narrator's identity is modified by place (Korea) and role (bride/daughter-in-law) before it becomes a narrative (diary).

Title Breakdown

韓国 Kankoku "Korea / South Korea" — The country that modifies everything that follows. In Japanese, Kankoku (韓国, "Korea") is written with the kanji 韓 (one of the ancient Three Kingdoms of Korea) and 国 (country / nation). For Japanese readers, Kankoku evokes a complex set of associations: the geographically closest foreign country, the source of the "Korean Wave" (Hallyu / 韓流) in pop culture, a nation with a shared history of both conflict and cultural exchange with Japan, and increasingly, a popular travel destination and source of fashion, cosmetics, and food trends. But the title does not say "Korean" (Kankoku no / 韓国の) as an adjective; it says Kankoku as a noun placed directly before yomeiri, creating a compound in which Korea is not merely the setting but the grammatical modifier of the marriage itself. This is not a diary of marriage that happens to take place in Korea; it is a diary of "Korea-marriage" — a distinct category, a particular type of cross-cultural union. The juxtaposition of Kankoku and yomeiri also carries a faint echo of historical resonance: during the colonial period (1910-1945), marriages between Japanese and Koreans were common but politically charged, and in the postwar period, they have been shaped by diplomatic tensions, cultural stereotyping, and the gradual normalization of Korean pop culture in Japan. By 2017, when this book was published, a Japanese woman marrying a Korean man was no longer unusual — but it was still a story, still a crossing of boundaries that required explanation and narration.
嫁入り yomeiri "marrying into husband's family / bride's entry" — This is the emotional and cultural core of the title. Yomeiri (嫁入り) combines yome (嫁, "bride / daughter-in-law") and iri (入り, "entering / entry"), and it refers specifically to the traditional Japanese practice of patrilocal marriage: the bride enters (iri) her husband's household and becomes a daughter-in-law (yome) to his parents. The word is distinct from kekkon (結婚), the modern, legally neutral term for "marriage" that implies partnership and equality. Yomeiri carries the weight of tradition, of family hierarchy, of the bride as someone who must learn the ways of a new household. In contemporary Japanese, yomeiri is still used but has a slightly old-fashioned or rural flavor — it evokes the world of traditional family structures, of ie (家, "house / household") and kazoku (家族, "family"), where the daughter-in-law's position is defined by service and adaptation. When Matsuda uses yomeiri rather than kekkon, she is signaling that her experience is not merely a love marriage across national boundaries but a traditional daughter-in-law's entry into a Korean family — with a Korean mother-in-law (omma / オンマ), Korean food, Korean language, and Korean domestic expectations. The word also resonates with the Korean equivalent, si-ga jip-deul-i (시집들이), the bride's entry into her husband's family home — a concept that is if anything even more strongly emphasized in Korean culture than in Japanese. The title thus uses a Japanese word for a practice that is equally Korean, creating a cross-linguistic recognition: what Japanese call yomeiri, Koreans experience as si-ga jip-deul-i, and both cultures understand the mixture of love, anxiety, comedy, and endurance that the word contains.
日記 nikki "diary / journal / daily record" — The final element transforms the preceding compound into a narrative genre. Nikki (日記) combines nichi (日, "day / sun") and ki (記, "record / account / note"), and it refers to the diary as a literary form — one of the oldest and most intimate in Japanese literature, from the Heian-period Kagero Nikki (蜻蛉日記, "The Gossamer Years") and Izumi Shikibu Nikki (和泉式部日記, "The Diary of Izumi Shikibu") to the modern blog. The choice of nikki rather than essai (エッセイ, "essay") or tales (物語) is significant: a diary is daily, fragmentary, personal, and cumulative. It does not claim the authority of analysis or the coherence of a constructed narrative; it claims only the authenticity of lived experience, recorded day by day. The diary form also creates a particular relationship with the reader: we read a diary as voyeurs, peering into someone's private record, and the intimacy of the form generates both comedy (the small absurdities of daily life) and emotion (the accumulation of small struggles into a portrait of resilience). By calling her book a nikki, Matsuda places herself in a long tradition of Japanese women writers who have used the diary form to record their negotiations with family, society, and self — from the aristocratic women of the Heian court to the shishosetsu (私小説, "I-novel") writers of the modern period. Her nikki is a working woman's diary, a mother's diary, a foreign bride's diary — but it participates in the same tradition of using daily record as a form of self-assertion and cultural commentary.
Literary context: The title's structure — [foreign country] + [yomeiri] + [nikki] — places the book in a recognizable genre of Japanese women's writing about cross-cultural marriage. It echoes titles like Chugoku no Yome ni Narimashita (中国の嫁になりました, "I Became a Bride in China") and anticipates the flood of "international marriage" memoirs that followed the Korean Wave. But Matsuda's title is more specific and more traditional than many: by using yomeiri rather than kekkon, she signals that her story is not a romance but a domestic ethnography — the record of a woman learning to live in a family that is not her own, in a country that is not hers, in a language she is still acquiring. The diary form makes this ethnography personal rather than academic: we learn about Korea not from a guidebook but from a woman who burned the first pot of rice she tried to cook for her mother-in-law.
Author

松田 水菜子 — Matsuda Minako

松

松田 水菜子

Matsuda Minako · b. 1978 · Nara / New York / Ulsan

Matsuda Minako is a Japanese essayist, blogger, and cross-cultural communicator whose life trajectory reads like a map of contemporary global mobility: born in Nara Prefecture, educated in Osaka and Alabama, employed in New York, married in Korea, and writing for readers across East Asia. Born in 1978 in Nara — Japan's ancient capital, land of temples and deer — she grew up with the Kansai sensibility that permeates her prose: direct, funny, self-deprecating, and alert to the absurdities of social pretension. She entered Otani Women's University (大谷女子大学) in Osaka, majoring in English Literature in the Faculty of Letters, and during her studies she participated in an exchange program at the University of Alabama — her first experience of living in a foreign country, and the beginning of a pattern that would define her life: leaving Japan, adapting to somewhere else, and writing about the gap between the two.

After graduating, she moved to New York City, where she worked in internships and developed the English fluency that would later become her professional foundation. New York in the early 2000s — the city of 9/11 and its aftermath, of gentrification and globalization — shaped her understanding of what it means to be a foreigner in a metropolis: the daily small humiliations and triumphs of operating in a second language, the discovery that competence in one culture does not translate automatically into competence in another. But New York was only a waystation. Through circumstances that the book describes with characteristic humor, she found herself in South Korea, working as a Japanese language teacher. And there, in a classroom in Ulsan (蔚山 / 울산), a port city on South Korea's southeastern coast, she met him: a younger Korean student, a "powerful Korean man" (Kankoku no pawafulu danshi / 韓国のパワフル男子) who would become her husband, the father of her children, and the reason she has spent over fifteen years living as a Kankoku yome (韓国嫁, "Korean bride").

Ulsan is not Seoul. It is not the Korea of K-dramas and K-pop, of Gangnam glamour and Han River romance. It is an industrial city, home to Hyundai's shipyards and automobile plants, a working-class metropolis where the rhythms of life are shaped by factory shifts and family networks rather than by the global cultural industries that have made Korea famous. This is the Korea that Matsuda writes about: not the Korea of tourist brochures but the Korea of mother-in-laws and morning markets, of kimchi refrigerators and heated floors (ondol), of family hierarchies that a Japanese woman from Nara must learn to navigate with a mixture of bewilderment, defiance, and growing affection. Her blog, "Kankoku Yomeiri Nikki" (韓国嫁入り日記), launched in 2014 and became a cult hit — readers were drawn to her frank, funny accounts of life with her omma (mother-in-law), her "darling" husband, and her "prince" son. The book, published by Poem Piece in November 2017, collected and expanded these blog entries into a coherent narrative of cultural adaptation.

In 2025, the book was adapted into a four-panel manga (yonkoma / 四コマ版), illustrated by Takada Kimiko and published by Mirai Publishing — a testament to the book's popularity and its visual, comic potential. The crowdfunding campaign for the manga edition raised 1,138,550 yen (113% of its goal) from 157 backers, demonstrating the loyal readership Matsuda has built over years of blogging. Today, in her fifteenth year of Korean marriage, she continues to write her blog, now also working as a certified ki (氣) practitioner under the name MINA, bringing the same openness to spiritual exploration that she has brought to cross-cultural marriage. What distinguishes Matsuda as a writer is her refusal to perform either tragedy or romance. She does not present her marriage as a sacrifice or a fairy tale; she presents it as life — complicated, funny, sometimes exhausting, and ultimately chosen, day after day, in a country that remains, as she puts it, "close and far" (chikakute tooi kuni / 近くて遠い国).

Cast & Contents

Characters & Chapters

The People in Matsuda's Korean Life

ミナコ（松田水菜子）

Minako / The Narrator

The protagonist and narrator — a Japanese woman from Nara who finds herself, through a chain of improbable decisions (New York, Japanese teacher in Korea, romance with a student), living as a daughter-in-law in Ulsan. Her Kansai dialect inflections, her self-deprecating humor, and her refusal to romanticize or demonize her experience make her an unusually grounded narrator of cross-cultural marriage. She is neither victim nor heroine; she is simply a woman trying to cook edible rice for her mother-in-law while remaining herself.

ダーリン（年下の韓国人夫）

Darling / The Husband

Matsuda's husband, whom she calls "Darling" (dārin / ダーリン) — a term that is simultaneously affectionate and slightly ironic, capturing the mixture of romance and comedy in their relationship. He is younger than her (年下, toshi-shita), a "powerful Korean man" (pawafulu danshi / パワフル男子) whose energy and confidence both attract and exasperate her. As a Korean husband, he embodies the cultural expectations that Matsuda must navigate: filial piety toward his mother, patriarchal assumptions about household roles, and a deep, unspoken emotional bond with his family that a Japanese wife must learn to understand rather than judge.

オンマ（姑・義母）

Omma / The Mother-in-Law

The book's most vivid character — the Korean mother-in-law whom Matsuda calls omma (オンマ, the Korean word for "mother," rather than the Japanese shutome / 姑 or okāsan / お母さん). The choice of word is significant: by calling her mother-in-law omma, Matsuda signals both her integration into the family and the particular intensity of the Korean mother-son relationship that she must navigate. The omma is the gravitational center of the Korean family — the one who cooks, commands, criticizes, and ultimately loves with a ferocity that Matsuda gradually learns to read as affection. Their relationship is the book's central drama: not a simple battle but a slow, comic, sometimes painful negotiation between two women from different cultures who both love the same man.

プリンス（息子）

Prince / The Son

Matsuda's son, nicknamed "Prince" — a term that captures both maternal adoration and gentle mockery of the child's privileged position in a Korean-Japanese household. The "prince" is also a cultural type in Korea: the beloved son, the carrier of the family line, the one around whom grandmother's love revolves most intensely. Matsuda's observations of her son growing up between two languages and two cultures provide some of the book's most tender and thought-provoking moments.

Eight Chapters

第1章 韓国との運命の出合い

Chapter 1: A Fateful Encounter with Korea

How a woman from Nara, via Osaka and New York, ended up in a Japanese language classroom in Ulsan — and fell in love with a student. The chapter that explains the improbable chain of decisions that led to her Korean marriage.

第2章 ダーリンとのあま〜い新婚生活。そして、箱入り息子誕生

Chapter 2: Sweet Newlywed Life with Darling — and the Birth of a Boxed-In Son

The honeymoon period and its collision with reality: pregnancy, childbirth, and the discovery that a Korean family does not consist of two people but of an entire network of relations, with the mother-in-law at the center.

第3章 韓国のナナフシギ

Chapter 3: Korea's Seven Wonders / Strange Things

The small astonishments of daily life in Korea — the customs, habits, and assumptions that seem perfectly normal to Koreans and utterly baffling to a Japanese newcomer. The comedy of cultural disorientation at its finest.

第4章 大韓民国は、オンマ（お母さん）を中心に回っている

Chapter 4: The Republic of Korea Revolves Around Omma

The central thesis of the book, stated in the chapter title: Korean family life is organized around the mother. This chapter explores the power dynamics, emotional bonds, and daily negotiations of living with a Korean mother-in-law.

第5章 韓国の伝統文化と食を知ろう

Chapter 5: Learning Korean Traditional Culture and Food

From kimchi to ancestral rites, from table manners to holiday customs — Matsuda's education in the cultural practices that define Korean family identity. Food is both barrier and bridge.

第6章 知ってビックリ玉手箱

Chapter 6: A Box of Surprises — Things That Shock You When You Know Them

The deeper discoveries: the social rules, gender expectations, and unspoken codes that only become visible after years of living in Korea. The tamatebako (玉手箱, "jeweled box" of surprises) from the Urashima Taro legend — open it and reality changes.

第7章 近くて遠い国といわれるワケ

Chapter 7: Why They Call It a Close Yet Distant Country

The book's reflective core — an exploration of why Japan and Korea, geographically adjacent and culturally intertwined, remain so psychologically distant. History, politics, media representation, and personal experience all converge in this chapter.

第8章 韓国人の体は情でできている

Chapter 8: Koreans Are Made of Emotion

The final chapter's poetic assertion: Korean identity is defined not by logic or rules but by jō (情, "emotion / human feeling / compassion"). Matsuda's ultimate discovery is that understanding Korea requires understanding its emotional logic — a logic that is different from Japan's but no less valid.

Story

Synopsis

From Nara to Ulsan: An Improbable Journey

The book opens with the narrator's astonishment at her own life: "Masaka watashi ga Kankoku ni yome ni kuru to wa!" (「まさか私が韓国に嫁にくるとは！」 — "I can't believe I ended up marrying into Korea!"). This exclamation, repeated like a refrain throughout the book, captures the tone of Matsuda's narrative: not regret, not triumph, but amazed acceptance. She did not plan to marry a Korean man. She did not plan to live in Ulsan. She did not plan to become a daughter-in-law to a Korean mother. These things happened, step by step, decision by decision, and the book is the record of what she made of them.

The first chapters trace her trajectory: Nara childhood, Osaka university, Alabama exchange, New York internships, Korean teaching job, classroom romance, marriage, pregnancy. Each step is narrated with the same combination of humor and honesty. The New York chapter establishes her credentials as a global citizen — she is not a naive provincial who stumbled into a foreign marriage; she is a woman who chose to live abroad, who has experienced life in multiple countries, who understands what foreignness feels like. This matters because it establishes her authority: when she describes the difficulties of Korean family life, she is not complaining about the exotic; she is comparing it, implicitly, to her experiences in America and her knowledge of Japan. The comparison makes her observations specific rather than generic, grounded rather than touristic.

The Omma Problem

The heart of the book is the narrator's relationship with her mother-in-law — the omma who is simultaneously the obstacle and the key to her happiness in Korea. In Japanese culture, the mother-in-law (shutome / 姑) is a classic figure of domestic tension, the subject of countless folk tales and TV dramas. In Korean culture, the relationship between daughter-in-law (myeonuri / 며느리) and mother-in-law (si-eomoni / 시어머니) is if anything even more culturally charged — governed by expectations of filial service, culinary competence, and emotional deference that can feel overwhelming to a modern Japanese woman.

Matsuda does not simplify this relationship into conflict or harmony. She describes the daily negotiations: the omma's criticism of her cooking, her own gradual improvement, the small victories (a meal praised, a chore completed correctly) and the ongoing failures (language mistakes, cultural faux pas, moments of homesickness). She writes about the omma's love for her son and grandson with genuine appreciation, even as she struggles with the way that love constrains her own autonomy. The chapter title "The Republic of Korea Revolves Around Omma" is not a complaint; it is an observation, offered with the humor of someone who has learned to live within a system she did not create. The book's achievement is to make the omma comprehensible — not as a monster or a saint, but as a woman with her own history, her own losses, her own fierce love for her family.

Food, Language, and the Body

Three domains structure Matsuda's account of cultural difference: food, language, and the body. Food is the most immediate: the kimchi refrigerator that every Korean household possesses, the elaborate preparation of side dishes (banchan / パンチャン), the expectation that the daughter-in-law will participate in cooking, the humiliation of burned rice. Matsuda writes about Korean food with the precision of someone learning it from the inside — not as a restaurant customer but as a family cook, responsible for reproducing dishes that carry generations of meaning. Her failures are comic; her gradual competence is quietly triumphant.

Language is the deeper barrier. Matsuda speaks Japanese and English; her husband and his family speak Korean. The book records her struggles with Korean — not just vocabulary and grammar but the emotional registers of a language, the ways of expressing affection, complaint, and respect that differ from Japanese. She notes the moments when language fails her: when she cannot express her frustration, when she misunderstands a tone, when a joke falls flat. But she also records the moments when language succeeds: when she makes her mother-in-law laugh, when she comforts her husband in his own language, when she realizes that her children are growing up bilingual in a way she never did.

The body is the most intimate domain. Matsuda writes about pregnancy and childbirth in a Korean hospital, about the postpartum recovery practices (sanhu jori / 産後調理) that Korean culture prescribes, about the way her Japanese body learned to desire Korean food and tolerate Korean spice. The body becomes the site of cultural transformation: it is through her body — eating, giving birth, aging — that she most deeply becomes someone who lives in Korea rather than merely visiting it.

Close and Far: Japan and Korea

The book's penultimate chapter addresses the political and historical dimension of Matsuda's personal story. Japan and Korea are "close yet far" (chikakute tooi kuni / 近くて遠い国) — geographically adjacent, culturally intertwined, yet separated by history, politics, and media-fueled mutual stereotyping. Matsuda writes about the moments when this macro-political reality intrudes on her micro-domestic life: a news report about Dokdo/Takeshima, a relative's comment about "those people," a Korean friend's assumption about Japanese women. She does not offer a political solution; she offers a domestic one. By living with a Korean family, by loving a Korean man, by raising Korean-Japanese children, she has created a small zone of connection in a landscape of official tension. The book's final assertion — "Koreans are made of emotion" (Kankokujin no karada wa jō de dekite iru / 韓国人の体は情でできている) — is not a stereotype; it is a discovery, earned through years of learning to read the emotional cues of a culture that expresses feeling differently from Japan. The Japanese value enryo (遠慮, "restraint / reserve") and kuuki wo yomu (空気を読む, "reading the air"); Koreans, Matsuda discovers, value direct expression, passionate argument, and visible affection. Neither is better; both are ways of being human. The book ends not with resolution but with continuation — she is still living in Korea, still learning, still writing her diary.

Themes

  • Cross-cultural marriage as daily negotiation: The book's central theme is that cross-cultural marriage is not a dramatic clash of civilizations but a continuous, low-intensity negotiation about food, language, family roles, and emotional expression. The drama is in the dailiness.
  • The mother-in-law as culture-bearer: The omma is not merely a character but a representative of Korean family culture. Through her, Matsuda learns not just recipes but values — what Korean families expect, demand, and give in return.
  • Language as identity: The book explores how speaking (or not speaking) a language shapes one's sense of self. Matsuda's Korean improves; her children's bilingualism raises questions about belonging; her Japanese remains her inner voice.
  • Food as cultural code: Every meal is a lesson in cultural difference. The kimchi refrigerator, the banchan array, the proper way to cook rice — these are not just culinary details but expressions of family identity and filial obligation.
  • Humor as survival strategy: Matsuda's humor is not an escape from difficulty but a way of processing it. By making her struggles funny, she makes them bearable — for herself and for her readers. The comedy is the book's ethical achievement: it refuses to turn cross-cultural marriage into either tragedy or romance.
Text

Key Passages

These passages capture the book's tonal range — from the opening exclamation of astonishment to the philosophical conclusion about Korean emotional culture.

01
The Opening Cry
「まさか私が韓国に嫁にくるとは！」「隣の国なのに全然知らなかった！」
"Masaka watashi ga Kankoku ni yome ni kuru to wa!" "Tonari no kuni na no ni zenzen shiranakatta!"
"I can't believe I ended up marrying into Korea!" "It's the country next door, and I knew nothing about it!"

Nuance Note

These two exclamations, which appear on the book's cover and in its promotional copy, function as a combined overture to the entire narrative. The first — "Masaka watashi ga Kankoku ni yome ni kuru to wa!" — is a masterpiece of compressed emotional grammar. Masaka (まさか) is an adverb of disbelief, meaning "no way / it can't be / surely not" — it expresses astonishment at something that has actually happened, the shock of reality exceeding imagination. The grammar ...to wa (とは) after a verb phrase creates an exclamatory emphasis: yome ni kuru to wa (嫁にくるとは) means not merely "I married into Korea" but "that I should marry into Korea!" — the to wa carries a sense of ironic destiny, of the universe playing a joke. The phrase yome ni kuru (嫁にくる) uses the directional verb kuru (来る, "to come") rather than iku (行く, "to go"), and this is crucial: kuru implies movement toward the speaker's current location or toward a destination conceived as "here" rather than "there." By saying she "came" (kuru) as a bride to Korea, Matsuda subtly frames Korea as her present reality, the place she now is, rather than a distant "there" she went to. The second exclamation — "Tonari no kuni na no ni zenzen shiranakatta!" (「隣の国なのに全然知らなかった！」) — introduces the book's central paradox. Tonari no kuni (隣の国, "the country next door") establishes geographical intimacy: Korea is close, visible, reachable. Na no ni (なのに, "and yet / despite the fact that") creates a concessive contrast — the grammar of disappointed expectation. Zenzen shiranakatta (全然知らなかった, "I knew nothing at all / I had no idea") uses the emphatic negative zenzen (全然, "at all / completely") with the past negative shiranakatta (知らなかった, "did not know") to create an absolute negation: not "I knew a little" but "I knew nothing." The paradox is political and personal: how can a Japanese person know so little about the country that is closest to Japan? The answer the book offers is that geographical proximity does not guarantee cultural understanding — and that the "Korea" Japanese people think they know (from K-dramas, news, or stereotypes) is not the Korea of daily family life. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese shame embedded in zenzen shiranakatta — the admission of ignorance about something one feels one should have known. In Japanese culture, where keigo (敬語, honorific language) requires knowing the social position of everyone one speaks to, admitting ignorance about an entire country carries a particular weight of self-criticism.

02
The Republic of Omma
大韓民国は、オンマ（お母さん）を中心に回っている。
"Daikanminkoku wa, omma (okāsan) wo chūshin ni mawatte iru."
"The Republic of Korea revolves around Omma (Mother)."

Nuance Note

This chapter title is the book's most famous line — a sentence that has become a shorthand for understanding Korean family structure among Japanese readers. The grammar is deceptively simple: Daikanminkoku wa (大韓民国は, "as for the Republic of Korea") + omma (okāsan) wo chūshin ni (オンマ（お母さん）を中心に, "with Omma (Mother) as the center") + mawatte iru (回っている, "is revolving / turning"). The verb mawatte iru (回っている) is the progressive form of mawaru (回る, "to turn / to revolve / to go around"), and it creates a cosmic metaphor: the Korean nation is not merely organized around mothers; it revolves around them like planets around a sun. The grammatical construction wo chūshin ni (を中心に, "with ~ as the center / focusing on ~") is a standard Japanese pattern for describing organization or structure, but applied to a nation and a mother, it becomes something more — a sociological claim rendered in the grammar of family intimacy. The parenthetical okāsan (お母さん) after omma is a crucial translation device: Matsuda knows that her Japanese readers may not know the Korean word omma (엄마), so she provides the Japanese equivalent. But the equivalence is not exact. Omma in Korean carries a particular emotional weight — it is the word children use for their mothers throughout their lives, not a term that shifts to more formal language in adulthood as Japanese often does. By keeping omma as the primary term and adding okāsan as a gloss, Matsuda performs a small act of cultural translation: she introduces a Korean concept through Japanese grammar while preserving its foreignness. What is lost in English is the double recognition the Japanese reader experiences: seeing omma and learning a Korean word, while seeing okāsan in parentheses and understanding its emotional range. The English "Omma (Mother)" flattens this double vision into a single label. The sentence's power in Japanese comes from its audacity: to say that an entire nation "revolves around" mothers is to make a domestic observation into a political theory. Matsuda is not being entirely serious — the tone is comic, hyperbolic — but the hyperbole contains a truth that Korean readers recognize and Japanese readers find illuminating. The grammar of wo chūshin ni mawatte iru makes the mother not merely important but gravitational, the center of a field of force that holds the family — and by extension, the nation — together.

03
Close and Far
近くて遠い国といわれるワケ
"Chikakute tooi kuni to iwareru wake."
"The reason they call it a close yet distant country."

Nuance Note

This chapter title contains one of the most frequently used phrases in Japanese discourse about Korea: chikakute tooi kuni (近くて遠い国, "a close yet distant country"). The phrase is so common that it has become a cliché — and Matsuda's chapter title acknowledges this by using the passive to iwareru (といわれる, "is said to be / they call it") rather than asserting the description herself. The grammar ...to iwareru wake (…といわれるワケ, "the reason why it is said that...") creates a meta-discursive frame: the chapter will not simply describe Korea as close and far but will explain why people say this, what produces the perception, and whether it is accurate. The phrase chikakute tooi (近くて遠い) uses the continuative te-form of chikai (近い, "close / near") connected to tooi (遠い, "far / distant") — a paradox compressed into a single adjectival compound. The te-form here functions not as a sequential connector ("close and then far") but as a coordinate connector ("close yet far"), creating an oxymoron that captures the paradox of Japan-Korea relations. Geographically, Korea is Japan's nearest neighbor; historically and emotionally, it can feel impossibly distant. The word kuni (国, "country / nation") is simple but carries weight: this is not merely a personal observation but a national characterization. What is lost in English is the history embedded in the phrase chikakute tooi kuni. Japanese readers have encountered this description in newspapers, textbooks, and political speeches for decades. It is the standard journalistic formula for describing the ambivalence of Japan-Korea relations — the geographical intimacy that makes conflict painful and the historical distance that makes reconciliation difficult. By using this phrase as a chapter title, Matsuda places her personal story within a national narrative. Her marriage is not merely a private romance; it is a small experiment in closing the distance that the phrase describes. The chapter promises to explain the wake (ワケ, "reason / cause") — a word that in casual Japanese suggests not logical causation but narrative explanation, the "why" of a story rather than the "because" of an argument. This is characteristic of Matsuda's method: she does not analyze Japan-Korea relations politically; she explains them through the accumulation of domestic detail — a burned pot of rice, a misunderstood joke, a moment of shared laughter over kimchi.

04
Made of Emotion
韓国人の体は情でできてる。
"Kankokujin no karada wa jō de dekite iru."
"Koreans are made of emotion."

Nuance Note

The book's final chapter title is its most poetic assertion — a sentence that moves from observation to philosophy, from memoir to cultural theory. The grammar is a classic Japanese descriptive pattern: Kankokujin no karada wa (韓国人の体は, "as for the bodies of Koreans / Koreans' physical selves") + jō de dekite iru (情でできている, "are made of / constructed from emotion"). The verb dekite iru (できている) is the progressive form of dekiru (できる), which means both "to be able to" and "to be made / to be completed" — in the de-form + iru construction, it describes a state of completed composition: "is constituted by / is made from." The pattern X wa Y de dekite iru (XはYでできている) is the standard way to describe material composition in Japanese — kono tēburu wa ki de dekite iru (このテーブルは木でできている, "this table is made of wood"). By using this pattern with jō (情, "emotion / feeling / human compassion") as the material, Matsuda makes a striking ontological claim: Koreans are not merely emotional; they are constituted by emotion in the same way a table is constituted by wood. The word jō (情) is one of the most important and untranslatable words in East Asian philosophy. It means "emotion" but also "human feeling," "compassion," "empathy," and "the bonds between people." It is distinct from kanjō (感情, "feeling / sentiment"), which is more individual and psychological; jō is relational and ethical. In Chinese philosophy, qing (情) is one of the fundamental categories of human existence, alongside li (理, "principle / reason"). Matsuda's claim that Koreans are "made of jō" is thus not merely a stereotype about emotional expressiveness; it is a philosophical characterization of Korean culture as one that privileges relational emotion over individual rationality. The English "Koreans are made of emotion" captures the meaning but loses the philosophical resonance of jō and the material metaphor of de dekite iru. In Japanese, the sentence sounds like a discovery — the conclusion of fifteen years of learning to read a culture's emotional cues. It is also, implicitly, a comparison: if Koreans are made of jō, what are Japanese made of? The book never answers this question, but the implication hovers: perhaps Japanese are made of enryo (遠慮, "restraint"), or kuuki (空気, "air / atmosphere / unspoken context"), or some other substance that makes direct emotional expression difficult. The sentence thus functions as both conclusion and invitation — an invitation to Japanese readers to consider what they themselves are made of, and whether the Korean model of emotional directness might offer something they need.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Matsuda's prose in Kankoku Yomeiri Nikki is deceptively casual — the voice of a Kansai woman chatting with friends, full of exclamations, comic asides, and the particular intimacy of blog writing. Much of what makes her voice distinctive is embedded in Japanese dialect, family terminology, and the cultural specificity of cross-cultural marriage.

1. Yomeiri (嫁入り) vs. Kekkon (結婚) as Cultural Role

JP 韓国に嫁にくる / 韓国嫁入り日記
EN "marrying into Korea" / "Korean marriage diary"
Loss The distinction between yomeiri (嫁入り) and kekkon (結婚) is central to the book's meaning and cannot be preserved in English because English has no equivalent word for yomeiri. Kekkon (結婚) is the modern, legally neutral term for marriage — it implies partnership, equality, and individual choice. Yomeiri (嫁入り) is the traditional term for patrilocal marriage — it implies the bride's entry into her husband's family, her subordination to her mother-in-law, and her responsibility to learn the ways of a new household. When Matsuda says she "came as a bride" (yome ni kuru / 嫁にくる) to Korea, she is not merely saying she married a Korean man; she is saying she entered a Korean family structure with specific expectations about daughter-in-law behavior. The English "marrying into" captures some of this directionality but loses the specifically Japanese (and Korean) cultural weight of the yome role. In Japanese, yome is a word with centuries of literary and folkloric resonance — the suffering daughter-in-law is a stock figure in folk tales, and the yome's gradual acceptance into the family is a classic narrative arc. By calling herself a yome, Matsuda places herself in this tradition while also subverting it: she is a modern, university-educated, globally mobile woman who has chosen this role, not a village girl given in marriage by her parents. The English reader who sees "marriage" receives none of this cultural layering. What is lost is the sense that Matsuda's experience is not merely personal but archetypal — a modern version of one of the oldest stories in East Asian family life.

2. Omma (オンマ) as Untranslatable Kinship

JP オンマ（お母さん）を中心に回っている
EN "revolves around Omma (Mother)"
Loss Omma (엄마) is the Korean word for "mother," and its use in a Japanese text is already a translation — a carrying-across of Korean family intimacy into Japanese prose. But the translation is not complete. Omma in Korean carries a specific emotional register: it is what children call their mothers from infancy through adulthood, a word of absolute intimacy and dependency. Japanese adults typically shift to more formal or indirect ways of referring to their mothers (okāsan / お母さん when speaking to her, haha / 母 when referring to her formally). By using omma as the primary term and adding okāsan in parentheses as a gloss, Matsuda does something subtle and important: she respects the Korean word's primacy in her family life while making it comprehensible to Japanese readers. The English translation must choose: "Omma (Mother)" preserves the foreign word but loses the kinship nuance; "Mother" loses the foreignness entirely. What is lost in either case is the experience of learning a family member's name in a foreign language and discovering that the foreign word carries emotions the native word cannot quite capture. Matsuda's use of omma throughout the book is a small act of linguistic immigration: she has not merely married into a Korean family; she has learned to call her mother-in-law by a Korean name, and in doing so, she has partially become Korean herself. The English reader sees a word in parentheses; the Japanese reader sees a woman transforming her vocabulary to match her transformed life.

3. Chikakute Tooi Kuni (近くて遠い国) as Political Cliché and Personal Truth

JP 近くて遠い国といわれるワケ
EN "The reason they call it a close yet distant country"
Loss The phrase chikakute tooi kuni (近くて遠い国) is so common in Japanese discourse about Korea that it functions as a shared cultural reference — a cliché that everyone recognizes and that writers invoke with a mixture of irony and resignation. The English "close yet distant country" translates the meaning but loses the cliché-status, the history of repetition, the sense that this phrase has been uttered by politicians, journalists, and ordinary people for decades. In Japanese, the phrase carries the weight of postwar Japan-Korea relations: the 1965 normalization treaty, the comfort women issue, the Dokdo/Takeshima dispute, the "history wars" over textbooks, the cycles of diplomatic tension and cultural rapprochement. Every time a Japanese person says chikakute tooi kuni, they are participating in a national conversation about a relationship that remains unresolved. Matsuda's chapter title uses the passive to iwareru (といわれる, "is said to be / they call it") to create distance from the cliché — she is not asserting the description herself but investigating why others use it. This grammatical choice is crucial: it allows her to both acknowledge the political discourse and claim a personal perspective on it. The English "they call it" captures the passive but loses the specific history of who "they" are — Japanese politicians, media commentators, and ordinary citizens who have been calling Korea a "close yet distant country" since the 1960s. What is also lost is the emotional undertone of chikakute tooi as a description of failed intimacy. In Japanese, the phrase sounds sad — the sadness of neighbors who cannot quite become friends, of cousins who share a grandfather but have grown apart. The English "close yet distant" is accurate but clinical; the Japanese chikakute tooi is a sigh.

4. Jō (情) as Philosophical Untranslatability

JP 韓国人の体は情でできている
EN "Koreans are made of emotion"
Loss Jō (情) is one of the most philosophically dense words in East Asian culture and one of the most difficult to translate into English. It means "emotion," but also "human feeling," "compassion," "empathy," "the bonds that connect people," and "the quality of being fully human." In Chinese philosophy, qing (情) is one of the fundamental categories of existence, alongside li (理, "principle / reason") and qi (氣, "vital energy"). In Japanese, jō appears in compounds like jōhō (情愛, "affectionate love"), jōnin (人, "lover / sweetheart"), and jōzu (情緒, "emotion / pathos / lyricism"). It is distinct from kanjō (感情, "feeling / sentiment"), which refers to individual emotional states, and from ai (愛, "love"), which is more universal and abstract. Jō is specifically relational: it is the feeling that exists between people, the compassion that motivates care, the emotional intelligence that allows one to read another's heart. When Matsuda says Koreans are "made of jō," she is making a claim that is simultaneously psychological, philosophical, and cultural. The English "made of emotion" sounds like a pop-psychology generalization; the Japanese jō de dekite iru sounds like a discovery earned through years of intimate observation. The material metaphor (de dekite iru, "made from / constituted by") is also lost in translation: "made of emotion" in English suggests composition, while jō de dekite iru in Japanese suggests essence — not merely that Koreans are emotional but that jō is their foundational substance, what you would find if you analyzed them to their core. What is lost is the philosophical depth of the claim and the specifically East Asian tradition of thought that makes jō a category of human flourishing rather than merely a psychological trait.

5. Kansai Dialect and Blog Intimacy

JP 関西弁の語尾、擬態語、ブログ調の親密さ
EN "Kansai dialect endings, mimetic words, blog-style intimacy"
Loss Matsuda's prose carries the rhythms of Kansai dialect even when she writes in standard Japanese — the directness, the comic timing, the self-deprecating humor that Kansai people use as a social lubricant. The blog form amplifies this intimacy: blog writing in Japanese is characterized by short paragraphs, frequent exclamation marks, conversational particles like na (な) and yona (よな), and a direct address to the reader that creates the feeling of chatting with a friend. English has no equivalent register for Japanese blog prose — the closest might be a particularly confessional lifestyle blogger, but even that lacks the specific cultural coding of Japanese niki (日記, diary) writing. What is lost is the sense of eavesdropping on a conversation among Japanese women — the kūki (空気, "atmosphere") of shared recognition when Matsuda describes a mother-in-law's criticism or a husband's obliviousness. The English reader receives information; the Japanese reader receives community. The mimetic words (giongo / 擬音語 and gitaigo / 擬態語) that pepper Matsuda's prose — words like monzetsu (悶絶, "writhing in agony," used comically) and bikkuri (ビックリ, "surprise") — create a sound-texture that makes the prose feel alive and bodily. English can translate the meanings but not the sonic intimacy: a Japanese reader hears Matsuda's voice in these words, while an English reader reads descriptions of her experience. The blog origins of the book also mean that much of its charm depends on the illusion of immediacy — the sense that these words were written today, for you, about a life that is still ongoing. The book format removes some of this immediacy, but the prose retains the breathless, confessional quality of good blog writing. English translation would need to invent an equivalent voice, and whatever voice it invented would be culturally specific in ways that obscured the Japanese original.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns from Matsuda's exclamatory prose, her chapter titles, and the conversational grammar of blog-style memoir.

〜とは（驚き・感嘆・意外） N3

...to wa: exclamatory emphasis — "that ~ should... / to think that ~"

まさか私が韓国に嫁にくるとは！

"I can't believe [to think] that I should marry into Korea!" — The to wa construction turns a statement into an exclamation of astonishment, carrying the sense of ironic destiny.

〜なのに（譲歩・対照） N4

Na no ni: "despite the fact that / and yet / even though" — concessive contrast expressing disappointed expectation.

隣の国なのに全然知らなかった！

"It's the country next door, and yet I knew nothing about it!" — The na no ni construction creates the grammar of surprised disappointment at the gap between expectation and reality.

〜を中心に（組織・構成） N3

Wo chūshin ni: "with ~ as the center / centering on ~ / focusing on ~"

オンマを中心に回っている

"revolves around Omma" — The wo chūshin ni pattern describes organizational structure; applied to a mother and a nation, it creates a cosmic metaphor of gravitational centrality.

〜ている（進行・継続・習慣） N5

Te iru: progressive / continuous / habitual aspect — describing ongoing states and actions.

大韓民国は…回っている / 韓国人の体は…できている

"is revolving / is made of" — The progressive te iru form turns verbs into descriptions of ongoing states: the nation that is always revolving, the body that is constituted (and remains constituted) by emotion.

〜でできている（材料・構成） N4

De dekite iru: "is made of / is constituted from / is composed of [material]"

情でできている

"is made of emotion" — The pattern X wa Y de dekite iru describes material composition; using it with an abstract noun (jō) creates an ontological claim about essential nature.

〜といわれる（受身・通説） N4

To iwareru: passive quotation — "is said to be / they call it / it is called"

近くて遠い国といわれる

"is called a close yet distant country" — The passive to iwareru distances the speaker from the description, creating a meta-discursive frame for examining a cultural cliché.

〜ワケ（理由・説明・口語） N4

Wake: "reason / cause / why" — casual explanatory noun, often in katakana for emphasis.

近くて遠い国といわれるワケ

"the reason why they call it a close yet distant country" — The katakana wake (ワケ) gives a casual, conversational tone; the grammar promises narrative explanation rather than logical analysis.

〜嫁にくる（方向・移動） N5

Yome ni kuru: directional verb — "to come as a bride" (not "go"), framing the destination as "here."

韓国に嫁にくる

"to come as a bride to Korea" — The use of kuru (来る, "come") rather than iku (行く, "go") subtly frames Korea as the speaker's present reality, the "here" she has arrived at.

Study tip: Matsuda's grammar is the grammar of spoken Japanese — simple constructions carrying complex emotions. Pay attention to how to wa turns statements into exclamations of astonishment, how na no ni creates the grammar of disappointed expectation, how wo chūshin ni makes a mother into the center of a nation's gravity, and how de dekite iru transforms emotion into a substance that constitutes identity. The grammar is as unpretentious as the prose, and as precise.
