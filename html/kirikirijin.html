Title Analysis

Title Grammar: 吉里吉里人

The title Kirikirijin (吉里吉里人) is a compound noun of extraordinary density: four kanji that contain an entire fictional nation. Kirikiri (吉里吉里) is a reduplicated toponym — the name of a real place in Otsuchi, Iwate Prefecture, on the Pacific coast of northeastern Japan, which Inoue Hisashi borrowed and amplified into the name of an imaginary village, then an imaginary country. The repetition (kiri-kiri) creates a phonetic insistence, a stutter of place that suggests both familiarity and strangeness — this is a real name from a real landscape, but doubled, it becomes something else entirely. The final character jin (人, "person / people") transforms the place into a population: not "Kirikiri" the village but "Kirikiri-jin" — the people of Kirikiri, a nationality, an ethnicity, a new category of human being that did not exist before the novel created it. The grammar is simple — a place name + jin is the standard Japanese way of forming ethnonyms (Nihon-jin, 日本人; Chugoku-jin, 中国人) — but the simplicity is deceptive. By calling his characters "Kirikiri-jin," Inoue performs a radical act of linguistic nation-building: he invents a people by naming them, and he names them using the same grammatical mechanism that the Japanese state uses to define its own citizens. The title is thus both a description and a declaration of independence in linguistic form.

Title Breakdown

吉里 kiri "Kiri" — The first half of the reduplicated toponym. The kanji 吉 means "good fortune / auspicious / lucky," and 里 means "village / hamlet / hometown / li" (a traditional unit of distance). Together, 吉里 suggests "auspicious village" or "lucky hamlet" — a name that carries the gentle irony characteristic of Inoue's humor. The real Kirikiri in Otsuchi is a small fishing community; in the novel, it becomes the capital of a nation. The kanji choice is Inoue's own invention — the real place may use different characters or be written in katakana — and this act of rechristening is itself a fictional appropriation, a writer claiming a landscape by giving it a new written form. The phoneme kiri also resonates with other Japanese words: kiri (霧, "fog / mist"), kiri (切り, "cutting"), kiri (桐, "paulownia tree"), creating a halo of associations around the name that the novel exploits — Kirikiri is a place that emerges from fog, that cuts itself off from Japan, that grows like a tree in the reader's imagination.
吉里（反復） kiri-kiri "Kirikiri" — The reduplication. In Japanese, reduplicated place names often suggest smallness, intimacy, or childishness (Pompompurin, ポムポムプリン; Doraemon's Gian's real name is Jaian, ジャイアン, not reduplicated). But reduplication can also create emphasis or persistence — kiri-kiri sounds like something ongoing, something that will not stop. In the Tohoku dialect, kirikiri is an adverb meaning "tightly / closely / strictly" (kirikiri to shimeru, きりきりと締める, "to tighten securely"), and this semantic layer adds a political dimension to the name: Kirikiri is a place that holds itself tightly together, that closes its borders, that maintains strict control. The reduplication also mimics the rhythm of Tohoku dialect speech — the repetition of sounds, the musicality of northeastern Japanese — so that the name itself performs the linguistic nationalism that the novel will explore. The four characters 吉里吉里 create a visual symmetry on the page, a square of doubling that mirrors the novel's structure: two worlds (Japan and Kirikiri), two languages (standard Japanese and Kirikiri-go), two systems (centralization and autonomy), held in tense, comic opposition.
人 jin / hito "person / people" — The character 人 is one of the oldest and most fundamental in the Japanese writing system: a simple pictograph of two legs supporting a body, the universal human. When appended to a place name, jin (人) creates an ethnonym — a word for the people who belong to that place. Nihon-jin (日本人) are people of Japan; Kirikiri-jin (吉里吉里人) are people of Kirikiri. The grammar is identical, and this identical grammar is the novel's political point: if "Japanese" is a category created by appending jin to "Nihon," then "Kirikiri-jin" is equally valid as a category, created by the same grammatical rule. The novel's declaration of independence is performed first in the title, which creates a nationality before the first sentence creates a nation. The character 人 also carries philosophical weight in Japanese: it appears in compounds for humanity (jinrui, 人類), human rights (jinken, 人権), and artificiality (ningen, 人間, "human being"). By calling his characters Kirikiri-jin, Inoue places them in this philosophical lineage — they are not merely villagers who have declared independence; they are a new kind of human being, defined by their relationship to a specific place and language. What the English translation "The People of Kirikiri" cannot capture is the compactness of the original: four kanji that do the work of a constitutional assembly.
Literary context: The title's structure — place name + jin — places the novel in a tradition of Japanese fiction that invents nations through language. It recalls Shimazaki Toson's Yoake Mae (夜明け前, "Before the Dawn," 1929-1935), which explored the formation of Japanese national identity through regional consciousness, and anticipates Murakami Haruki's Nejimaki-dori Kuronikuru (ねじまき鳥クロニクル, "The Wind-Up Bird Chronicle," 1994-1995), which also invents a fictional nation in Manchuria. But Inoue's invention is unique in its comic audacity: he does not invent a nation in a distant colony or an alternative history; he invents one in the middle of contemporary Japan, on a train line that exists, in a landscape his readers might have passed through. The title's grammar — the simple addition of jin to a real place — makes the impossible sound bureaucratically inevitable.
Author

井上 ひさし — Inoue Hisashi

井

井上 ひさし

Inoue Hisashi · November 16, 1934 – April 9, 2010 · Yamagata / Tokyo

Inoue Hisashi was one of the most versatile and beloved writers in postwar Japanese literature — a novelist, playwright, broadcast writer, and essayist whose work moved between high literary seriousness and popular comedy with an ease that few have matched. Born in 1934 in Komatsu-cho, Higashi-Okitama-gun, Yamagata Prefecture (now Kawanishi-machi), he lost his father at age five and grew up reading his father's library — particularly the complete works of Shakespeare in Tsubouchi Shoyo's translation and the Complete Works of Modern Drama. These early encounters with dramatic literature shaped his lifelong commitment to the theater and his understanding of language as performance. After high school in Sendai, he entered Sophia University's Faculty of Letters, German Literature Department, but took a leave of absence and spent over two years in Kamaishi working as a civil servant at the National Kamaishi Sanatorium. He later transferred to the French Department of the Faculty of Foreign Studies, graduating while working as a scriptwriter at the Asakusa France-za, a striptease theater in Tokyo's Asakusa district — an apprenticeship in popular entertainment that would inform his comic genius for decades.

His breakthrough as a broadcast writer came in 1964 when he co-wrote the scripts for NHK's puppet theater series Hyokkori Hyotan-jima (ひょっこりひょうたん島, "The Unexpected Gourd Island"), which ran for five years and became a national phenomenon. The show's theme song — "Naku no wa iya da waracchaou" (泣くのはいやだ笑っちゃおう, "I don't want to cry, let's laugh instead") — might serve as Inoue's artistic manifesto. He made his theatrical debut in 1969 with Nihonjin no Heso (日本人のへそ, "The Navel of the Japanese"), written for the Theater Echo company, and his literary debut as a novelist in 1970. In 1972, he won both the Kishida Kunio Drama Prize and the New Artist Award of the Arts Festival for Dogen no Boken (道元の冒険, "The Adventures of Dogen"), and the Naoki Prize for Tesagari Shinju (手鎖心中, "Handcuffed Double Suicide") a rare double achievement in drama and fiction in the same year.

Kirikirijin (吉里吉里人, 1981) was the work that confirmed Inoue's status as a major novelist beyond the theater. Serialized in Shosetsu Shincho from May 1978 to September 1980 (after an earlier, incomplete serialization in Shumatsu Kara from 1973-1974), the novel won the 2nd Japan SF Prize, the 33rd Yomiuri Prize for Literature, and the 13th Seiun Award — a triple crown that recognized its achievement across genre boundaries. The novel's origins lay even earlier: in 1964, Inoue had written a radio drama called Kirikiri Dokuritsu-su (吉里吉里独立す, "Kirikiri Declares Independence"), which contained the seed of the novel's premise. Inoue's career continued with major works in every decade: Yabuhara Kengyo (藪原検校, 1973, play), Shinsetsu Tono Monogatari (新釈遠野物語, 1976), Yon-senmanpo no Otoko (四千万歩の男, 1986), Fuchu Koroki (腹鼓記), Fuchu Koroki (不忠臣蔵, which won the Yoshikawa Eiji Prize), Shanhai Mun (シャンハイムーン, 1991, which won the Tanizaki Prize), and Tokyo Seven Roses (東京セブンローズ, 1999, which won the Kikuchi Kan Prize). In 1984, he formed his own theater company, Komatsu-za (こまつ座), for which he wrote and produced numerous plays. He served as president of the Japan PEN Club from 2003 to 2007, was named a Person of Cultural Merit in 2004, and received the Japan Art Academy Prize Imperial Gift in 2009. He died of lung cancer in 2010 at his home in Kamakura at the age of 75.

What distinguishes Inoue's work across genres is his belief in laughter as a form of intelligence. He was a writer of the left — a member of the Article 9 Association (九条の会), critical of militarism and centralization — but his politics were always expressed through comedy rather than polemic. Kirikirijin is his most sustained political work, yet it is also one of his funniest: a novel in which armed farmers stop a train with hunting rifles and demand to see passports, in which the national assembly meets in a converted charcoal bus, in which the declaration of independence is delivered in a dialect that the Japanese government cannot understand. The comedy is not an escape from politics but its medium: Inoue believed that laughter could disarm power, that absurdity could reveal the absurdity of the powerful, and that a small village speaking its own language could be more revolutionary than any manifesto.

Notable Works

手鎖心中

Tesagari Shinju / Handcuffed Double Suicide · 1972

Winner of the Naoki Prize. A comic reimagining of the shinju (double suicide) tradition of Edo-period theater, rendered with Inoue's characteristic wit and historical erudition.

藪原検校

Yabuhara Kengyo · 1973

One of his most performed plays — a comic masterpiece about a blind kengyo (master musician) that demonstrates Inoue's deep engagement with Japanese performing arts traditions.

吉里吉里人

Kirikirijin / The People of Kirikiri · 1981

Winner of the Japan SF Prize, Yomiuri Prize for Literature, and Seiun Award. His most ambitious novel — an epic of linguistic nationalism, agricultural resistance, and comic utopia set in a village that declares independence from Japan.

シャンハイムーン

Shanhai Mun / Shanghai Moon · 1991

Winner of the Tanizaki Prize. A historical novel of Shanghai that moves beyond Japan to explore the cosmopolitanism and violence of prewar Asia — Inoue at his most internationally expansive.

Cast

Characters

Main Characters

古橋 健二

Furahashi Kenji / The Novelist

A novelist and the novel's protagonist. He is traveling on the express train Towada 3 (十和田3号) from Ueno to Aomori on assignment for the magazine Tabi to Rekishi (旅と歴史, "Travel and History") when the train is stopped at Sekiheki (赤壁, "Red Cliff"), near Ichinoseki, by armed men. Kenji becomes the first writer to visit the newly independent nation of Kirikiri, and the novel follows his perspective as he is drawn into the chaos, comedy, and idealism of the separatist state. As a novelist, he is both observer and participant — his profession gives him access to the inner workings of the new country, but his Japanese identity makes him an outsider in a nation that has just declared itself no longer Japanese. Kenji is Inoue's surrogate: a writer from Tokyo who discovers that the provinces contain a vitality, a humor, and a political imagination that the capital has forgotten.

佐藤 久夫

Sato Hisao / The Editor

Kenji's editor at Tabi to Rekishi, traveling with him on the fateful train. Sato represents the publishing industry's relationship to literature — practical, deadline-oriented, always thinking about the marketability of a story. His presence alongside Kenji creates a double perspective: the writer who wants to understand and the editor who wants to sell. As the Kirikiri crisis unfolds, Sato's concerns about getting the story back to Tokyo contrast with Kenji's growing identification with the Kirikiri cause, and this tension becomes one of the novel's running comic threads.

吉里吉里の農民たち

The Farmers of Kirikiri

The collective protagonist of the novel — the 4,187 residents of Kirikiri Village who declare independence from Japan. They are not revolutionary ideologues but ordinary farmers driven to extraordinary action by decades of agricultural neglect. Their grievances are specific and economic: Japan's postwar industrialization has devalued their labor, centralized policymaking has ignored their needs, and the government's agricultural policy has treated them as expendable. Their revolution is not armed in the conventional sense — they have hunting rifles, not armies — but it is armed with something more powerful: a language (Kirikiri-go), a currency (the yen, イエン, distinct from the Japanese yen), and a constitutional principle ("Medical Nation, Agricultural Nation, Lustful Nation" / 医学立国、農業立国、好色立国). Inoue treats them with a mixture of comedy and respect that is the novel's ethical achievement: he laughs at their absurdities without ever laughing at them, and he celebrates their resistance without romanticizing their contradictions.

防衛同好会

The Defense Association

Kirikiri's militia — a "defense association" (boei dokokai) rather than an army, composed of local farmers with hunting rifles who monitor the borders by land and air. The name itself is comic: a dokokai (同好会) is a hobby club, the kind of organization formed by people who share an interest in photography or hiking. That Kirikiri's national defense is a "hobby club" is both a joke and a serious political statement — it suggests that defense, in a truly autonomous community, is everyone's responsibility and everyone's interest, not a specialized profession. The Defense Association's methods are as unorthodox as their name: they stop trains, patrol skies, and use the landscape they know intimately as their primary weapon against the Japanese state's vastly superior forces.

日本政府・自衛隊

The Japanese Government & JSDF

The antagonists of the novel — not as villains but as bureaucratic entities that cannot comprehend what has happened. The Japanese government's response to Kirikiri's declaration is a masterpiece of Inoue's satirical method: the officials do not know whether to treat the secession as a joke, a crime, or an international incident. The Japan Self-Defense Forces (JSDF) are dispatched to retrieve the "nation's greatest trump card" — the 40,000 tons of gold that the Kirikiri farmers claim as their sovereign fund (allegedly hidden by Fujiwara Kiyohira, the founder of the Oshu Fujiwara dynasty, in the 12th century). The government's inability to take Kirikiri seriously until it is too late is the novel's central political joke: a superpower brought low by its own arrogance, defeated not by force but by the refusal of a small village to play by its rules.

藤原 清衡

Fujiwara Kiyohira / The Historical Patron

Though long dead (1056-1128), the founder of the Oshu Fujiwara dynasty in Hiraizumi haunts the novel as a kind of patron saint. The Kirikiri farmers claim that Kiyohira hid 40,000 tons of gold in their territory, and this legendary treasure becomes the financial foundation of their independent state. Kiyohira represents the deep history of Tohoku — a region that was once the center of Japanese civilization (the Hiraizumi temples were rivals to Kyoto) before being marginalized by the rise of the Kanto and Kansai plains. By invoking Kiyohira, the Kirikiri farmers connect their contemporary rebellion to a thousand-year history of Tohoku resistance to central rule, and they transform a local legend into a national origin myth. Inoue's use of Kiyohira is characteristic of his historical method: he treats legend as a form of political capital, a story that becomes true because people act as if it were true.

Story

Synopsis

The Train Stops at Red Cliff

The novel opens with a scene of comic shock: on an early June morning, the express train Towada 3 (十和田3号), bound from Ueno to Aomori, is brought to an emergency halt near Sekiheki (赤壁, "Red Cliff") station, between Ichinoseki and the border of Iwate Prefecture. Armed men board the train carrying hunting rifles and demand to see the passengers' passports. "Anda ryogen ba motte e da ga" (あんだ旅券ば持って居だが) — "You got your passport?" — they ask, in a dialect that the passengers cannot quite place. It is not standard Japanese, not quite Tohoku dialect, but something else — the first inkling the passengers (and readers) have that the linguistic rules have changed.

On this morning at 6:00 AM, the village of Kirikiri — population 4,187, located on the border of Miyagi and Iwate Prefectures — has unilaterally declared its separation and independence from Japan. The reason, the novel will reveal, is decades of accumulated grievance: Japan's postwar agricultural policy has systematically devalued farming, centralized decision-making in Tokyo has ignored the needs of rural communities, and the farmers of Kirikiri have reached a breaking point. But the declaration is not delivered as a manifesto; it is delivered as a fait accompli, announced by armed farmers on a train, in a language that the Japanese state does not recognize as legitimate. The comedy of the opening — the absurdity of a passport check on a domestic train — is also the announcement of a serious political claim: if you will not recognize us as Japanese, we will stop being Japanese.

The Nation of Kirikiri

The Kirikiri farmers have not merely declared independence; they have built a state. Their sovereign fund is the legendary 40,000 tons of gold allegedly hidden by Fujiwara Kiyohira, which they claim as historical reparations for a thousand years of Tohoku marginalization. Their currency is the yen (イエン), distinct from the Japanese yen and backed by this gold reserve. Their national language is Kirikiri-go (吉里吉里語), a standardized version of the local Tohoku dialect, elevated from "hick speech" to national tongue. Their legislature meets in a converted charcoal bus (国会議事堂車, "National Diet Building Vehicle") that tours the country, bringing democracy to the people rather than requiring the people to come to the capital. Their defense is handled by the Defense Association (boei dokokai), a militia of farmers with hunting rifles who know every path and hill of their territory. Their constitution is summed up in three principles: "Medical Nation, Agricultural Nation, Lustful Nation" (医学立国、農業立国、好色立国) — a triad that replaces Japan's "Peace Nation" (heiwa kokka) with a more earthy, bodily set of priorities.

Inoue devotes hundreds of pages to the detailed working-out of this imaginary state: its economy, its foreign relations, its legal system, its cultural policies. This is the novel's great formal achievement — it does not merely describe a utopia; it builds one, brick by brick, joke by joke, in the same way that the Kirikiri farmers build their nation. The reader learns how the Kirikiri currency appreciates against the Japanese yen, how international corporations attempt to invest in the new country, how assassins and detectives infiltrate from outside, and how the JSDF mobilizes to reclaim the gold. The novel's middle section is a political thriller disguised as a comedy, a state-building simulator rendered in prose.

Language as Revolution

The deepest revolution in Kirikirijin is not political or economic but linguistic. The Kirikiri farmers do not merely reject Tokyo's rule; they reject Tokyo's language. Kirikiri-go (吉里吉里語) is the novel's most brilliant invention — a fully realized fictional language based on Tohoku dialect but systematized, codified, and elevated to the status of a national tongue. The particle ba (ば) replaces standard Japanese wo (を) and wa (は); the copula de gasu (でがす) and da ga (だが) replace desu (です); the sentence-ending dat-cha (だっちゃ) and be-na (べな) create a rhythmic, musical syntax that is unmistakably Tohoku yet newly authoritative.

For Inoue, this linguistic secession is the novel's true subject. He had spent his career moving between registers — between the French he studied at university, the German of his early major, the standard Japanese of Tokyo literature, the Asakusa dialect of popular theater, and the Tohoku dialect of his childhood. Kirikirijin is his declaration that all of these languages are equal, that no dialect is "standard" and all others "deviant," and that the power to name, to legislate language, is the power that underlies all other powers. The Japanese government's inability to understand Kirikiri-go is not merely a comic device; it is a political diagnosis. A state that cannot comprehend the language of its own citizens has already lost the moral authority to govern them.

The Collapse and the Legacy

The novel's tragicomic climax arrives with the collapse of the Kirikiri state — on the second day of independence, the utopia begins to unravel. The Japanese government, having failed to comprehend the situation, resorts to force; the JSDF moves to seize the gold; foreign assassins and domestic detectives converge on the village; and the first casualties of the independence movement fall. The comic novel becomes, briefly, a tragedy — not because Inoue believes that resistance is futile, but because he knows that the powerful do not surrender their power willingly, and that the laughter of the weak is always shadowed by the violence of the strong.

Yet the novel's ending is not despairing. Even as the Kirikiri state collapses, its linguistic and imaginative legacy persists. The farmers have demonstrated that another Japan is possible — a Japan of regional languages, agricultural dignity, and decentralized power. They have shown that a village of 4,187 people can bring a nation of 120 million to a halt, if it has the courage to speak in its own voice. The novel's final meaning is not in the success or failure of the Kirikiri state but in the fact that it existed at all — that for two days, in the imagination of a novelist and the actions of his characters, a different Japan was real. Inoue's genius is to make the reader feel that this two-day nation was worth more than the permanent state that destroyed it.

Themes

  • Centralization vs. regional autonomy: The novel's political core is a critique of postwar Japanese centralization — the concentration of economic, political, and cultural power in Tokyo at the expense of rural and regional communities. Kirikiri's independence is a fantasy of reversal: what if the periphery could secede from the center?
  • Language and power: The elevation of Tohoku dialect to a national language is the novel's most radical gesture. Inoue treats linguistic standardization as a form of colonialism and dialect revival as a form of decolonization. Kirikiri-go is not merely a comic device but a political program.
  • Agricultural dignity: The farmers' grievances are economic and moral: Japan has treated agriculture as a backward sector to be sacrificed for industrial growth. Kirikiri's "Agricultural Nation" principle is a reclamation of farming as a noble, foundational activity.
  • Comedy as resistance: Inoue's method throughout is to make the powerful ridiculous and the ridiculous powerful. The comedy of Kirikirijin is not an escape from politics but its most effective weapon — laughter disarms authority, and absurdity reveals the absurdity of power.
  • History as resource: The invocation of Fujiwara Kiyohira and the Oshu Fujiwara dynasty transforms local legend into political capital. Inoue shows that history is not merely the past but a toolbox for the present — a set of stories that can be mobilized, reinterpreted, and deployed.
Text

Key Passages

These passages capture the novel's tonal range — from the shock of the train stopping to the linguistic comedy of Kirikiri-go to the political philosophy of the three national principles.

01
The Passport Check
「あんだ旅券ば持って居だが」
"Anda ryogen ba motte e da ga."
"You got your passport?"

Nuance Note

This is the novel's opening line — spoken by an armed farmer boarding a domestic express train in northeastern Japan, and it is one of the most politically charged first sentences in Japanese literature. The grammar is Kirikiri-go, not standard Japanese, and every element performs a revolutionary function. Anda (あんだ) is the Kirikiri-go second-person pronoun, corresponding to standard Japanese anata (あなた) but stripped of politeness and urbanity — it is the "you" of equals, or of superiors addressing inferiors, and its use by a farmer to a train passenger immediately inverts the social hierarchy. Ryogen (旅券) is the formal word for "passport" (ryoken in standard Japanese), but the Kirikiri pronunciation shifts the vowel, making the familiar word strange — a technique Inoue uses throughout to create the effect of a language that is almost Japanese but not quite. The particle ba (ば) replaces standard Japanese wo (を) or wa (は), marking the object or topic with the Tohoku dialect form. Motte e (持って居) is the continuative form of motsu (持つ, "to hold / to have") combined with the progressive auxiliary iru (居る, "to be"), rendered in the Tohoku form e instead of iru or oru. The final da ga (だが) is the Kirikiri copula, replacing standard desu ka (ですか) or arimasu ka (ありますか), with the question function carried by intonation rather than the interrogative particle ka. What is lost in English is the entire political grammar of the sentence. "You got your passport?" is a plausible English translation of the meaning, but it sounds like a casual border check — an American TSA agent at an airport. The original is something far more radical: a farmer speaking a language that the Japanese state considers inferior, demanding documentation that the Japanese state does not require, on a train that the Japanese state considers its own territory. The sentence performs a triple usurpation: of language (dialect over standard), of authority (farmer over passenger), and of territory (Kirikiri over Japan). The English reader receives information; the Japanese reader receives a declaration of independence in seven words.

02
Love Your Language
俺達の国語ば可愛がれ
"Oretachi no kokugo ba kawaigare."
"Love our national language."

Nuance Note

This is the title of Chapter 2, and it is the novel's linguistic manifesto in five words. The grammar is deceptively simple: oretachi no kokugo (俺達の国語, "our national language") + ba (ば, object marker) + kawaigare (可愛がれ, imperative of kawaigaru, 可愛がる, "to love / to cherish / to treat with affection"). But each word carries layers of political and emotional weight. Oretachi (俺達) is the rough, masculine first-person plural — "we guys," "us fellas" — the pronoun of working men, not the refined watashitachi (私達) of standard Japanese. By using oretachi, the title claims the national language for the working class, the farmers, the dialect speakers whom standard Japanese has marginalized. Kokugo (国語, "national language") is the official term for Japanese in the education system — the subject taught in schools, the language of the state. By claiming kokugo status for Kirikiri-go, the title performs a constitutional act: it declares that Kirikiri-go is not a dialect (hogen, 方言) but a national language (kokugo), with all the institutional authority that term implies. The verb kawaigare (可愛がれ) is the imperative form of kawaigaru (可愛がる), a verb of deep affection — not merely "like" (suki ni naru) or "respect" (sonkei suru) but "love as one loves a child or a pet." Kawaigaru suggests nurturing, protecting, and taking pleasure in the beloved's growth. The imperative is directed at the reader, at the Japanese public, at anyone who encounters Kirikiri-go: love this language, care for it, help it grow. The particle ba (ば) marks "our national language" as the object of this imperative love, and the absence of any subject (who should love it?) makes the command universal. What is lost in English is the tenderness of kawaigaru — "love" is too generic, "cherish" too formal. The title asks for a familial, bodily affection for a language, the kind of love one has for something that is both dependent and precious. It is a mother's command to her children: love your little sister. The grammar makes language into kin.

03
The Three Principles
医学立国、農業立国、好色立国
"Igaku rikkoku, nogyo rikkoku, koshoku rikkoku."
"Medical Nation, Agricultural Nation, Lustful Nation."

Nuance Note

This triad is the constitutional foundation of Kirikiri State — its answer to Japan's postwar principle of "Peace Nation" (heiwa kokka, 平和国家). The grammar is a classical Japanese rhetorical form: three noun phrases in parallel, each consisting of a field (igaku, 医学, "medicine"; nogyo, 農業, "agriculture"; koshoku, 好色, "lust / eroticism") + rikkoku (立国, "nation-building / founding principle"). The rikkoku construction is the same used in historical phrases like bunbu rikkoku (文武立国, "founding the nation on civil and military arts") and shinto rikkoku (神道立国, "founding the nation on Shinto"). By adopting this classical form, Kirikiri claims historical legitimacy: its principles are expressed in the same grammatical structure used by Japanese states for a thousand years. The first two principles — Medical Nation and Agricultural Nation — are serious political statements. "Medical Nation" asserts that healthcare is a fundamental responsibility of the state, not a commodity to be purchased. "Agricultural Nation" asserts that farming is the foundation of national life, not a sector to be sacrificed for industrial growth. Both are direct critiques of Japanese policy, which had privatized healthcare and devalued agriculture. The third principle — koshoku rikkoku (好色立国, "Lustful Nation") — is where Inoue's comedy enters the constitution. Koshoku (好色) means "lustfulness / amorousness / fondness for sensual pleasure" and is the word used to describe the eroticism of writers like Ihara Saikaku (井原西鶴) or the "shunpon" (春画, erotic prints) of ukiyo-e. To make lust a founding principle of the state is absurd — and precisely the point. Inoue is saying that a nation founded only on medicine and agriculture would be worthy but dull; a nation needs pleasure, eros, the body as well as the breadbasket and the hospital. The triad thus contains Inoue's entire philosophy: a state should care for its people's bodies (medicine), feed them (agriculture), and give them joy (lust). What is lost in English is the shock of seeing koshoku in a constitutional context. In Japanese, koshoku is a literary word, associated with the ukiyo (浮世, "floating world") of Edo-period pleasure districts. To place it alongside igaku and nogyo — sober, modern, policy-oriented words — creates a comic collision of registers that English "Lustful Nation" cannot fully reproduce. "Lustful" in English sounds merely vulgar or comic; koshoku in Japanese carries the aroma of literature, of Saikaku and ukiyo-e, of a tradition of erotic art that the modern Japanese state has repressed. The triad is thus not only funny; it is a reclamation of Japan's repressed erotic tradition as a national value.

04
The Quiet Eyes
吉里吉里人は眼はァ静がで
"Kirikiri-jin wa me wa shizuka de."
"The people of Kirikiri have quiet eyes."

Nuance Note

This is the title of Chapter 3, and it is the novel's most poetic statement of national character. The grammar is a topic-description construction: Kirikiri-jin wa (吉里吉里人は, "as for the people of Kirikiri") + me wa (眼は, "as for [their] eyes") + shizuka de (静かで, "quiet [and...]"). The de (で) ending is the continuative form of the copula da (だ), suggesting that the sentence continues — their eyes are quiet, and... what? The trailing de creates a sense of unfinished observation, as if the narrator has noticed something that cannot be fully captured in words. The word shizuka (静か, "quiet / calm / still") is one of the most aesthetically charged words in Japanese. It describes not merely the absence of noise but a positive quality of being — the stillness of a pond, the hush of snowfall, the composure of someone who has made a difficult decision. To say that the Kirikiri people have "quiet eyes" is to describe a national temperament: they are not agitated, not hysterical, not fanatical. Their revolution is conducted with the calm of people who have thought carefully about what they are doing. The grammar of me wa shizuka de also contains a subtle emphasis: by topicalizing me (眼, "eyes") with wa (は), the sentence draws attention to the eyes specifically — the windows of the soul — rather than making a general claim about their character. It is their eyes that are quiet, and in Japanese aesthetics, the eyes are the seat of kokoro (心, "heart / mind"). The title thus suggests that the Kirikiri people's inner selves are calm, resolved, at peace with their radical action. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese aesthetic resonance of shizuka and its association with moral composure. "Quiet eyes" in English might suggest shyness or dullness; me wa shizuka in Japanese suggests depth, deliberation, and the stillness that comes from knowing one's own mind. The title is a portrait in four words — a national character drawn not through action but through gaze.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Inoue's prose in Kirikirijin is a high-wire act between standard Japanese and Kirikiri-go, between literary seriousness and popular comedy, between political manifesto and puppet show. Much of what makes the novel revolutionary is embedded in its language and cannot be transferred into English without fundamental transformation.

1. Kirikiri-go (吉里吉里語) as Untranslatable Revolution

JP 「あんだ旅券ば持って居だが」「俺達の国語ば可愛がれ」
EN "You got your passport?" "Love our national language."
Loss Kirikiri-go is not merely a dialect of Japanese; it is a political assertion rendered in grammatical form. When a Kirikiri farmer says ba instead of wo or wa, he is not making a mistake; he is making a revolution. The English translation can convey the meaning of Kirikiri-go sentences, but it cannot convey their political function — the way each non-standard particle, each dialectal verb ending, each Tohoku pronunciation is an act of resistance against the linguistic authority of Tokyo. In English, a dialect is a flavor, an accent, a regional color. In Japanese, dialect is hierarchy: standard Japanese (hyojun-go) is the language of education, employment, and state power, while dialects are the languages of home, of the provinces, of the unofficial. Kirikiri-go's elevation to national language status is thus a reversal of this hierarchy, and the English reader who encounters "You got your passport?" has no way of knowing that the original sentence performs a constitutional act. What is lost is the entire political grammar of the novel: the fact that language choice is never neutral in Japan, that speaking Tohoku dialect in Tokyo is a class marker, a regional marker, a statement of identity that carries social consequences. Inoue's genius is to make this invisible hierarchy visible by inverting it, and the English translation, by flattening Kirikiri-go into standard English, undoes the inversion. The revolution disappears into grammatical normalcy.

2. Funman (ふんまん) as Accumulated Grievance

JP 農業軽視政策に対するふんまん
EN "resentment against agricultural neglect policies"
Loss Funman (憤懣) is a Sino-Japanese compound of fun (憤, "indignation / anger") and man (懣, "depression of spirits / pent-up feeling"), meaning "pent-up resentment / smoldering indignation." It is a word of extraordinary emotional precision: it describes not the explosive anger of protest but the slow accumulation of grievance over years, the sense of injustice that builds in silence until it can no longer be contained. Funman is the emotion of people who have been ignored, dismissed, and devalued — not for a day or a year but for decades, for generations. The farmers' funman is historical: it stretches back through the postwar period, through the industrialization that drained the countryside of its youth, through the agricultural policies that treated farming as a problem to be solved rather than a foundation to be preserved. The English "resentment" is accurate but thin — it suggests a personal emotion, perhaps even a petty one. Funman is collective, historical, and righteous. It is the emotion that fuels revolutions, and Inoue's choice of this word rather than the simpler ikari (怒り, "anger") or funnu (憤怒, "rage") tells us that Kirikiri's independence is not a spontaneous outburst but the culmination of a long, patient suffering. What is lost is the sense of time in funman — the implication that this grievance has been building for as long as anyone can remember, and that its explosion, when it comes, is both inevitable and surprising.

3. Kokugo (国語) vs. Hogen (方言) as Linguistic Hierarchy

JP 国語（標準語）と方言
EN "national language (standard language)" and "dialect"
Loss The distinction between kokugo (国語, "national language") and hogen (方言, "dialect") is one of the most politically charged linguistic hierarchies in Japan. Kokugo is the language taught in schools, used in government, spoken on national television, and expected in job interviews. It is Tokyo dialect elevated to national standard, and its authority is backed by the entire apparatus of the modern Japanese state. Hogen is everything else — the regional languages that children learn at home and are taught to abandon at school, the "accents" that mark speakers as provincial, uneducated, or old-fashioned. The English words "standard language" and "dialect" carry some of this hierarchy, but they lack the specifically Japanese institutional weight of kokugo. In Japan, kokugo is a school subject, a ministry portfolio, a constitutional concept. To challenge the kokugo/hogen distinction is to challenge the Japanese state's authority over its citizens' speech. Kirikiri's claim that its dialect is a kokugo is thus not merely a linguistic assertion but a constitutional one: it claims the same institutional authority for Tohoku dialect that the Japanese state claims for Tokyo dialect. The English reader sees a dialect movement; the Japanese reader sees a constitutional crisis. What is lost is the sense that kokugo is not merely a linguistic category but a form of citizenship — the language in which one becomes a fully recognized member of the nation. By making Kirikiri-go a kokugo, the farmers claim a new form of citizenship, and the English translation cannot reproduce this claim because English has no equivalent institutional category.

4. Rikkoku (立国) as Constitutional Poetry

JP 医学立国、農業立国、好色立国
EN "Medical Nation, Agricultural Nation, Lustful Nation"
Loss Rikkoku (立国) is a compound of ritsu (立, "to stand / to establish") and koku (国, "nation / country"), meaning "to found a nation" or "nation-building principle." It is a word from classical Japanese political discourse, used in phrases like bunbu rikkoku (文武立国, "founding the nation on civil and military arts") that date back to the Heian and Kamakura periods. By using rikkoku, Inoue places Kirikiri's constitution in a thousand-year tradition of Japanese statecraft — the farmers are not improvising; they are speaking the language of Fujiwara Kiyohira and the Oshu Fujiwara dynasty, of the Ritsuryo state and the Meiji Restoration. The English "nation" loses this historical resonance — "Medical Nation" sounds like a modern policy slogan, not a constitutional principle rooted in Japanese political tradition. What is also lost is the rhythmic power of the triad: igaku-rikkoku, nogyo-rikkoku, koshoku-rikkoku. The repetition of rikkoku creates a drumbeat of founding principles, a constitutional mantra. The English translation must either repeat "Nation" (which becomes monotonous) or drop it (which loses the parallelism). In either case, the poetry of the original — four syllables repeated three times, each time with a different first element — is diminished. The triad is not merely a list of policies; it is a poem in the form of a constitution, and the English reader receives the policies without the poem.

5. Kotoba no Miryoku (言葉の魅力) as the Novel's Own Subject

JP 言葉の魅力を満載して描く大作
EN "a monumental work filled with the charm of words"
Loss The publisher's promotional copy describes Kirikirijin as a work "filled with kotoba no miryoku" (言葉の魅力, "the charm of words / the fascination of language"), and this phrase is not mere marketing — it is the novel's own self-description, its claim about what literature can do. Kotoba (言葉, "word / language / speech") is one of the most important words in Japanese literary criticism, carrying resonances that English "word" or "language" cannot capture. Kotoba includes not only written and spoken language but also the particular way a person speaks — their kotoba-zukai (言葉遣い, "word usage") — and by extension, their character, their region, their class. Miryoku (魅力, "charm / fascination / attraction") combines mi (魅, "fascination / enchantment") and ryoku (力, "power / force"), suggesting not merely "charm" in the English sense of pleasantness but a power that draws people in, that enchants, that transforms. Kotoba no miryoku is thus not "nice words" but "the transformative power of language" — the ability of words to create worlds, to invent nations, to make the impossible sound inevitable. This is precisely what Kirikirijin does: it uses the "charm of words" to create a country. The English "charm of words" sounds quaint, literary, perhaps even trivial. The Japanese kotoba no miryoku sounds like a force of nature — which, for Inoue, it is. What is lost is the novel's self-awareness: it knows that it is performing the very thing it describes, using the charm of words to build Kirikiri even as it writes about Kirikiri being built by the charm of words. The novel is a mise en abyme of linguistic creation, and the English translation cannot preserve this self-reflexive structure because it cannot preserve the specific gravity of kotoba in Japanese culture.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns from Kirikiri-go, standard Japanese narration, and the political rhetoric of nation-building that drives the novel's linguistic comedy.

〜ば（吉里吉里語・対象・主題） Dialect

Ba: Kirikiri-go particle replacing standard Japanese wo (を) and wa (は).

あんだ旅券ば持って居だが / 俺達の国語ば可愛がれ

"You got your passport?" / "Love our national language" — The ba particle marks both objects and topics in Kirikiri-go, collapsing the standard Japanese distinction and creating a fluid, musical syntax that the Japanese state cannot parse.

〜でがす / 〜だが（吉里吉里語・断定） Dialect

De gasu / da ga: Kirikiri-go copulas replacing standard desu (です).

〜でがす（丁寧）/ 〜だが（普通体・疑問）

"~de gasu" (polite) / "~da ga" (plain, often interrogative) — The Kirikiri copula system replaces the standard Japanese hierarchy of politeness with its own, asserting linguistic autonomy at the most fundamental grammatical level: the verb "to be."

〜だっちゃ / 〜べな（東北語尾） Dialect

Dat-cha / be-na: Tohoku sentence endings expressing assertion, exclamation, or speculation.

これがまんず最初の切札なんだっちゃ

"This is, well, our first trump card, dat-cha" — The dat-cha ending adds a note of rough assertion; be-na adds exclamatory force. Together they create the musical, emphatic quality of Tohoku speech that Kirikiri-go elevates to national language.

〜は〜で（主題・継続） N5

Wa...de: topic marker + continuative copula, creating open, descriptive statements.

吉里吉里人は眼はァ静がで

"The people of Kirikiri have quiet eyes [and...]" — The wa...de construction creates a portrait in progress, a description that trails off rather than concluding. The grammar enacts the novel's method: observation without closure.

〜立国（複合名詞・憲法） N1

Rikkoku: "nation-building on ~" — classical compound for constitutional principles.

医学立国、農業立国、好色立国

"Medical Nation, Agricultural Nation, Lustful Nation" — The X + rikkoku pattern places each field on the same grammatical level, making medicine, agriculture, and lust equally valid foundations for statecraft.

〜て居る（進行・継続・方言） N4

Te iru / te e: progressive aspect, rendered in Kirikiri-go as te e.

持って居だが（持っているのか）

"Do you have [it]?" (literally: "are holding?") — The progressive te e (持って居) replaces standard te iru (持っている), and the interrogative function is carried by intonation rather than ka. The grammar makes the familiar strange.

〜人（民族名・接尾語） N5

Jin: suffix forming ethnonyms from place names.

吉里吉里人 / 日本人 / 中国人

"Kirikiri-jin / Nihon-jin / Chugoku-jin" — The jin suffix uses the same grammatical mechanism for all three, making Kirikiri nationality as linguistically valid as Japanese or Chinese nationality. The grammar performs nation-building.

〜に対する〜（対象・文脈） N3

Ni tai suru: "toward / against / in response to" — creating contexts of grievance.

農業軽視政策に対するふんまん

"resentment toward agricultural neglect policies" — The ni tai suru construction frames the farmers' emotion as a response to a specific target, making their grievance concrete and directed rather than diffuse.

Study tip: Inoue's grammar in Kirikirijin operates on two levels simultaneously: the standard Japanese of the narrator (Kenji) and the Kirikiri-go of the farmers. The novel's comedy and politics both depend on the friction between these two grammars. Pay attention to how ba replaces wo/wa, how de gasu replaces desu, how dat-cha and be-na create a rhythm that standard Japanese cannot replicate, and how the classical rikkoku construction gives constitutional weight to the farmers' absurd triad. The grammar is the revolution, written in particles and copulas.
