Title Analysis

Title Grammar: 生体解剖―731部隊の少年兵だった私

The title Seitai Kaibō — 731 Butai no Shōnenhei Datta Watashi (生体解剖―731部隊の少年兵だった私) is a title of devastating directness. It consists of two elements separated by an em-dash: seitai kaibō (生体解剖, "vivisection / dissection of a living body") and 731 butai no shōnenhei datta watashi (731部隊の少年兵だった私, "I who was a boy soldier of Unit 731"). The em-dash functions as a caesura, a pause between the act and the actor, between the crime and the witness who both suffered and perpetrated it. The title does not say "I witnessed vivisection" or "I know about vivisection"; it says "Vivisection — I was a boy soldier of Unit 731," as if the two phrases were interchangeable, as if the unit and the act were so inseparable that to name one is to evoke the other. The first-person pronoun watashi (私, "I") at the very end of the title places the speaker in a position of radical vulnerability: this is not a historian's overview, not a journalist's investigation, but a confession in the first person, an "I" who must answer for what "I" did and saw.

Title Breakdown

生体解剖 seitai kaibō "vivisection / dissection of a living body" — The compound seitai kaibō (生体解剖) combines seitai (生体, "living body / organism") and kaibō (解剖, "dissection / anatomy / autopsy"). The word kaibō is the standard medical term for the anatomical dissection of a cadaver — the foundational practice of Western medicine since the Renaissance, the ritual by which medical students learn the architecture of the human body. In ordinary usage, kaibō is performed on the dead; the modifier seitai (living) transforms it into something that violates the fundamental premise of medical ethics. Seitai kaibō is not merely "human experimentation"; it is the cutting open of a living human being while they are still conscious, still breathing, still feeling. The word carries a specific historical weight in Japan: it was the term used to describe the medical crimes committed by Japanese military doctors during the war — not only at Unit 731 but also at the Kyushu University vivisection of American POWs in 1945 (the subject of Endō Shūsaku's novel Umi to Dokuyaku / 海と毒薬). By placing seitai kaibō first in the title, Kamata frames his entire testimony around this single, unspeakable act. The title does not soften, does not euphemize, does not retreat into bureaucratic language. It names the crime in the language of the perpetrators and then claims it as the experience of a child soldier. What is lost in English is the specifically medical resonance of kaibō — a word that every Japanese medical student knows as the noble foundation of their profession, here transformed into the name of a crime. The English "vivisection" is accurate but clinical; seitai kaibō in Japanese carries the shock of a medical term turned inside out.
少年兵 shōnenhei "boy soldier / child soldier" — The compound shōnenhei (少年兵) combines shōnen (少年, "boy / juvenile / youth") and hei (兵, "soldier / private / troops"), and it refers to a child or adolescent serving in a military capacity. The word carries connotations of exploitation and tragedy: a shōnenhei is not a volunteer in any meaningful sense but a child drawn into the machinery of war by forces beyond their comprehension or control. In the context of Unit 731, the shōnenhei were the Youth Corps members (shōnen-tai / 少年隊) — boys aged 14 to 18 recruited from impoverished families with promises of education and advancement, then deployed as assistants in experiments they did not understand and could not refuse. The title's use of shōnenhei rather than shōnen-taiin (少年隊員, "Youth Corps member") is significant: shōnenhei emphasizes the military nature of their service and their status as soldiers rather than students. Kamata was not merely a "member" of a "youth team"; he was a shōnenhei, a child conscripted into the army of vivisection. The word also carries a moral ambiguity that shōnen-taiin lacks: a shōnenhei can be both victim and perpetrator, both the exploited child and the obedient soldier who followed orders. This ambiguity is central to Kamata's testimony: he was a child who was made to do things that no child should do, and his confession is an attempt to hold both truths — the truth of his victimization and the truth of his complicity — in a single sentence. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese resonance of shōnenhei as a figure of postwar reflection: the child soldiers of the Pacific War, the young kamikaze pilots, the boys who fired bamboo spears at imaginary invaders — all are shōnenhei, and Kamata places himself in this tragic lineage while insisting on the unique horror of his particular service.
だった私 datta watashi "I who was / I who used to be" — The phrase datta watashi (だった私) uses the past tense copula datta (だった, "was / used to be") with the first-person pronoun watashi (私, "I"), creating a construction that emphasizes the speaker's distance from their former self. Datta is the plain past tense of da (だ), the informal copula, and its use here (rather than the more formal deshita / でした) creates a tone of raw, unadorned confession — the voice of an old man speaking plainly about what he once was. The construction "[noun] + datta + watashi" is a common pattern in Japanese memoir titles: it creates a temporal gap between the narrator's present self and the self being described. Watashi is the most neutral first-person pronoun in Japanese — not as humble as boku (僕) or watakushi (わたくし), not as masculine as ore (俺) — and its neutrality is precisely what makes it appropriate here: Kamata speaks not as a particular type of man but as a human being who must bear witness. The phrase datta watashi carries a sense of finality and irrevocability: I was this thing, and I can never not have been this thing. The past tense does not release the speaker from responsibility; it confirms that the identity is fixed, unchangeable, permanent in memory if not in present reality. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese grammar of identity-through-tense: the English "I who was" suggests a narrative distance that the Japanese datta watashi both creates and collapses — the distance of time and the collapse of that distance in the act of confession.
―（ダッシュ） em-dash The em-dash (―) that separates seitai kaibō from 731 butai no shōnenhei datta watashi is doing crucial work in this title. In Japanese typography, the em-dash is less common than in English, and its appearance here creates a visual and conceptual break that is also a connection. The dash separates the act from the actor, the crime from the criminal-victim, the general phenomenon of vivisection from the specific experience of one boy soldier. But it also connects them: the dash suggests that the two phrases are two sides of the same reality, that "vivisection" and "I was a boy soldier of Unit 731" are not separate facts but a single truth experienced from the inside. The dash creates a space of silence between the words — a space that the reader must cross, a pause in which the horror of the connection becomes felt before it is fully understood. In this, the dash functions like the Japanese concept of ma (間, "interval / space / pause"): the meaningful silence between events, the gap that gives form to what surrounds it. The title without the dash — Seitai Kaibō 731 Butai no Shōnenhei Datta Watashi — would be a continuous phrase; the dash makes it a confrontation. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese visual and conceptual weight of the em-dash as a rare and deliberate choice — a typographical decision that mirrors the rupture in Kamata's life that the book describes.

Author

鎌田 信雄 — Kamata Nobuo (1923–)

鎌

鎌田 信雄 / Kamata Nobuo

Former Unit 731 Youth Corps member · "Phantom" first-generation recruit · Witness

Kamata Nobuo was born in 1923, a child of imperial Japan's impoverished rural society, and was recruited into what would become the most notorious military unit in Japanese history before he was old enough to understand what he was being asked to do. He was one of the "phantom" first-generation members (maboroshi no shōnen-tai issei-sei / まぼろしの少年隊一期生) of Unit 731's Youth Corps — a precursor cohort gathered by Ishii Shirō himself before the formal, numbered recruitment system was established. These boys were Ishii's personal experiment: he would call them to his office, speak to them as a father (oyaji / おやじ), and promise them education, advancement, and a role in saving the nation. What he did not tell them was that they would be assistants in murder.

The phrase "phantom Youth Corps" (maboroshi no shōnen-tai / まぼろしの少年隊) is significant: these earliest recruits existed outside the formal structure that would later be documented and partially acknowledged. They were Ishii's personal project, a small group of boys who received special attention from the commander himself and who were deployed in ways that the later, larger cohorts were not. Kamata's testimony suggests that he was made to participate in vivisection — the cutting open of living human beings — as part of his "training" or "service" to the unit. The passive construction he uses — yarasareta (やらされた, "I was made to do it") — is the grammar of coercion: not "I did it" but "it was done to me through my own hands," a formulation that preserves both the fact of his participation and the fact of his victimization.

After the war, Kamata returned to Japan with a burden that he carried in silence for decades. The postwar order imposed a double silence on former Unit 731 members: they were forbidden from speaking by military secrecy oaths, and they were shunned by a society that did not want to know what had been done in its name. Like many former Youth Corps members, Kamata found himself unemployable in medical or scientific fields — the very fields for which he had been "trained" — because his association with the unit made him a liability. He lived with his memories in the shadows of postwar Japan, watching as the country rebuilt itself without ever acknowledging the foundations of ash and bone on which that reconstruction rested.

In October 1995, seventy years after his birth and fifty years after the war's end, Kamata broke his silence. His testimony — recorded by researchers and published first in the collective volume Saikinsen Butai (1996) and later cited in Nishisato Fuyuko's Seibutsusen Butai 731 (2002) — is brief but devastating. Unlike Akiyama Hiroshi's full memoir or Morimura Seiichi's investigative trilogy, Kamata's testimony is a single, concentrated act of witness: a few pages in which an old man confesses what a boy was made to do. Its power comes not from comprehensiveness but from specificity — the detail of a single vivisection, the memory of a single face, the grammar of a single sentence: seitai kaibō wo yarasa reta (生体解剖をやらされた, "I was made to perform vivisection").

What distinguishes Kamata from other witnesses is his position as a "phantom" — a first-generation recruit who existed in the liminal space between Ishii's personal favor and the formal military structure. He called Ishii "father" (oyaji), a term that suggests both the paternalism of the commander-recruit relationship and the grotesque perversion of that paternalism into something that led children to the dissecting table. Kamata's testimony is thus not merely a historical document but a psychological one: the account of a child who was groomed by a father figure to participate in crimes, and who spent the rest of his life trying to understand what had been done to him and what he had done through the hands that were not entirely his own.

History

The Phantom Youth Corps: Ishii's First Experiment in Child Soldiers

The "Phantom" First Generation

The formal Youth Corps (shōnen-tai / 少年隊) of Unit 731 was organized into numbered cohorts beginning in 1938, with four recognized generations (issei through yon-sei / 一期〜四期) recruited between 1938 and 1945. But before this formal system existed, Ishii Shirō gathered a small group of boys through personal connections and direct recruitment — boys who would become known as the "phantom" first generation. These were not volunteers in the usual sense; they were children from poor families, from rural areas, from households that could not afford to send their sons to middle school. Ishii offered them what seemed like salvation: education, training, a path to becoming "important personnel for the nation" (kuni no jūyō na jin-zai / 国家の重要な人材).

Kamata Nobuo was one of these phantom recruits. Born in 1923, he was approximately 15 to 20 years old during his service — old enough to obey orders, too young to question them. The fact that he called Ishii "oyaji" (おやじ, "father / old man") suggests a relationship of unusual intimacy: Ishii did not merely command these boys; he cultivated them. He would summon them to his office, speak to them about their studies, their futures, their duty to the nation. This paternal grooming served a military purpose: boys who felt personal loyalty to the commander would be more obedient, more reliable, more willing to do what ordinary soldiers might refuse. The phantom recruits were Ishii's experiment in creating perfectly obedient assistants — and Kamata's testimony suggests that the experiment succeeded beyond anything that medical ethics could have permitted.

Vivisection as "Training"

The specific crime that Kamata confesses — seitai kaibō (生体解剖, vivisection) — was not an aberration within Unit 731's operations; it was central to its research methodology. Vivisection served two purposes: it allowed researchers to observe the effects of pathogens and trauma on living human organs, and it ensured that no witness survived to tell what had been done. The victims — called maruta (丸太, "logs") in the unit's dehumanizing slang — were routinely dissected without anesthesia, their organs removed and examined while they were still alive. For a Youth Corps member to be made to assist in this procedure was a form of initiation — a brutal induction into the unit's moral universe in which the boundary between human and material had been permanently erased.

Kamata's use of the passive causative — yarasa reta (やらされた, "I was made to do it") — is the grammar of a child who could not refuse. In Japanese, the passive-causative construction (-saserareru / 〜させられる) combines the causative (being made to do something) with the passive (having something done to oneself), and it is the standard grammatical form for describing coercion by authority. A soldier does not "do" things in war; things are "done through" the soldier by the chain of command. Kamata's grammar acknowledges his own hands' participation while locating responsibility in the structure that compelled them — the commander who gave the order, the institution that normalized the act, the state that required the research. This is not evasion; it is the precise grammar of institutional crime.

The Postwar Silence and Its Costs

After 1945, former Unit 731 members faced a silence enforced from two directions. From above, the American occupation authorities and the Japanese government had every interest in suppressing the full story: the Americans because they had made the immunity deal, the Japanese because the crimes implicated the medical and military establishments that the postwar order needed to rehabilitate. From below, Japanese society — exhausted by war, hungry for reconstruction, eager to believe in its own victimhood — had little appetite for confronting the crimes committed in its name. Former Youth Corps members found themselves in a particularly cruel position: they had been children when they were recruited, children when they witnessed and participated in crimes, and adults when they were expected to forget what children cannot forget.

Kamata's 1995 testimony thus represents a double breakthrough: the breaking of a personal silence that had lasted fifty years, and the breaking of a collective silence that had enveloped an entire generation. When he said seitai kaibō wo yarasa reta — "I was made to perform vivisection" — he was not merely reporting a fact; he was performing a speech act that transformed private memory into public testimony. The grammar of his confession is the grammar of historical reckoning: the passive-causative that distributes responsibility, the first-person pronoun that claims authorship of memory, the medical term that names the crime without euphemism. In a few sentences, Kamata achieved what decades of historical research had struggled to accomplish: he made the crime personal, specific, and irrefutable.

Structure

The Testimony as Confession

The Form of Witness

Kamata's testimony does not follow the structure of a conventional memoir. It is not divided into chapters, does not trace a narrative arc from innocence to experience to reflection, does not build toward a moral conclusion. It is, instead, a single sustained act of witness — a confession that proceeds by accumulation of detail rather than by narrative development. The form is dictated by its content: there is no "story" that can make vivisection comprehensible, no plot that can render it meaningful. There is only the fact, and the voice that speaks it.

The testimony as published in Saikinsen Butai (1996) follows a structure common to oral histories recorded by researchers: an introductory note by the editor establishing context, the witness's own words (often edited for clarity but preserved in their essential form), and occasionally follow-up questions or clarifications. Kamata's testimony is presented under the simple, devastating heading "生体解剖をやらされた" (I Was Made to Perform Vivisection) — a title that is simultaneously a summary and a confession. The reader who encounters this heading in the table of contents knows, before reading a single sentence of the testimony, what kind of document they are about to enter. The title is the content; the testimony is the proof.

The Voice of the Phantom

What distinguishes Kamata's voice from other Unit 731 testimonies is its quality of haunted specificity. Akiyama Hiroshi writes as a witness who saw much and understood little; his memoir has the quality of a child's attempt to make sense of incomprehensible events. Morimura Seiichi writes as an investigator who has assembled fragments into a pattern; his prose has the urgency of accusation. Kamata writes — or speaks, for his testimony is oral history rendered into text — as a man who has carried a single, unbearable memory for fifty years and who has finally found the grammatical form in which to speak it.

The "phantom" quality of Kamata's position — a first-generation recruit who called the commander "father," who existed outside the formal structure, who was personally groomed by Ishii Shirō — gives his testimony a unique perspective. He speaks not as a functionary describing a system but as a child describing a father who led him to atrocity. The psychological complexity of this position — the betrayal of paternal trust, the corruption of filial obedience, the impossibility of separating love (or its semblance) from complicity — is what makes Kamata's brief testimony resonate beyond its length. In a few pages, he raises questions that volumes of historical analysis struggle to answer: How does a child say no to a father? How does a soldier refuse an order that is also a "lesson"? How does a man live with the memory of hands that were his own but not entirely under his control?

The Citation in Later Works

Kamata's testimony has been cited and contextualized in subsequent works on Unit 731, most notably in Nishisato Fuyuko's Seibutsusen Butai 731 (2002), where it appears as part of a broader investigation into the unit's operations and postwar legacy. In Nishisato's framing, Kamata represents the "phantom" generation — the unacknowledged precursor to the formal Youth Corps system — and his testimony serves as evidence that the unit's crimes were not merely the work of adult researchers but involved the systematic exploitation of children. The citation of Kamata's testimony in scholarly and journalistic works has transformed his private confession into public evidence, his personal memory into historical documentation. This is the trajectory of witness in the modern world: the individual who speaks becomes, through the citation and circulation of their words, a node in the network of historical truth.

Text

Key Passages

These passages capture the grammatical, psychological, and historical weight of Kamata's testimony — a voice that speaks from the intersection of victimhood and complicity, childhood and atrocity, memory and confession.

01
The Title Itself
生体解―731部隊の少年兵だった私
"Seitai Kaibō — 731 Butai no Shōnenhei Datta Watashi."
"Vivisection — I Who Was a Boy Soldier of Unit 731."

Nuance Note

The title Seitai Kaibō — 731 Butai no Shōnenhei Datta Watashi is a study in the grammar of confession. The em-dash creates a caesura between the medical term for the crime and the first-person identification of the criminal-victim, a pause that forces the reader to make the connection themselves. The word seitai kaibō (生体解剖) is placed first, as if it were the subject of a sentence whose predicate is the speaker's entire life. The phrase 731 butai no shōnenhei datta watashi (731部隊の少年兵だった私) uses the past-tense copula datta (だった) to create temporal distance — "I who was" — but the confession collapses that distance by the very act of speaking. The first-person pronoun watashi (私) at the end of the title is the grammatical and moral anchor: everything in the title leads to this "I" who must answer for what it names. What is lost in English is the shock of the medical term kaibō (解剖) in its native context — a word that Japanese medical students encounter on their first day of anatomy class, here transformed into the title of a war crimes confession. The English "vivisection" is accurate but lacks the specifically Japanese resonance of kaibō as a foundational term of modern Japanese medicine, a term that carries the weight of professional identity and ethical obligation. Kamata's title is an accusation against that profession and that ethics, spoken in their own vocabulary.

02
The Confession's Core
生体解剖をやらされた。
"Seitai kaibō wo yarasa reta."
"I was made to perform vivisection."

Nuance Note

This sentence — the heading of Kamata's testimony and its moral center — is a masterpiece of grammatical precision. The verb yaru (やる, "to do") is in the passive-causative form yarasa reta (らされた), which combines the causative (-saseru / 〜させる, "make someone do") with the passive (-rareru / 〜られる, "be done to"). The resulting construction means literally "I was caused-to-do by someone else" — a grammatical form that distributes agency between the speaker and the structure that compelled them. Kamata does not say shita (した, "I did it") — which would claim full agency — nor does he say sarereta (された, "it was done to me") — which would deny all agency. He says yarasa reta (やらされた), which means: my hands performed the act, but the will that moved them was not mine. This is the grammar of institutional crime, of the soldier who follows orders, of the child who obeys the father. It is also the grammar of survival: by using the passive-causative, Kamata acknowledges what he did without claiming that he chose to do it — a distinction that is morally crucial and legally significant. The object seitai kaibō (生解剖) is placed first, before the verb, giving it emphasis and weight: the crime precedes the confession, the fact precedes the speaker's relationship to it. The particle wo (を) marks the direct object with stark simplicity — no elaboration, no mitigation, no euphemism. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese grammatical subtlety of yarasa reta as a form that preserves both the fact of participation and the fact of coercion — a form for which English has no exact equivalent. The English "I was made to" captures the causative but loses the passive; "I was forced to" captures the coercion but loses the grammatical precision; "I had to" captures the necessity but loses the sense of external compulsion. The Japanese yarasa reta holds all of these meanings in a single form, and its use in Kamata's confession is the grammatical evidence of a man who has thought for fifty years about how to say what must be said without saying either too much or too little.

03
The Phantom's Origin
私は石井部隊長の発案で集められた「まぼろしの少年隊1期生」でした。
"Watashi wa Ishii butai-chō no hatsuan de atsumareta 'maboroshi no shōnen-tai issei-sei' deshita."
"I was one of the 'phantom Youth Corps first-generation members' gathered at the initiative of Commander Ishii."

Nuance Note

This sentence establishes Kamata's unique position within the Unit 731 archive. The phrase maboroshi no shōnen-tai issei-sei (まぼろしの少年隊一期生, "phantom Youth Corps first-generation members") uses maboroshi (まぼろし / 幻, "phantom / illusion / dream") to describe a group that existed outside official records — a cohort that Ishii gathered personally before the formal Youth Corps system was established. Maboroshi is a word of haunting: it suggests something that was real but is now gone, something that left traces but no documents, something that exists in memory but not in archives. The "first generation" (issei-sei / 一期生) designation claims a priority and a purity that the later, numbered cohorts cannot claim: these were Ishii's original experiment in child recruitment, the prototype for the system that would later process hundreds of boys through the unit's machinery. The phrase Ishii butai-chō no hatsuan de (石井部隊長の発案で, "at the initiative of Commander Ishii") uses hatsuan (発案, "proposal / initiative / idea") — a word that suggests personal creativity rather than institutional procedure. Ishii did not merely approve the Youth Corps; he "hatched the idea" (hatsuan), he conceived it, he fathered it. The particle de (で) marks the means by which the boys were gathered — through Ishii's personal initiative — and the passive verb atsumareta (集められた, "were gathered") places the boys as objects of that initiative, not as agents of their own recruitment. The grammar is the grammar of children who were chosen, not of adults who chose. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese resonance of maboroshi as a literary and historical term — a word that appears in classical poetry (manyōshū / 万葉集) and modern historiography alike, carrying connotations of the vanished, the unrecoverable, the real that has become dream. Kamata's phantom first generation is not merely "unofficial"; it is spectral, a ghostly presence at the origin of the unit's child-exploitation system. The Japanese reader feels the haunting; the English reader sees a footnote.

04
The Paternal Betrayal
石井隊長をおやじと呼ぶ。
"Ishii taichō wo oyaji to yobu."
"Calling Commander Ishii 'father.'"

Nuance Note

This phrase — cited by Nishisato Fuyuko as the heading for her discussion of Kamata's testimony — contains the psychological core of Kamata's experience. The word oyaji (おやじ) is a familiar, slightly rough term for "father" — less formal than chichi (父), more intimate than otōsan (お父さん). It is the word a working-class boy might use for his father, or a soldier for his commanding officer who has earned his trust. In the context of Unit 731, the fact that Kamata called Ishii "oyaji" reveals the nature of the commander's relationship with his phantom recruits: he was not merely their superior; he was their surrogate father, the man who had "saved" them from poverty and obscurity, who had given them purpose and identity. The betrayal encoded in this term is profound: the father who led his children to the dissecting table, the oyaji who taught them to cut open living human beings. In Japanese culture, where filial piety (kō / 孝) has been a central moral virtue for millennia, the corruption of the father-son relationship into a mechanism for atrocity represents a violation that transcends the individual and touches the foundations of ethical order. The grammar is simple — wo...to yobu (を…と呼ぶ, "call...as") — but the cultural weight is immense. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese emotional register of oyaji — a word that carries connotations of rough affection, of paternal authority without distance, of the father as both protector and disciplinarian. The English "father" is accurate but flatter; oyaji is a word of complex intimacy, and its use in this context transforms Ishii from a distant commander into a familiar betrayer, from a historical figure into a personal demon. Kamata's testimony is, at its deepest level, the testimony of a son who discovered that his father was a monster — and who must now speak that discovery in the language of filial devotion turned inside out.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Kamata's testimony exists at the intersection of grammatical precision and psychological devastation. Every word has been chosen — or has survived fifty years of memory — to carry maximum weight with minimum elaboration. Translation must preserve not only the fact but the grammar of coercion, not only the content but the form of confession.

1. Yarasa Reta (やらされた) as the Grammar of Coerced Crime

JP 生体解剖をやらされた。
EN "I was made to perform vivisection." / "I was forced to perform vivisection." / "Vivisection was done through me."
Loss The passive-causative verb form yarasa reta (やらされた) is the grammatical heart of Kamata's confession, and it has no exact equivalent in English. The form is built from yaru (やる, "to do") + causative -saseru (〜させる, "make/let do") + passive -rareru (〜られる, "be done to"), producing a construction that means simultaneously: (1) someone else caused the action to happen, (2) the speaker performed the action, and (3) the speaker was affected by the action as something done to them. English can approximate this with "I was made to" (causative only), "I was forced to" (coercive but not passive), or "it was done through me" (passive but not causative), but none of these captures the triple structure of the Japanese. The grammatical precision matters because it is the form in which Kamata has chosen to bear witness: he will not say "I did it" (full agency) or "it was done to me" (no agency) but "I was made to do it" — a form that preserves the moral complexity of the child soldier who is simultaneously victim and perpetrator. What is lost in English is not merely a grammatical nuance but the entire moral architecture of the confession. The Japanese reader who encounters yarasa reta understands immediately that this is a grammar of coercion, of institutional crime, of the soldier who follows orders; the English reader must infer this from context, because the translation cannot reproduce the form that makes the meaning visible.

2. Kaibō (解剖) as Medical Term Turned Accusation

JP 生体解剖
EN "vivisection" / "dissection of a living body" / "living autopsy"
Loss The word kaibō (解剖) is the standard Japanese term for anatomical dissection — the noble foundation of modern medicine, the practice by which doctors learn the structure of the human body. Every Japanese medical student takes a kaibō course; every Japanese hospital has a kaibō department. The word carries no inherent moral weight — it is simply what doctors do to learn. When Kamata places seitai (生体, "living") before kaibō, he performs a grammatical and ethical inversion: he takes the most respectable term in medical vocabulary and makes it the name of a crime. The shock of this inversion is specific to Japanese medical culture: it is as if an American doctor were to confess to "Hippocratic vivisection" or a British surgeon to "clinical murder." The English "vivisection" is accurate as a description but lacks the specifically Japanese resonance of kaibō as a term of professional identity and ethical obligation. In English, "vivisection" has always been a controversial term; in Japanese, kaibō was a term of professional pride before it became, in the context of Unit 731, a term of historical accusation. What is lost is the shock of recognition — the moment when a Japanese reader realizes that the vocabulary of their own medical tradition has been permanently stained by what was done in its name.

3. Oyaji (おやじ) as Familial Betrayal

JP 石井隊長をおやじと呼ぶ。
EN "calling Commander Ishii 'father'" / "referring to Ishii as 'the old man'"
Loss The word oyaji (おやじ) is one of the most emotionally complex terms for "father" in Japanese. Unlike chichi (父), the formal written term, or otōsan (お父さん), the polite spoken term, oyaji is familiar, slightly rough, and emotionally direct. It is what a working-class son calls his father, what a soldier calls his respected commander, what a boy might call the man who has taken him under his wing. The word carries connotations of paternal authority without distance, of protection without sentimentality, of the father as both shelter and disciplinarian. In the context of Kamata's testimony, oyaji encodes the entire psychology of Ishii's relationship with his phantom recruits: the commander who "saved" them from poverty, who gave them identity and purpose, who then led them to atrocity. The betrayal is not merely military but filial: the oyaji who asked his children to commit crimes. English "father" captures the literal meaning but loses the specifically Japanese emotional register of oyaji as a term of rough intimacy. "Old man" captures the familiarity but loses the filial resonance. What is lost is the cultural depth of the betrayal — the violation of kō (孝, filial piety), the foundational virtue of Confucian ethics, by a father who turned his children into assistants in murder. The Japanese reader feels the violation of a sacred relationship; the English reader sees a description of a nickname.

4. Maboroshi (まぼろし) as Spectral History

JP まぼろしの少年隊一期生
EN "phantom Youth Corps first-generation members" / "unofficial Youth Corps" / "ghost first cohort"
Loss The word maboroshi (まぼろし / 幻) is one of the most evocative in Japanese, and its translation as "phantom" captures only part of its meaning. Maboroshi derives from the classical Japanese maboroshi (幻), which appears in the Man'yōshū and later poetry to describe something that appears real but is not, something that vanishes when approached, something that exists in the space between dream and waking. In modern usage, maboroshi describes things that were real but have left no trace, institutions that existed but were never documented, people who were present but have disappeared from memory. The "phantom Youth Corps" were not merely "unofficial" — they were spectral, a ghostly presence at the origin of Unit 731's child-exploitation system. The word suggests that these boys were always already half-erased, that their existence was precarious even when they were alive, that their testimony would be needed precisely because they had been made to disappear. What is lost in English is the literary and spiritual resonance of maboroshi as a term for the vanished real — the real that has become dream, the historical that has become spectral. The Japanese reader feels the haunting; the English reader sees an adjective. Kamata's testimony is, in this sense, the exorcism of a phantom — the attempt to make the spectral real, to give substance to what was made to disappear, to speak the name of the ghost that has haunted postwar Japan's medical conscience.

5. Datta Watashi (だった私) as Fixed Identity

JP 731部隊の少年兵だった私
EN "I who was a boy soldier of Unit 731" / "I, who used to be a boy soldier of Unit 731"
Loss The construction datta watashi (だった私) is a common pattern in Japanese memoir titles, but its very commonness should not obscure its grammatical and psychological precision. The past-tense copula datta (だった) creates temporal distance — "I who was" — but the first-person pronoun watashi (私) collapses that distance by claiming the identity in the present act of speech. The result is a paradox: the speaker is both distant from and identical with the self being described. This paradox is the temporal structure of trauma: the traumatic event is in the past, but its effects are in the present; the speaker has survived, but the survivor is permanently marked by what they survived. Datta watashi is the grammatical form of this paradox — a form that says: I am no longer that person, and I will always be that person. English "I who was" captures the temporal distance but loses the collapse; "I, who used to be" captures the irrevocability but loses the present claim. What is lost in English is the specifically Japanese grammar of traumatic identity the way that datta watashi holds past and present in a single phrase, refusing to release the speaker from what they were while insisting that they have survived to speak of it. The Japanese reader feels the weight of the past tense as a permanent burden; the English reader sees a biographical statement.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns from the language of coerced confession, traumatic memory, and the specific horror of vivisection testimony.

〜させられる（受け身使役） N3

...saserareru: passive-causative — "be made to do / be forced to do / have something done through oneself." Combines causative (make someone do) with passive (be done to).

生体解剖をやらされた。

"I was made to perform vivisection." — The grammatical heart of Kamata's confession: not "I did it" (full agency) nor "it was done to me" (no agency) but "I was made to do it" (distributed agency).

〜だった私（過去の自己） N4

...datta watashi: "I who was / I who used to be..." — past-tense copula + first-person pronoun, creating temporal distance that the confession simultaneously collapses.

731隊の少年兵だった私

"I who was a boy soldier of Unit 731" — The grammar of traumatic identity: the speaker is both distant from and identical with their former self.

〜の発案で（発案・立案） N3

...no hatsuan de: "at the initiative of / through the proposal of..." — attributes creative agency to a person rather than an institution.

石井部隊長の発案で集められた

"gathered at the initiative of Commander Ishii" — Hatsuan suggests personal conception rather than bureaucratic procedure; Ishii "hatched the idea" of the phantom Youth Corps.

〜を〜と呼ぶ（呼称） N5

...wo...to yobu: "to call...as / to refer to...as..." — simple construction that carries complex emotional and social weight.

石井隊長をおやじと呼ぶ

"calling Commander Ishii 'father'" — The grammar of filial betrayal: a simple verb construction that encodes the entire psychology of paternal grooming.

〜の〜（所有・定義） N5

...no...: genitive particle — creates relationships of belonging, definition, and identity.

731部隊の少年兵

"a boy soldier of Unit 731" — The no particle defines the boy soldier by his belonging to the unit; the identity is constituted by the institution.

〜で集められた（受け身・手段） N4

...de atsumareta: "were gathered by means of / through..." — passive voice with instrumental de marking the means of recruitment.

石井部隊長の発案で集められた

"were gathered through Commander Ishii's initiative" — The boys are grammatical objects (atsumareta, passive) of Ishii's agency (hatsuan, initiative).

〜をやらされた（他動詞の受身使役） N3

...wo yarasa reta: "was made to do [direct object]" — transitive verb in passive-causative with direct object marked by wo.

生体解剖をやらされた

"I was made to perform vivisection" — The direct object wo places the crime before the verb, giving it emphasis and unalterable factuality.

まぼろしの〜（修飾・幽霊性） N3

maboroshi no...: "phantom... / spectral... / illusory..." — adjectival modifier that transforms its noun into something haunting and half-erased.

まぼろしの少年隊一期生

"phantom Youth Corps first-generation members" — Maboroshi makes the recruits spectral: real but undocumented, present but vanished, historical but ghostly.

Study tip: Kamata's grammar is the grammar of coercion, confession, and traumatic memory. Pay attention to how -saserareru distributes agency between the speaker and the structure that compelled them, how datta watashi holds past and present in a single identity, how hatsuan de atsumareta makes children the objects of a commander's initiative, and how wo...to yobu encodes the psychology of filial betrayal in a simple verb phrase. The grammar is as precise as the memory it preserves — and as impossible to fully translate.
