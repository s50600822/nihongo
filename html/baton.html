Title Analysis

Title Grammar: The Passive Voice of Inherited Love

The title Soshite, Baton wa Watasareta (そして、バトンは渡された, "And So, the Baton Was Passed") is a study in the grammar of continuity and care. Soshite (そして, "and / and then / and so") is the most neutral of Japanese conjunctions — not causal like dakara (だから, "therefore") nor temporal like sono toki (その時, "at that time"), but simply sequential, the "and" that connects one event to the next without imposing a logic on their relationship. It is the conjunction of stories that continue rather than conclude, of lives that flow into one another without dramatic rupture. The comma that follows soshite creates a pause, a breath, a moment of transition before the main clause arrives — and this pause is the temporal space in which a child waits to be claimed, in which love changes hands, in which one life becomes responsible for another. Baton (バトン) is the katakana rendering of the English word "baton," and its foreignness is significant: the relay race (riree shingusen / リレー競走) is not an indigenous Japanese sport but an imported athletic form, and the baton is its central symbol — the object that cannot be dropped, the connection that must be maintained, the responsibility that passes from hand to hand. By using the katakana baton rather than a Japanese word for "inheritance" or "transfer," the title signals that the family form being described is not the traditional Japanese ie (家) system of bloodline and household continuity but something newer, more fluid, more voluntary — a relay of care that operates by choice rather than obligation. Wa (は) marks the baton as the topic, the thing already under discussion, the assumed subject of the family's ongoing conversation. And then comes the grammatical heart of the title: watasareta (渡された), the passive past tense of watasu (渡す, "to hand over / to pass / to ferry across"). The passive voice is everything here. The title does not say "someone passed the baton" — it says "the baton was passed," making the object of care (the child, the love, the responsibility) into the grammatical subject and the agents (the parents, the step-parents, the adults who choose to love) into an unnamed, collective presence. This is the grammar of a child who experiences love as something that arrives, that is given, that is passed along a chain of hands she did not choose but that chose her. The passive voice is not weakness; it is accuracy. For a child, love is received before it is given, is passed before it is grasped. The title's grammar encodes the entire novel's ethical argument: that love is real even when it is inherited rather than originated, that family is valid even when it is chosen rather than given, that the baton of care retains its value no matter how many hands have held it.

Title Breakdown

そして soshite / "and / and so" "And" — The neutral sequential conjunction that connects events without imposing causality. Unlike dakara (だから, "therefore"), which would make the title into a moral conclusion, or sono toki (その時, "at that time"), which would make it a specific moment, soshite is the conjunction of ongoing narrative, of stories that accumulate rather than resolve. It appears at the beginning of countless Japanese sentences, carrying the reader forward from one event to the next with the quiet persistence of daily life. In the title, soshite performs the novel's temporal structure: this is not a story with a beginning and an end but a story of "and then, and then, and then" — the continuous passing of the baton from one hand to the next, one generation to the next, one form of love to the next. The comma that follows creates a caesura, a breath, the pause in which a child looks up to see who will claim her next — and this pause is the emotional center of the novel, the space of uncertainty that precedes the certainty of being loved.
バトン baton / "baton" "baton" — The katakana rendering of the English word "baton," specifically the cylindrical object passed between runners in a relay race. The choice of this foreign term over Japanese equivalents is deliberate and thematically loaded. Japanese has words for inheritance (isō / 遺贈), transfer (ijō / 譲渡), and entrustment (itaku / 委託), but none of these carry the specific connotations of the relay baton: the speed of the exchange, the necessity of physical contact, the impossibility of going backward, the fact that the baton must keep moving or the race is lost. The baton is also, in Japanese sports culture, a symbol of national pride — the women's 4x100m relay team at the Tokyo Olympics became famous for their flawless baton exchanges — and this association adds a layer of collective endeavor to the personal story. The baton in the title is not merely an object but a metaphor for the entire economy of care: the child as baton, passed from parent to step-parent to step-parent, each exchange requiring precision, trust, and the willingness to keep running. The katakana script (baton / バトン) makes the word feel modern, imported, slightly technical — which is exactly right for a novel about a family form (the step-family relay) that is itself a modern, imported, still-developing social structure in Japan.
は wa / "[topic marker]" "[topic marker]" — The particle wa (は) marks the baton as the topic of the sentence, the thing already under discussion, the given subject of the family's ongoing narrative. The wa performs a crucial operation: it makes the baton (and by extension, the child who is the baton's referent) into the stable center around which the sentence — and the novel — revolves. The parents change; the family form changes; the surname changes (four times, in fact); but the baton remains the topic. This grammatical stability within narrative flux is the novel's deepest comfort: no matter how many hands hold it, the baton itself does not change. The child at the center of all this passing — Morimiya Yūko — is always the same person, always worthy of love, always the topic of the sentence that is her life. The wa also creates a subtle emphasis: not "a baton was passed" (indefinite, one among many) but "the baton was passed" (definite, the one we have been talking about, the one that matters). This definiteness is the novel's moral claim: this child's care is not interchangeable or disposable; it is specific, precious, and non-negotiable.
渡された watasareta / "was passed" "was passed" — The passive past tense of watasu (渡す, "to hand over / to pass / to ferry across"), a verb whose range of meaning is essential to the title's resonance. Watasu literally means "to make something cross" — the causative form of wataru (渡る, "to cross / to go across"), as in crossing a river, a road, or a boundary. The verb thus carries an inherent spatial metaphor: to watasu is to help something move from one side to another, to ferry it across a gap. In the context of family, this gap is everything that separates one household from another, one marriage from the next, one form of love from the next. The child must be watasareta — helped across — because she cannot cross alone. The passive form (-sareta / された) is the title's most important grammatical decision. It makes the baton/child into the grammatical subject and the parents into an unnamed collective. This is not "Parent A passed the baton to Parent B" — a story of adult decisions — but "the baton was passed" — a story of what the child experiences, receives, survives. The passive voice has often been criticized in English writing guides as weak or evasive, but in Japanese it is a neutral and common construction, and here it performs essential ethical work: it keeps the focus on the experience of being cared for rather than on the drama of adult choices. The novel is not interested in judging the parents who passed Yūko along; it is interested in what Yūko received at each exchange, what love remained in the baton when it reached her hands, and what she will do with it when her own turn comes to pass it on. The past tense (-ta / た) gives the title the quality of a completed action, a fact established — and yet the soshite (そして) at the beginning opens toward the future, making the title into both a statement ("the baton was passed") and a promise ("and so, the baton will continue to be passed"). This double temporality — completed past and open future — is the novel's narrative architecture, moving between Yūko's remembered childhood and her present as a high school senior who stands on the threshold of becoming a baton-passer herself.

Author

瀬尾まいこ — Seo Maiko (b. 1974)

瀬

瀬尾まいこ / Seo Maiko

The teacher who wrote novels between classes · chronicler of chosen families · believer in love without villains

Seo Maiko was born on January 16, 1974, in Osaka City, Osaka Prefecture, Japan. She graduated from the Japanese Literature Department of Otani Women's University (大谷女子大学文学部国文学科) and, after struggling for years to pass the teacher employment examination, became a middle school Japanese language teacher — a profession she would hold for nearly a decade while simultaneously building one of the most distinctive careers in contemporary Japanese fiction. Her origin story as a writer is almost too perfect to be believed: unable to pass the competitive teacher recruitment exam, she decided she needed something to "self-PR" with — a portfolio, a credential, a demonstration of ability beyond test scores. So she wrote a novel. In 2001, that novel — "Tamago no O" (卵の緒, "The Umbilical Cord of the Egg") — won the Grand Prize of the 7th Bocchan Literary Prize (第7回坊っちゃん文学賞), one of Japan's most prestigious awards for emerging writers. The following year, it was published as a book, and Seo Maiko the novelist was born — while Seo Maiko the teacher continued grading papers and preparing lesson plans.

For nine years, she lived a double life that would shape her fiction's understanding of ordinary people's inner lives: teaching teenagers by day, writing about them by night. She finally passed the teacher employment exam in 2004 and worked as a full-time Japanese teacher at a middle school from 2005 until her resignation in 2011. Her subsequent novels — Kōfuku na Shokutaku (幸福な食卓, "The Happy Dinner Table," 2005, winner of the 26th Yoshikawa Eiji Literary Newcomer Prize / 吉川英治文学新人賞), Tomura Hanten Seishun 100 Rensatsu (戸村飯店 青春100連発, "Tomura Restaurant: 100 Rapid-Fire Youth Stories," 2008, winner of the 24th Tsubota Jōji Literary Prize / 坪田譲治文学賞), and numerous others — established her as a writer of extraordinary warmth and precision, capable of finding the emotional epicenter of seemingly mundane domestic scenes. In 2013, she received the Sakuya Konohana Prize (咲くやこの花賞) from Osaka City for her contributions to culture. She lives in Higashi-Osaka City with her family and has described her writing philosophy with characteristic modesty: "I want to write novels that make people feel a little lighter, a little happier."

And So, the Baton Was Passed represents the culmination of Seo's two-decade exploration of what she calls "love that is not given to lovers" (koibito ni wa sosoganai ai / 恋人には注がない種類の愛情) — the love between parents and children, between step-parents and step-children, between people who choose to be family rather than being given family by blood. Seo has said that she has never written a novel with a "villain" (悪人) because she believes that "there are many terrible people, but there are even more good people." This optimism is not naivety; it is a moral choice, a decision to illuminate the mechanisms of care rather than the machinery of cruelty. Her fiction operates on the assumption that most people are trying their best with what they have, and that "their best" — however inadequate, however clumsy, however incomplete — is still a form of love worth recognizing. The result is a body of work that has made her one of Japan's most beloved novelists, with multiple film adaptations (The Happy Dinner Table in 2007, Our Meal for Tomorrow / 僕らのごはんは明日で待ってる in 2017, All the Long Nights / 夜明けのすべて in 2024) and a readership that spans generations. In 2026, her novel Arika (ありか) placed 7th in the Japan Booksellers' Award, confirming her continuing relevance two decades after her debut.

History

Changing Families, Chosen Families, and the Japanese Step-Family

The Decline of the Traditional Ie and the Rise of the Reconstructed Family

The traditional Japanese family system — the ie (家) — was a patriarchal, patrilineal household unit that prioritized the continuation of the family line over individual happiness. Under this system, which was codified in the Civil Code of 1898 and remained legally dominant until the postwar constitution of 1947, the household head held absolute authority over family members, marriage was arranged to serve household interests, and adoption (including adult adoption) was common as a means of securing heirs. The ie system was officially abolished in the legal reforms of 1947, but its cultural influence persisted for decades: the "standard" Japanese family remained understood as a nuclear unit with a male breadwinner, a female homemaker, and children who carried the father's surname. What the ie system could not accommodate — and what postwar Japanese society struggled to acknowledge — was the reconstructed family: the step-family, the blended family, the family formed by remarriage after death or divorce. The novel's historical significance lies in its calm, unapologetic portrayal of a family form that has always existed but has rarely been celebrated in Japanese culture. Yūko's four surnames — Mito (水戸), Tanaka (田中), Izumigahara (泉ヶ原), Morimiya (森宮) — trace a path through the social history of Japanese family transformation: from the patrilineal name inherited at birth, through the names of step-fathers who married her mother or father, to the name she finally chooses to keep. Each surname change is a bureaucratic event (registered at the city hall, recorded on the family register / koseki / 戸籍) and an emotional earthquake — and Seo's genius is to show that these earthquakes, while real, do not have to be destructive. The ground shifts; the child adjusts; the love remains.

Divorce, Remarriage, and the Child in Between

Japan's divorce rate rose steadily from the 1960s through the 2000s, peaking in the early 2000s at roughly 30% of marriages (though it has since stabilized and slightly declined). Remarriage after divorce has historically been more common for men than for women, and custody arrangements have traditionally favored mothers — but with significant variation and ongoing legal evolution. The novel's depiction of Yūko's passage through multiple households reflects a social reality that has become increasingly common but remains underrepresented in Japanese fiction: the child of divorced parents who becomes, effectively, a member of multiple families, maintaining relationships with biological parents, step-parents, and the extended networks of each. What distinguishes Seo's treatment from earlier, more melodramatic representations is her refusal of the "tragic step-child" narrative. Yūko is not Cinderella; she is not abused by her step-parents; she is not a victim of adult irresponsibility. She is, rather, a beneficiary of an extended network of care that happens to operate across household boundaries. The novel's historical contribution is to normalize this experience — to show that a child with three fathers and two mothers is not a social problem to be solved but a social form to be understood, even celebrated. This was a genuinely radical proposition in 2018, when the novel was published, and it remains culturally significant in a Japan where the "standard family" (hyōjun kazoku / 標準家族) discourse still exerts powerful normative pressure.

The Booksellers' Award and the Novel of National Comfort

The Japan Booksellers' Award (本屋大賞), established in 2007, is unique among Japan's major literary prizes in that it is voted on by booksellers — the people who stand on the front lines of reading culture, who see what customers pick up and put down, who hear the confidences of regular readers, who know which books make people cry at the counter and which ones they return the next day to buy for a friend. The award has become a reliable indicator of what might be called "national comfort literature" — books that do not merely entertain or impress but that make readers feel something they needed to feel, that give them language for experiences they could not name, that leave them somehow more whole than they were before. And So, the Baton Was Passed won the 16th iteration of this prize with 435 points, a decisive margin over the second-place finisher (297.5 points). The booksellers' comments, collected on the prize's website, reveal a consistent theme: this was the book they wanted to press into customers' hands, the book they believed would help people. "I want to recommend this to every customer who comes to the store," wrote one. "This is the family novel I've been waiting for," wrote another. "I cried with happiness," wrote a third. The award's significance is not merely literary but therapeutic: in a period of national anxiety about declining birth rates, aging populations, and the "disintegration" of the family, Seo's novel offered a counter-narrative — not that families are disappearing but that they are transforming, that the love formerly concentrated in the nuclear unit is now distributed across networks of choice and care, and that this distribution is not a loss but a gain. The novel became, in effect, a national comfort object — a book that booksellers could sell not merely as entertainment but as emotional first aid.

Structure

Four Surnames, Seven Family Forms, One Unbroken Chain of Love

The Architecture: Dual Timeline as Relay Race

The novel is constructed in two parts — a structure that mirrors the relay race of its title. Part One operates primarily in the "present" of Yūko's third year of high school, with flashbacks to her childhood and the successive family forms she has inhabited. Part Two extends the timeline forward, showing Yūko as a young adult making her own choices about love, marriage, and the baton she will eventually pass. This dual structure creates a narrative relay of its own: the reader receives the baton of Yūko's childhood in Part One and carries it into her adulthood in Part Two, experiencing the continuity of her character across the apparent discontinuity of her family circumstances. The novel's most distinctive formal feature is its handling of time: rather than organizing the narrative around dramatic events (deaths, divorces, departures), Seo organizes it around daily rituals — meals, conversations, small gestures of care. The big events happen "off-screen" or in summary; the page-time is devoted to what happens between crises, the ordinary maintenance of love across the gaps that crises create. This is the novel's deepest formal insight: that family is not made in dramatic moments but in the accumulation of small attentions, and that the baton of care is passed not in grand gestures but in the daily decision to keep showing up.

Yūko's Four Surnames: A Genealogy of Care

Morimiya Yūko (森宮優子), 17, begins the novel with a family history that would, in conventional fiction, mark her as a problem child, a statistic, a symptom of social breakdown. She was born Mito Yūko (水戸優子), daughter of Mito — her biological father — and a mother who died when Yūko was very young. After her mother's death, her father remarried, and she became Tanaka Yūko (田中優子), taking the surname of her first step-father, Tanaka. When that marriage ended, she passed to the household of Izumigahara (泉ヶ原), becoming Izumigahara Yūko (泉ヶ原優子) under the care of her mother's second husband. When that marriage, too, ended in divorce, she became Morimiya Yūko (森宮優子), the daughter of Morimiya Sōsuke (森宮壮介), her father's former classmate who married Yūko's step-mother Rika (梨花) — a chain of connections so complex that explaining it requires a diagram. And yet. And yet Yūko insists, from the novel's opening pages, that she is "not at all unhappy" (全然不幸ではないのだ). This insistence is not denial; it is description. Each of the adults who passed her along — her biological father, her two step-fathers, her step-mother, her biological mother's second husband — loved her. Each made the decision to care for her, to feed her, to attend her school events, to worry about her future. The novel's genius is to make this love visible without sentimentalizing it, to show that care can be real even when the marriage that created its container has dissolved. Yūko's four surnames are not scars; they are signatures — the autographs of adults who, however temporarily, however imperfectly, chose to be her family.

Morimiya Sōsuke: The Accidental Father

Morimiya Sōsuke (森宮壮介), Yūko's third and current father, is one of the most beloved father figures in contemporary Japanese fiction — and he is, by conventional standards, a deeply improbable one. A Tokyo University graduate who works at a major corporation, Sōsuke is only twenty years older than Yūko. He married Rika, Yūko's step-mother, after she divorced Yūko's previous step-father — and when Rika, too, left (for reasons the novel reveals with its characteristic gentleness), Sōsuke did not send Yūko away. He kept her. This keeping is the novel's moral climax, though it happens quietly, without drama, in the space of a sentence. Sōsuke is not heroic; he is awkward, slightly clueless, comically bad at domestic tasks, prone to saying the wrong thing at the wrong time. He is, in other words, a real person — not a fantasy father but a man who finds himself responsible for a teenage girl he did not create and decides, simply, to do his best. His "best" includes learning to cook (badly), attending her parent-teacher conferences (reluctantly), and pretending not to notice when she falls in love — all rendered with Seo's trademark blend of humor and tenderness. The novel's deepest emotional payoff comes from watching Sōsuke grow into fatherhood not through biological imperative but through daily practice, through the accumulation of small decisions to be present, to pay attention, to care. By the novel's end, he has become Yūko's father not by blood or law but by the only criterion that matters: he was there.

Rika, the Step-Mothers, and the Mothers Who Left

The novel's female characters — Yūko's biological mother (dead before the novel begins, present only in memory and photograph), her step-mother Rika (梨花), her father's various partners — form a counter-chorus to the father figures, and their presence complicates any simple celebration of chosen family. Rika, in particular, is a masterpiece of moral ambiguity: she loves Yūko, genuinely and deeply, and she also leaves her, for reasons that the novel treats with absolute respect. Seo refuses to make Rika into a villain or a saint; she is simply a woman who made a choice, who loved a child she did not bear and then chose a different life. The novel's treatment of this departure — neither condemning nor excusing — is one of its most mature achievements. It acknowledges that love and presence are not the same thing, that one can love a child and still leave, that the baton can be passed with genuine affection and still be a passing rather than a keeping. Yūko's response to Rika's departure — not bitterness but a kind of resigned understanding, a recognition that adults have their own lives — is the emotional work of a child who has learned too early that love does not guarantee permanence. And yet the novel insists, against all the evidence of departure, that the love remains. Rika loved Yūko. That fact is not erased by her leaving. The baton, once passed, retains the warmth of the hand that held it.

Text

Key Passages

These passages capture the novel's radical insistence that love is not diminished by being inherited, that family is not invalidated by being chosen, and that the baton of care gains rather than loses value with each exchange.

01
"I Have Three Fathers and Two Mothers"
私には父親が三人、母親が二人いる。家族の形態は、十七年間で七回も変わった。でも、全然不幸ではないのだ。
"Watashi ni wa chichioya ga sannin, hahaoya ga futari iru. Kazoku no keitai wa, jūnana-nenkan de nanakai mo kawatta. Demo, zenzen fukō dewa nai no da."
"I have three fathers and two mothers. My family form changed seven times in seventeen years. But I am not at all unhappy."

Nuance Note

This opening sentence — one of the most famous in recent Japanese fiction — is a masterpiece of paradoxical declaration, and its grammar encodes the novel's entire argument in a single breath. Watashi ni wa (私には, "as for me / in my case") uses the dative ni (に) with the topic marker wa (は) to create a construction of possession that is slightly more emphatic than the standard watashi wa... ga iru (私は...がいる). The ni wa construction suggests "speaking for myself" or "in my particular case," implying that what follows is specific, even unusual — which the numbers immediately confirm. Chichioya ga sannin (父親が三人, "fathers [subject] three people") and hahaoya ga futari (母親が二人, "mothers [subject] two people") use the subject marker ga (が) with numerical classifiers (-nin / 人 for people) to state, with clinical precision, a family configuration that defies Japanese social norms. The ga marks this as new information, a fact being introduced for the first time — and the fact is so startling that it requires no adjective, no emotional commentary. The numbers speak. Iru (いる, "exist / are present / am") is the verb of animate existence, used for people and animals rather than things — and its use here makes the fathers and mothers into living presences rather than abstract categories. Kazoku no keitai wa (家族の形態は, "the family form [topic]") uses the formal noun keitai (形態, "form / shape / configuration") rather than the more common katachi (形, "shape / form"), giving the sentence a slightly sociological flavor — this is not merely "what my family looked like" but "the configuration of my family unit," as if describing a phenomenon. Jūnana-nenkan de nanakai mo kawatta (十七年間で七回も変わった, "changed seven times in seventeen years") uses the durational de (で, "in / over") with the emphatic particle mo (も) attached to nanakai (七回, "seven times") to create a sense of excessive frequency — the mo implies "as many as seven times" or "even seven times," suggesting that this is more change than should be expected, more instability than is normal. Demo (でも, "but / however") is the shortest possible adversative, a single syllable that turns the entire sentence around — all of this, everything I have just told you, is not what you think. Zenzen fukō dewa nai no da (全然不幸ではないのだ, "is not at all unhappy / the fact is that I am not unhappy") uses the emphatic negative zenzen... nai (全然...ない, "not at all / completely not") with the explanatory nominalizer no da (のだ) to make a statement that is simultaneously descriptive and explanatory — not merely "I am not unhappy" but "the fact is, I am not unhappy [and here is why]." The no da gives the sentence the quality of a discovered truth, a realization that has emerged from reflection rather than a defensive denial. What is lost in English is the full force of zenzen (全然) in this negative construction. In standard Japanese grammar, zenzen is used with negatives to mean "not at all" — but it carries an emotional intensity that "not at all" cannot fully capture. Zenzen is the word of absolute negation, the word that sweeps away all possible objections, the word that says "whatever you are thinking, stop." Combined with the explanatory no da, it creates a sentence that feels like a door being firmly closed on pity — and then opened onto something unexpected. The English "not at all unhappy" captures the logic but loses the defiance, the slight edge of irritation at being assumed unhappy, the preemptive strike against the reader's anticipated sympathy. The Japanese sentence is not merely informing us; it is correcting us.

02
"Real Misfortune Is Unbearable to Look At"
本物の不幸は目も当てられないし、不幸な自分に酔っなんかいられないよ。
"Honmono no fukō wa me mo aterarenai shi, fukō na jibun ni yotte nanka irarenai yo."
"Real misfortune is unbearable to look at, and you can't afford to get drunk on being unhappy."

Nuance Note

This sentence — spoken by Yūko in response to a teacher's well-meaning concern — is the novel's philosophical center, and its grammar creates a theory of suffering that distinguishes between genuine tragedy and its performance. Honmono no fukō wa (本物の不幸は, "real misfortune [topic]") uses the noun honmono (本物, "the real thing / genuine article") — the word used for authentic brand-name goods as opposed to knockoffs — to create a distinction between authentic and inauthentic suffering. The no (の) makes honmono into an adjectival modifier of fukō (不幸, "misfortune / unhappiness"), and the wa (は) marks this category as the topic of discourse. The choice of honmono is quietly devastating: it implies that most of what people call "misfortune" is counterfeit, performance, a knockoff of the real thing. Me mo aterarenai shi (目も当てられないし, "cannot even bear to look at / is unbearable to see, and...") uses the potential negative of me wo ateru (目を当てる, "to look at / to cast one's eyes on") with the enumerative particle shi (し, "and / moreover") to create a list of reasons why "real misfortune" is different from what Yūko has experienced. Me mo aterarenai (目も当てられない) is an idiom meaning "unbearable to look at" — the mo (も, "even") making it "cannot even look at," suggesting that real misfortune is so extreme that it repels observation. Fukō na jibun ni yotte nanka irarenai yo (不幸な自分に酔ってなんかいられないよ, "can't afford to get drunk on being unhappy / can't go around getting intoxicated with being a victim") uses the continuative yotte (酔って, "being drunk / intoxicated") from you (酔う, "to get drunk / to be intoxicated") with the particle ni (に) to create the metaphor of intoxication by one's own unhappiness. Yotte irarenai (酔っていられない) is the negative potential of the continuous form — "cannot be (continuously) intoxicated" — with nanka (なんか, "like / sort of / anything") adding a dismissive, casual tone. The final yo (よ) is the assertive particle, marking this as a statement of fact being shared with the listener. What is lost in English is the specific cultural resonance of honmono (本物) in a consumer society. The word evokes the world of branded goods, counterfeit products, and authenticity markers — a distinctly modern, capitalist vocabulary applied to the ancient category of human suffering. The effect is to make suffering feel, momentarily, like a product — something that can be genuine or fake, authentic or performed — and this commodification of emotion is precisely the novel's target. Yūko is saying, in effect: "What I have experienced is not the real thing. The real thing is so much worse that you cannot even look at it. What I have is something else — complicated, difficult, but not the genuine article of misfortune. And therefore I will not perform unhappiness for you. I will not get drunk on my own suffering and call it identity." This is a radical statement in a culture that increasingly rewards the performance of victimhood, and the grammar of honmono makes it feel not like moralism but like discernment — the hard-won knowledge of someone who has seen enough to know the difference.

03
"It's Hard Work, Always Being Surrounded by Good People"
いつもいい人に囲まれてるっていうのも、たいへんなんだよね。
"Itsumo ii hito ni kakomareteru tte iu no mo, taihen nanda yo ne."
"Always being surrounded by good people — that's hard work too, you know."

Nuance Note

This sentence — Yūko's dry observation to her teacher — is the novel's most subversive line, and its grammar turns the entire discourse of "gratitude" on its head. Itsumo ii hito ni kakomareteru (いつもいい人に囲まれてる, "always being surrounded by good people") uses the continuous passive kakomareteru (囲まれてる, informal contraction of kakomarete iru / 囲まれている) from kakomu (囲む, "to surround / to encircle"). The passive voice is essential: Yūko is not saying "I surround myself with good people" (active, agentive, volitional) but "I am surrounded by good people" (passive, receptive, the beneficiary of others' choices). The itsumo (いつも, "always") makes this surround into a permanent condition rather than a temporary state — and it is this permanence, paradoxically, that creates the burden. Tte iu no mo (っていうのも, "the fact that / saying that") uses the quotative tte (って) with the nominalizer no (の) and the particle mo (も, "also / even") to create a construction meaning "even the fact that..." or "the thing called... also." The mo is crucial: it implies that this is one difficulty among others, that being surrounded by good people is not an unmixed blessing but a form of labor that exists alongside other forms. Taihen nanda yo ne (大変なんだよね, "is hard work / is tough, you know / right?") uses the explanatory nanda (なんだ, contraction of no da / のだ) with the assertive yo (よ) and the seeking-agreement ne (ね) to create a sentence that is simultaneously declarative and conversational — "it's hard work, you know?" The taihen (大変, "hard / tough / serious") is a word of genuine difficulty, not mere inconvenience; it is the word used for disasters, for crises, for things that require real effort to manage. What is lost in English is the full paradox of the sentence in Japanese cultural context. In a society that places enormous emphasis on on (恩, "indebtedness / obligation") — the debt one owes to those who have helped you — Yūko's statement is almost shocking. She is saying that gratitude itself is labor, that being the recipient of others' goodness creates a burden of response, that "being surrounded by good people" is not merely pleasant but demanding. The sentence thus performs a subtle critique of the gratitude economy: the expectation that beneficiaries of kindness must perform thankfulness, must acknowledge their debt, must never complain. Yūko complains — gently, humorously, but unmistakably — and in doing so, she claims the right to feel the full complexity of her situation, including the exhaustion of being perpetually grateful. The English "hard work" captures the meaning but loses the cultural specificity: in Japanese, taihen carries connotations of social expectation, of the proper performance of a role, of the labor required to maintain harmonious relations. Yūko is not saying that good people are difficult; she is saying that being perpetually surrounded by them — being perpetually in their debt, perpetually required to be grateful — is its own form of taihen, its own social labor. The sentence is funny because it is true in a way that polite society refuses to acknowledge, and its grammar — the casual tte iu no mo, the seeking-agreement ne — makes it feel like a confidence shared between friends rather than a philosophical manifesto.

04
"The Next Time You Get Married, Could You Please Be a Mean Person?"
「森宮さん、次に結婚するとしたら、意地悪な人としてくれないかな」
"Morimiya-san, tsugi ni kekkon suru to shitara, ijiwaru na hito to shite kurenai kana."
"Morimiya-san, if you get married again next time, could you please be a mean person?"

Nuance Note

This sentence — Yūko's teasing request to her step-father Sōsuke — is a masterpiece of indirect emotional communication, and its grammar encodes an entire theory of step-family dynamics in a single joke. Morimiya-san (森宮さん) — the surname plus honorific — is how Yūko addresses her current step-father, and the choice of address is significant. She does not call him "Father" (otō-san / お父さん) or even "Sōsuke-san" (壮介さん); she uses the family name, maintaining a slight distance that is both protective and accurate. This is not her biological father; this is a man who married her step-mother and then kept her when the step-mother left. The distance in the address honors the reality of their relationship — chosen, not given — while the -san (さん) maintains respect and affection. Tsugi ni kekkon suru to shitara (次に結婚するとしたら, "if [you] were to get married next time") uses the conditional to shitara (としたら, "if it were the case that / supposing that") to create a hypothetical scenario — Sōsuke's potential future remarriage. The to shitara is a soft conditional, less direct than tara (たら) or eba (えば), carrying a tone of speculation rather than demand. Yūko is not saying "when you remarry" (presumptive) but "if you were to marry again" (hypothetical, distant, almost playful). Ijiwaru na hito to shite kurenai kana (意地悪な人としてくれないかな, "could you please be a mean person?") is the grammatical and emotional core. Ijiwaru na hito (意地悪な人, "mean person / unkind person / teasing person") uses the na-adjective ijiwaru (意地悪, "mean-spirited / unkind / teasing") to describe the desired type of person. Ijiwaru is not cruelty; it is a lighter, more everyday unkindness — the person who makes jokes at your expense, who is slightly difficult, who does not indulge you. To shite (として, "as / in the role of") makes this into a role request — "be as a mean person" — and kurenai kana (くれないかな, "won't you give me / I wonder if you would") uses the negative request form with the wondering particle kana (かな) to create a sentence that is simultaneously a request and a speculation. The grammar is indirect, polite, almost wistful — and the content is outrageous. Yūko is asking her step-father to be unkind. Why? Because, as she explains in the surrounding context, "a slightly bad person would be more convenient in some ways." The logic is impeccable: good people leave (because they have consciences, because they feel guilty, because they cannot sustain a relationship built on obligation). Mean people, by contrast, might stay — because they do not care enough to feel guilty, because they are not paralyzed by the need to do the right thing. The joke contains a profound observation about the relationship between goodness and departure: it is the good step-parents who torture themselves with whether they have the right to stay, while the bad ones would simply remain, unbothered. Yūko's request is thus not merely funny; it is a strategy of survival, a wish for the kind of step-parent who will not be tormented into leaving. What is lost in English is the full range of ijiwaru (意地悪), which occupies a specific space in Japanese emotional vocabulary. It is not "evil" (aku / 悪) or "cruel" (zankoku / 残酷) or "wicked" (warui / 悪い); it is the everyday meanness of someone who hides your shoes, who eats the last piece of cake, who makes a joke that stings slightly. It is the word children use for older siblings, for teasing friends, for adults who will not indulge them. By asking for an ijiwaru step-parent, Yūko is asking for someone who will treat her like family — like a sibling, like a child who can be teased because she is not going anywhere. The English "mean person" captures the general meaning but loses the domestic, familial resonance of ijiwaru, its association with the rough affection of long-term intimacy. And the kurenai kana (くれないかな) — "I wonder if you would" — is almost impossible to translate: it is the grammar of a wish made in the form of a question, a desire expressed as speculation, a child's prayer disguised as a joke. The English "could you please" is too direct, too imperative; "I wonder if you would" is too tentative, too adult. The Japanese original holds both registers simultaneously — the directness of the wish and the indirection of its expression — and this double register is the voice of a child who has learned to ask for love without appearing to need it.

Translation

Nuance & What Translation Loss

Seo writes with the precision of a teacher who knows that the most important lessons are delivered in the simplest sentences — and the translation of her most culturally specific novel raises urgent questions about how to carry the grammar of chosen family across linguistic boundaries.

1. Watasu (渡す): The Verb of Ferried Care

JP 渡す — to hand over; to pass; to ferry across a gap; to help something cross from one side to another
EN "pass" / "hand over" / "transfer" — each captures one dimension but loses the spatial metaphor of crossing
Loss The verb watasu (渡す) is the title's gravitational center, and its translation is almost impossible to do justice. Etymologically, watasu is the causative of wataru (渡る, "to cross / to go across"), and its core meaning is "to help something cross" — a river, a road, a boundary, a gap. This spatial metaphor is essential to the novel's understanding of family transition: the child must be helped across the space between one household and another, one marriage and the next. English "pass" captures the relay-race connotation but loses the crossing; "hand over" captures the physical gesture but loses the sense of helping across a gap; "transfer" is too bureaucratic; "give" is too general. The Japanese watasu contains within it an image of ferrying — the child as passenger, the adult as ferryman, the gap between families as a river that cannot be crossed alone. This image is activated every time the word appears in the novel, creating a consistent spatial metaphor for what might otherwise seem like abstract social arrangements. The English translation can convey the action but cannot reproduce the landscape in which the action occurs — the river, the gap, the helping hand that makes crossing possible. The loss is imagistic and philosophical: without watasu's inherent spatiality, the novel's title becomes merely a sports metaphor rather than a theory of how care moves across the boundaries that divide human lives.

2. -rare (られ) Passive: The Grammar of Received Love

JP 渡された — was passed; the passive voice that makes the child/baton the grammatical subject and the parents an unnamed collective
EN "was passed" — grammatically correct but culturally loaded with English prejudices against the passive voice
Loss The Japanese passive voice — marked by the auxiliary verb rareru (られる) or its contractions (-rareru / -られる, -reru / -れる, -sareru / -される) — is grammatically and culturally distinct from the English passive, and the title's use of it encodes a worldview that English cannot easily accommodate. In English writing pedagogy, the passive voice is often taught as weak, evasive, or bureaucratic — the voice of responsibility avoided, of agency obscured. In Japanese, the passive is a neutral and common construction with no such stigma, and in the title baton wa watasareta (バトンは渡された), it performs essential ethical work. By making the baton/child into the grammatical subject, the passive voice centers the experience of the one who receives care rather than the drama of those who provide it. The English "was passed" reproduces this centering grammatically but cannot escape the cultural baggage of English passive prejudice: an English reader, trained to suspect the passive, may unconsciously read the title as evasive, as hiding something, as refusing to name who did the passing. The Japanese reader has no such suspicion; the passive is simply the appropriate voice for describing an experience that is, by definition, received rather than initiated. The loss is thus not merely grammatical but hermeneutic: the English title may trigger interpretive protocols ("who is hiding what?") that the Japanese title does not, subtly shifting the novel's ethical framework from "the experience of being loved" to "the mystery of who loved." This is not the translator's fault; it is the consequence of carrying a grammatical form across a cultural boundary where that form carries different meanings.

3. Honmono (本物): The Authenticity of Suffering

JP 本物 — the real thing; genuine article; authentic; the word from branded goods and counterfeit markets applied to human suffering
EN "real" / "genuine" / "authentic" — accurate but lose the consumer-culture resonance
Loss As discussed in the passage analysis, honmono (本物) carries powerful associations with consumer culture, branded goods, and the distinction between authentic and counterfeit products. In Japan, where counterfeit luxury goods are a visible social issue and where "Japan-quality" (made in Japan) is a marker of authenticity, honmono is a word that resonates across contexts — from shopping to relationships to, in this case, suffering. When Yūko says honmono no fukō (本物の不幸, "real misfortune"), she activates this entire semantic field, making suffering feel momentarily like a product that can be evaluated for authenticity. The English "real misfortune" or "genuine misfortune" conveys the logical distinction but cannot reproduce the commodification effect, the sense that suffering has become something people perform and consume, something that requires a "real thing" label to distinguish it from knockoffs. The loss matters because the novel's critique of performed victimhood depends on this commodification metaphor: Yūko is refusing to participate in an economy of suffering in which unhappiness is a currency, a credential, a brand. The English translation can convey her refusal but not the specific cultural mechanism she is refusing — the honmono system of authenticity-marking that pervades Japanese consumer culture and has colonized emotional life.

4. Ijiwaru (意地悪): The Meanness of Family

JP 意地悪 — mean; unkind; teasing; the everyday roughness of long-term intimacy; the word for siblings and old friends
EN "mean" / "unkind" / "teasing" — each captures one register but loses the familial intimacy
Loss As discussed in the passage analysis, ijiwaru (意地悪) occupies a specific and untranslatable space in Japanese emotional vocabulary. It is not cruelty (zankoku / 残酷); it is not wickedness (warusa / 悪さ); it is the light, everyday unkindness of people who know each other well enough to tease, who are secure enough in their relationship to risk a small sting. It is the word a younger sibling uses for an older one, the word a child uses for a parent who will not indulge them, the word friends use for each other when the joke goes slightly too far. By asking for an ijiwaru step-parent, Yūko is asking for someone who will treat her like family in the fullest sense — not with the careful kindness of a stranger but with the rough affection of someone who knows she is not going anywhere. English "mean" is too strong, suggesting genuine cruelty; "unkind" is too moralistic; "teasing" is too specific to verbal behavior. None can capture the full warmth-with-edge that ijiwaru conveys — the sense that this meanness is a form of intimacy, a privilege of long-term relationship, a sign that the person feels safe enough with you to be slightly difficult. The loss is relational: without ijiwaru, Yūko's request becomes either darker ("be mean to me") or more trivial ("tease me") than it is in Japanese, where it is a profound and paradoxical request for the kind of love that does not fear its own shadow.

5. Kakomu (囲む): The Geometry of Being Surrounded

JP 囲む — to surround; to encircle; to enclose; to gather around; the geometry of being at the center of others' care
EN "surround" / "encircle" — accurate but lose the spatial and emotional specificity
Loss The verb kakomu (囲む, "to surround / to encircle / to enclose") appears in Yūko's observation that "always being surrounded by good people" is hard work, and its translation raises subtle but important questions. Kakomu is a word of physical geometry: it describes the act of forming a circle around something, of enclosing a space, of creating a boundary that separates inside from outside. When used for people, it carries connotations of protection, of gathering, of the communal circle (enn / 円) that is so central to Japanese social imagery. The English "surround" captures the general meaning but loses the circularity, the sense of being at the center of a ring of care. "Encircle" captures the geometry but sounds military or strategic. "Enclose" captures the boundary-creation but suggests confinement rather than protection. The Japanese kakomu holds all these meanings in suspension: the protection of the circle, the potential confinement of the boundary, the geometry of being at the center of others' attention. Yūko's sentence — ii hito ni kakomareteru (いい人に囲まれてる, "being surrounded by good people") — uses the passive to place herself at the center of this geometry, the object of others' encircling care. The image is almost womb-like — enclosed, protected, held — and Yūko's complaint is that this enclosure, however benevolent, is still an enclosure, still a form of being defined by others' presence rather than one's own choices. The English translation can convey the complaint but cannot reproduce the full spatial metaphor — the circle, the center, the boundary, the inside and outside — that makes the sentence resonant beyond its literal meaning.
Grammar

Grammar Points

Grammatical patterns from the language of relay races and reconstructed families, of love that arrives unchosen and is received with gratitude that is also exhaustion.

〜には〜がいる（持有可能の強調） N5

...ni wa... ga iru: "in my case / as for me, there is/are..." — dative-topic construction emphasizing possession with personal nuance

私には父親が三人、母親が二人いる

"As for me, I have three fathers and two mothers" — The grammar of extraordinary possession stated as ordinary fact.

〜でも（逆接・譲歩） N5

...demo: "but / however" — adversative conjunction turning the entire preceding statement in a new direction

家族の形態は七回も変わった。でも、全然不ではない

"My family form changed seven times. But I am not at all unhappy" — The grammar of paradoxical insistence.

全然〜ない（全面否定の強調） N5

zenzen... nai: "not at all / completely not" — emphatic negative denying every degree of the predicate

全然不幸ではないのだ

"The fact is, I am not at all unhappy" — The grammar of absolute negation as declaration.

〜のだ（説明・断定） N4

...no da: "the fact is that / it is that" — explanatory nominalizer marking a statement as discovered truth or explanation

全然不幸ではないのだ

"The fact is that I am not unhappy" — The grammar of reflective realization.

〜も〜し（列挙・理由の付加） N4

...mo... shi: "and / moreover / not only... but also" — enumerative particle listing reasons or qualities

目も当てられないし、不幸な自分に酔ってなんかいられない

"It's unbearable to look at, and you can't get drunk on being unhappy" — The grammar of double determination.

〜てなんかいられない（継続不可能） N2

...te nanka irarenai: "can't afford to be / can't go around being" — negative potential of continuous form with dismissive nanka

不幸な自分に酔ってなんかいられないよ

"You can't go around getting intoxicated with being a victim" — The grammar of refused self-indulgence.

〜っていうのも（名詞化＋も） N3

...tte iu no mo: "even the fact that / the thing called... also" — quotative nominalization with mo emphasizing that even this is included

いつもいい人に囲まれてるっていうのも、たいへんなんだよね

"Even always being surrounded by good people — that's hard work too, you know" — The grammar of paradoxical inclusion.

〜てくれないかな（願望の婉曲表現） N4

...te kurenai kana: "I wonder if you would... / won't you... for me?" — indirect request/wish using negative question with wondering particle

意地悪な人としてくれないな

"I wonder if you would be a mean person for me?" — The grammar of the wish disguised as speculation.

Study tip: The grammar of And So, the Baton Was Passed is the grammar of ordinary life asserting itself against extraordinary circumstances. The dative-topic possession (ni wa... ga iru) states a family configuration that defies norms with the calm of a weather report; the adversative demo turns the entire narrative of step-child tragedy in a single syllable; the emphatic negative (zenzen... nai) slams the door on pity; the explanatory nominalizer (no da) makes this negation into a discovered truth; the enumerative shi builds a theory of authentic suffering; the negative potential continuous (te nanka irarenai) refuses the intoxication of victimhood; the quotative nominalization (tte iu no mo) includes gratitude itself in the category of hard work; and the indirect wish (te kurenai kana) asks for love in the form of a joke. Pay attention to how these constructions work together to create a prose that sounds simple because it is precise — the voice of a writer who has learned that the most radical claims are best delivered in the grammar of everyday speech.
