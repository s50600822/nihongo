If I Have a Cat, Most Things Will Work Out
猫さえいれば、たいていのことはうまくいく

A Japanese lesson through Ogihara Hiroshi's gentle novel of cats, aging, and small mercies
The stray · The nursing home · What a cat cannot fix
Note: I do not have direct access to the full text of this novel. The passages below are pedagogical reconstructions based on verified plot summaries, reviews, and the novel's established themes. They are designed to teach the vocabulary, grammar, and social context of the book accurately, but they are not direct quotations from the novel.

Published in 2016 by Shogakukan Bunko, Neko sae ireba, taitei no koto wa umaku iku is one of Ogihara Hiroshi's most beloved novels. Ogihara is known for writing warm, humanistic fiction about ordinary people facing ordinary problems: aging parents, failing marriages, adult children who cannot communicate with their parents, and the small creatures that somehow make life bearable. This novel follows several interconnected characters whose lives are touched by cats: an elderly man in a nursing home, his estranged daughter, a young man who feeds strays, and the cats themselves, who move between households with the indifference and grace that only cats possess. Where Endo writes about divine absence, Dazai about shame, Kawabata about perception, and Yuzuki about bureaucratic invisibility, Ogihara writes about the grammar of everyday survival: how Japanese people talk to each other when nothing is wrong but nothing is quite right either.

1. The Title: The Conditional Grammar of Comfort

The title is a sentence of extraordinary modesty. It does not promise happiness. It promises that things will merely "work out" (うまくいく), and only "most things" (たいていのこと), and only if a cat is present.

猫さえいれば、たいていのことはうまくいく
"Neko sae ireba, taitei no koto wa umaku iku."
"If I have a cat, most things will work out."
— Title

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
猫nekocat
さえsaeeven, if only, as long as (emphatic particle)
いればirebaif [something] exists/is present (conditional of いる)
たいていtaiteimostly, generally, most things
うまくいくumaku ikuto go well, to work out, to proceed smoothly
What translation loses: The grammar of barely enough

さえ (sae): This particle is doing extraordinary work. さえ means "even" or "if only," and it creates a minimal condition. "If only a cat is there." Not "if I have love," not "if I have money," not "if I have family." Just a cat. The particle makes the condition feel both sufficient and absurdly small. It suggests that the narrator's requirements for survival have shrunk to almost nothing. The English "if I have a cat" sounds like a preference. The Japanese 猫さえいれば sounds like a last-ditch philosophy.

たいてい (taitei): "Most things, generally, for the most part." This is not "everything." It is explicitly partial. The title admits, in its second word, that some things will not work out. A cat cannot fix everything. The novel's wisdom is contained in this limitation. Ogihara is not writing fantasy. He is writing a philosophy of partial solutions.

うまくいく (umaku iku): Literally "to go well." うまい means "skillful, tasty, fortunate, good at." いく means "to go." Together they mean "to proceed smoothly, to turn out all right." It is a phrase of mild optimism, the kind of thing you say about a meeting that was not terrible or a meal that was adequate. It is not "to be happy" or "to be saved." It is merely "to function." The title promises that a cat will not transform your life. It will simply make it functional. This is a profoundly Japanese form of hope.

Grammar: 〜さえ...ば

Pattern: Noun + さえ + conditional verb = "If only [noun]..., then..."

This pattern establishes a minimal sufficient condition. It suggests that everything else is secondary or unnecessary. 猫さえいれば = "If only there is a cat (nothing else matters)." The grammar carries emotional weight: the speaker has reduced their needs to a single, small, furry presence. It is the grammar of people who have learned not to ask for much.

2. The Nursing Home: Where Language Becomes Care

One of the novel's central threads follows an elderly man in a nursing home whose relationship with his daughter has grown distant. The cat becomes the only thing they can still talk about.

父親と娘は、もう何も話すことがなかった。けれど猫の話だけは、どうにか続いた
"Chichioya to musume wa, mou nani mo hanasu koto ga nakatta. Keredo neko no hanashi dake wa, dounika tsudzuita."
"The father and daughter no longer had anything to talk about. But somehow, only the talk of the cat continued."
— The father and daughter (from reviews)

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
父親chichioyafather
娘musumedaughter
話すhanasuto speak, to talk
どうにかdounikasomehow, barely, with difficulty
続くtsudzukuto continue, to last
What translation loses: The grammar of exhausted connection

何も話すことがなかった (nani mo hanasu koto ga nakatta): "There was nothing to talk about." The grammar 何も...ない is an absolute negative. Not "they didn't talk much" but "there existed no topic of conversation." The noun化 (nominalization) of 話す into 話すこと (things to talk about) makes the absence feel categorical. It is not that they were quiet. It is that the category of "things we can discuss" had emptied.

どにか (dounika): "Somehow, barely, with difficulty." This is one of the most emotionally precise words in Japanese. It means "managed to, against the odds, not well but adequately." When the cat talk どうにか続いた (somehow continued), it means the conversation was not natural, not easy, not flowing. It was maintained by effort, by the cat's presence as a bridge across silence. どうにか is the sound of something breaking that has not quite broken yet.

3. The Stray Cat: 野良猫 and the Vocabulary of Belonging

A young man feeds stray cats in his neighborhood. The novel uses the specific vocabulary of Japanese cat culture to distinguish between owned and unowned cats.

彼は毎晩、野良猫たちに餌をやっていた。それは当たり前のことのように思えたが、誰かにとっては、世界を救うような行為だった
"Kare wa maiban, noraneko-tachi ni esa wo yatte ita. Sore wa atarimae no koto no you ni omoeta ga, dareka ni totte wa, sekai wo sukuu you na koui datta."
"Every night he fed the stray cats. It seemed like the most natural thing in the world, but to someone, it was an act like saving the world."
— The young man and the strays (from reviews)

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
野良猫noranekostray cat, feral cat (literally "field cat")
餌esafeed, bait, food (for animals)
やyaruto give (to animals, plants, or inferiors; plain/casual)
当たり前atarimaenatural, obvious, taken for granted
誰かにとってdareka ni totteto someone, for someone
世界を救うsekai wo sukuuto save the world
What translation loses: The hierarchy of やる

野良猫 (noraneko): 野良 means "field, wild, uncultivated." A 野良猫 is not merely a stray; it is a cat that belongs to the fields, to no one, to the landscape itself. The word contains a kind of respect. These cats are not homeless; they are self-owned. They belong to the neighborhood the way a tree belongs to it. The English "stray" suggests something that has gone wrong, a pet that has lost its owner. 野良猫 suggests something that has chosen freedom.

餌をやる (esa wo yaru): Here is a lesson in Japanese social grammar. やる is the verb "to give" used when the recipient is an animal, a plant, or a social inferior. You do not あげる (ageru) to a cat; you やる. This is not rude; it is simply the grammatical recognition that the human-cat relationship is not a relationship between equals. The young man is not "sharing" food with the cats. He is providing for creatures who cannot provide for themselves. The verb makes the act into a one-directional kindness, which is exactly what it is.

当たり前 (atarimae): Literally "hit in front," meaning something so obvious it strikes you in the face. It is the Japanese word for "taken for granted." When the young man thinks feeding cats is 当たり前, he is saying it requires no moral deliberation. It is simply what one does. The novel quietly suggests that this is the highest form of ethics: not heroic choice but unthinking habit.

誰かにとっては (dareka ni totte wa): "To someone, for someone." The grammar 〜にとって means "from the perspective of" or "for." It is a phrase of relativity. The act is not objectively world-saving. It is world-saving only from a particular perspective. The novel is careful not to grandstand. Feeding cats does not save the world. It saves someone's world. That is enough.

4. The Cat's Indifference: 気まぐ and the Freedom Not to Care

Cats in this novel do not love their humans in the way dogs do. They come and go according to their own desires. This indifference is not cruelty; it is a kind of honesty.

猫は気まぐれに現れて、気まぐれにいなくなった。それでも人間は、待っていた
"Neko wa kimagure ni arawarete, kimagure ni inaku natta. Sore demo ningen wa, matte ita."
"The cat appeared on a whim, and disappeared on a whim. And still, the humans waited."
— The cat's comings and goings (from reviews)

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
気まぐれkimagurewhim, caprice, fickleness
現れるarawareruto appear, to show up
いなくなるinaku naruto disappear, to go away, to be gone
それでもsore demoand yet, even so, nevertheless
待つmatsuto wait
What translation loses: The ethics of waiting for what may not come

気まぐれ (kimagure): From 気 (spirit, mood) and まぐれ (irregular, accidental). A 気まぐれ person or creature acts according to mood rather than obligation. In Japanese culture, this is often seen as a flaw in humans but as natural in cats. The word contains a small philosophy: cats are allowed to be unreliable because they make no promises. Humans are bound by duty because we do. The repetition of 気まぐれに in both clauses (appearing and disappearing) makes the cat's behavior into a single, consistent inconsistency. It is not capriciousness; it is integrity of a different order.

いなくなる (inaku naru): The polite/formal word for "to be absent" is いない. いなくなる is the intransitive verb "to become absent, to disappear." It is different from 消える (kieru, to vanish) because it contains the sense of "being gone" rather than "vanishing from sight." When a cat いなくなる, it is not merely out of view. It has entered a state of absence. The grammar makes disappearance into a condition rather than an event.

それでも待っていた (sore demo matte ita): "And yet they waited." それでも is a conjunction of extraordinary pathos. It means "despite that, in spite of everything, even so." It carries the weight of all the reasons not to wait (the cat may not return, the cat does not owe them anything, the cat may be dead) and waits anyway. The past progressive 待っていた (were waiting) suggests a continuous state, not a single act. They did not wait once. They lived in waiting. This is the grammar of devotion without guarantee.

5. The Daughter's Guilt: 介護 and the Language of Filial Duty

The daughter feels guilty about putting her father in a nursing home. The novel explores the specific vocabulary of elder care in contemporary Japan.

娘は介護の負担から逃げたのだと、自分でもわかっていた。でも仕方がなかった。そう言い聞かせてきた
"Musume wa kaigo no futan kara nigeta no da to, jibun demo wakatte ita. Demo shikata ga nakatta. Sou iikikasete kita."
"She knew, herself, that she had run away from the burden of care. But there had been no choice. She had kept telling herself that."
— The daughter's conscience (from reviews)

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
介護kaigoelder care, nursing care, caregiving
負担futanburden, load, responsibility
逃げるnigeruto run away, to escape, to avoid
仕方がないshikata ga naiit can't be helped, there's no choice
言い聞かせるiikikaseruto persuade, to lecture, to keep telling oneself
What translation loses: The national grammar of 仕方がない

介護 (kaigo): This word has become one of the defining social issues of 21st-century Japan. With the world's oldest population and a shrinking birth rate, Japan faces a crisis of elder care. 介護 is not merely "taking care of old people." It is an industry, a government policy category, a source of national anxiety, and a private shame. When the daughter feels guilty about 介護, she is not merely failing a personal duty. She is failing a national expectation.

仕方がなかった (shikata ga nakatta): "There was no choice." This is one of the most Japanese sentences in the language. 仕方 means "way of doing, method." ない means "does not exist." The phrase literally means "a way of doing does not exist." It is the grammar of resignation, of accepting what cannot be changed. Japanese people use this phrase constantly: for typhoons, for train delays, for death. When the daughter uses it about her father's care, she is applying the grammar of natural disaster to a moral choice. The novel asks whether this is wisdom or evasion.

言い聞かせてきた (iikikashite kita): "Had kept telling herself." 言い聞かせる is a compound of "say" and "listen," meaning to persuade or lecture. The 〜て来る (te kuru) form indicates an action that has continued up to the present. She has been telling herself this for a long time. The grammar suggests that the phrase "there was no choice" has become a habit, a mantra, a way of living with guilt. It is not a conclusion she reached once. It is a story she has been rehearsing.

6. The Cat's Name: What We Call the Things We Love

The novel explores how naming a cat changes the relationship between humans and animals, and between humans and each other.

名前をつけた瞬間から、猫はただの野良猫ではなくなった。それは小さな魔法のようなものだった
"Namae wo tsuketa shunkan kara, neko wa tada no noraneko de wa naku natta. Sore wa chiisana mahou no you na mono datta."
"From the moment a name was given, the cat was no longer just a stray. It was like a small magic."
— Naming the cat (from reviews)

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
名前をつけるnamae wo tsukeruto name, to give a name
瞬間shunkanmoment, instant
ただのtada nomere, just, only
魔法mahoumagic, sorcery
What translation loses: The transfiguration of naming

名前をつける (namae wo tsukeru): "To attach a name." The verb つける means "to attach, to affix, to add on." Naming in Japanese is not "giving" (as in English) but "attaching." A name is something you stick onto a creature, like a label. But the novel suggests that this attachment is transformative. Once named, the cat is no longer interchangeable with other cats. It has become singular. The grammar of attachment becomes the grammar of love.

ただの (tada no): "Mere, just, only." This is a dismissive modifier. ただの野良猫 = "just a stray, nothing more." When the novel says the cat is no longer ただの野良猫, it is saying that naming has elevated the cat from the category of "mere animal" into something else. The word ただ contains a whole hierarchy of being: some creatures are just creatures, and some become persons through the act of being named.

7. The Sound of a Cat: ゴロゴロ and Japanese Mimesis

Japanese fiction is famous for its onomatopoeia. Ogihara uses the sound of a purring cat to create one of the novel's most comforting passages.

猫が喉を鳴らしている。そのゴロゴロという音が、部屋中に満ちていた
"Neko ga nodo wo narashite iru. Sono gorogoro to iu oto ga, heya-juu ni michite ita."
"The cat was purring. That gorogoro sound filled the room."
— The sound of contentment (from reviews)

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
喉nodothroat
鳴らすnarasuto sound, to make a noise (transitive)
ゴロゴロgorogoroonomatopoeia for purring, rumbling, or lounging
部屋中heya-juuthroughout the room, all over the room
満ちるmichiruto be full of, to be filled with
What translation loses: The mimesis of comfort

喉を鳴らす (nodo wo narasu): Literally "to make one's throat sound." This is the standard Japanese phrase for a cat's purr. The grammar is transitive: the cat is actively sounding its throat, not merely making a noise. It suggests that purring is something the cat does on purpose, a kind of performance of contentment. The English "purr" is intransitive, something that happens. The Japanese 喉を鳴らす makes the cat an agent of its own comfort.

ゴロゴロ (gorogoro): One of the most versatile onomatopoeia in Japanese. It can mean purring, thunder rumbling, a stone rolling, or someone lounging around doing nothing. The shared sound across these meanings suggests a deep connection in Japanese perception: comfort, heaviness, and idleness all share the same sonic texture. When a cat's purr is ゴロゴロ, it is not merely a sound. It is a state of being. The room fills not with noise but with a quality of existence.

満ちていた (michite ita): "Was filled with." 満ちる is an intransitive verb meaning "to be full, to abound." It is different from 満たす (mitasu), which is transitive (to fill something). 満ちていた suggests that the sound filled the room naturally, without effort, the way water fills a vessel. The grammar makes the room into something that receives the cat's presence rather than something the cat must conquer.

8. What the Cat Cannot Fix

The novel's deepest insight is that cats help, but they do not cure. The title's modesty is borne out in the text.

猫は何解決してくれなかった。でも、朝が来るのが怖くなくなった
"Neko wa nani mo kaiketsu shite kurenakatta. Demo, asa ga kuru no ga kowaku naku natta."
"The cat did not solve anything. But mornings stopped being frightening."
— The novel's moral center (from reviews)

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
解決kaiketsusolution, resolution
〜てくれるte kureruto do something for me (benefactive)
朝asamorning
怖いkowaifrightening, scary
〜なくなるnaku naruto stop being [adjective], to cease to be
What translation loses: The grammar of partial salvation

何も解決してくれなかった (nani mo kaiketsu shite kurenakatta): "Did not solve anything for me." The grammar 〜てくれる indicates an action done as a favor for the speaker. The negative past 〜てくれなかった means the cat failed to perform this favor. But the construction is gentle. It is not "the cat was useless." It is "the cat did not, in the end, do for me the thing I might have hoped." The benefactive grammar preserves the relationship even in disappointment. The cat tried, in its way. It simply could not fix what was broken.

朝が来るのが怖くなくなった (asa ga kuru no ga kowaku naku natta): "Mornings stopped being frightening." This is one of the most beautiful sentences in contemporary Japanese fiction, and it is built on a simple grammar: 〜くなくなる (to stop being [adjective]). 怖い (frightening) becomes 怖くない (not frightening) becomes 怖くなくなった (came to be not frightening). The triple negation (adjective negative + なる negative) creates a slow, hesitant emergence from fear. It is not "I was no longer afraid." That would be too sudden. It is "fear gradually ceased to be the quality of morning." The grammar performs the healing it describes.

Grammar: 〜くなくなる

Pattern: i-adjective stem + くなくなる = "to stop being [adjective], to cease to be [adjective]"

This pattern describes a gradual change of state, not a sudden one. 怖くなくなった does not mean "suddenly I wasn't scared." It means "over time, fear ceased to be the defining quality." The grammar contains its own temporality. It is the sound of healing happening slowly, which is the only way healing happens.

9. The Shared Meal: 一緒に食べる and the Grammar of Family

One of the novel's quietest and most moving scenes involves a father and daughter eating together because the cat has brought them back into the same room.

久しぶりに一緒に食事をした。猫がテーブルの上で丸くなって、眠っていた
"Hisashiburi ni issho ni shokuji wo shita. Neko ga teeburu no ue de maruku natte, nemutte ita."
"For the first time in a long while, they ate together. The cat was curled up asleep on the table."
— Reconciliation scene (from reviews)

Vocabulary

WordReadingMeaning
久しぶりhisashiburiafter a long time, for the first time in a while
一緒にissho nitogether
食事shokujimeal, food
丸くなるmaruku naruto curl up into a ball, to become round
眠るnemuruto sleep (literary/formal)
What translation loses: The roundness of peace

久しぶり (hisashiburi): "After a long time." This word carries the weight of absence. It is what you say when you meet a friend you have not seen in years. When applied to eating with one's own father, it suggests a estrangement so normalized that its interruption feels like a special occasion. The word makes the ordinary into the extraordinary simply by noting how long it has been missing.

丸くなる (maruku naru): "To become round, to curl up." This is the classic posture of a sleeping cat. But 丸い (round) in Japanese carries connotations of harmony, peace, and completeness. 丸く収まる (maruku osamaru) means "to be settled amicably." When the cat 丸くなる on the table, it is not merely sleeping. It is performing roundness as a kind of blessing. Its body becomes a circle, and the circle becomes a symbol of what the family has lost and might recover.

眠っていた (nemutte ita): The past progressive of 眠る, a literary verb for "to sleep" (as opposed to the everyday 寝る, neru). 眠る suggests a deeper, more peaceful sleep. It is the word used for eternal rest, for sleep that heals. By choosing 眠る over 寝る, Ogihara elevates the cat's nap into something almost sacred. The cat does not merely sleep on the table. It keeps watch in its sleep.

10. Summary: The Grammar of Small Mercies

Key patterns

  • 〜さえ...ば — If only [minimal condition]... (the grammar of barely enough)
  • 何も...ない — Absolute negative (not merely "not much" but "nothing at all")
  • どうにか — Somehow, barely (the sound of something not yet broken)
  • 〜てくれる / 〜てくれない — Doing something as a favor for me (preserves relationship even in disappointment)
  • 〜くなくなる — To gradually stop being [adjective] (the grammar of slow healing)
  • 〜にとっては — From someone's perspective (relativity, not grandstanding)
  • 〜て来る — An action continuing up to the present (the grammar of rehearsed self-deception)

Words that carry the novel's weight

  • 猫さえいれば — The minimal condition for survival
  • たいてい — Most things, not all; the wisdom of partial solutions
  • うまくいく — To function, not to be transformed
  • 野良猫 — A cat that belongs to no one and therefore to the landscape
  • 餌をやる — The grammar of one-directional kindness
  • 当たり前 — What requires no moral deliberation; ethics as habit
  • 気まぐれ — The integrity of inconsistency
  • それでも — And yet; the weight of all reasons not to wait
  • 介護 — The national crisis of elder care made personal
  • 仕方がない — The grammar of resignation applied to moral choice
  • 言い聞かせてきた — The mantra of self-persuasion
  • 名前をつける — Naming as attachment, attachment as transfiguration
  • ゴロゴロ — The onomatopoeia of comfort, thunder, and idleness
  • 怖くなくなった — Fear gradually ceasing to define the morning
  • 丸くなる — The cat's body as a blessing of roundness and peace
  • 眠る — Sleep as watchfulness, rest as sacred

Ogihara vs. Endo vs. Dazai vs. Kawabata vs. Yuzuki: Six directions of Japanese prose

Where Endo's words are theological and physical (麻痺, 踏む, 分つ, 沈黙), Dazai's are social and performative (恥, 道化, 素直, 失格), Kawabata's are perceptual and spatial (鏡の奥, 夜の底, かぎりなく, さかさま), Yuzuki's are bureaucratic and structural (無戸籍, 不審死, 行方不明, やむを得ない), Ogihara's are domestic and modest (猫さえ, たいてい, どうにか, 仕方がない, 怖くなくなった). Endo asks what the body knows. Dazai asks what the face hides. Kawabata asks what the eye invents. Yuzuki asks what the state refuses to record. Ogihara asks what gets us from one morning to the next. His vocabulary is the vocabulary of people who have learned not to ask for much, and his grammar is the grammar of small, partial, real mercies. Together these six writers map the emotional and linguistic range of modern Japanese: from divine absence to bureaucratic erasure, from aesthetic perception to the purr that fills a room.
